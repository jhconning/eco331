# The Economic Origins of Government

> **Source:** Allen, Robert C., Mattia C. Bertazzini, and Leander Heldring. 2023. “The Economic Origins of Government.” _American Economic Review_ 113 (10): 2507–45. ([PDF](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20201919)) ([student slides](https://drive.google.com/file/d/12L0K7KgJ_wnFipGP_SKsA3fz3xpH0-2D/view?usp=sharing))


## Overview
This paper, published in the *American Economic Review* (2023) by Allen, Bertazzini, and Heldring, investigates the fundamental drivers behind the formation of the world's first states. Using a novel archaeological panel dataset covering southern Iraq between 3900 BCE and 2700 BCE, the authors utilize a natural experiment—the sudden, exogenous shifting of the Euphrates and Tigris rivers—to test between "cooperative" (social contract) and "extractive" (predatory) theories of state origin. The authors conclude that governments emerged primarily as a cooperative mechanism to solve coordination failures in irrigation and public good provision.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Natural Experiment:** The rivers of southern Iraq shifted their courses periodically due to rainfall volatility in Turkey and Syria. A river shifting away from a settlement created a severe coordination problem: small-scale private irrigation was no longer viable, and large-scale public canal construction became necessary.
*   **Cooperative vs. Extractive Hypotheses:** 
    *   *Extractive theories* (e.g., Marx/Engels, Olson) predict that states should form where the tax base is most secure (i.e., where water is most accessible).
    *   *Cooperative theories* (e.g., Locke, Samuelson) predict that states should form where there is a "demand" for public goods—specifically, where the river shifted away and collective action was required to prevent economic collapse.
*   **Findings:** The authors find that states formed specifically where rivers shifted away. This formation was accompanied by:
    *   Increased construction of public irrigation canals.
    *   Increased construction of defensive walls.
    *   Increased recording of tribute payments and the expansion of administrative buildings.
*   **Institutional Mechanism:** The first states were not top-down impositions of power but rather "scaled-up" versions of existing extended kinship groups (lineages). The "lugal" (king) acted as the head of a ruling lineage that coordinated activities between subordinate lineage heads, effectively extending lineage-based reciprocity to a territorial level.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & State Formation:** The paper bridges the gap between historical archaeology and modern public economics. It challenges the "predatory state" narrative by providing empirical evidence for the "social contract" model in the earliest known human civilizations.
*   **Public Goods and Cooperation:** The core of the argument rests on the *private underprovision of public goods*. The shift in river geography effectively forced a transition from decentralized, private irrigation to centralized, public coordination.
*   **Geography and Institutions:** The authors demonstrate how physical geography—specifically the shifting of watercourses and the "gradient" of the plain—dictated the feasibility of irrigation-based cooperation. This echoes broader themes regarding how geographic constraints influence the emergence of institutional frameworks.
*   **Demographic Transition & Complexity:** The findings align with the view that as societal complexity increases—necessitated by environmental changes—lineage-based social structures prove insufficient, leading to the innovation of more formal, tiered government structures.
