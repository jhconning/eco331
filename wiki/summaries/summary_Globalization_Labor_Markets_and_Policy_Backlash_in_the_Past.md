# Globalization, Labor Markets and Policy Backlash in the Past

> **Source:** Williamson, Jeffrey G. 1998. "Globalization, Labor Markets and Policy Backlash in the Past." _Journal of Economic Perspectives_  12(4): 51–72. [PaperLinks to an external site.](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.12.4.51)


### Overview
In this paper, Jeffrey G. Williamson explores the economic history of the "Atlantic Economy" during the late 19th century (1870–1914), a period characterized by rapid globalization through mass migration, international trade, and capital flows. Williamson argues that while these forces drove significant convergence in living standards (specifically real wages), they also triggered a "policy backlash." As globalization altered domestic income distributions—increasing inequality in labor-scarce "New World" countries and decreasing it in labor-abundant "Old World" countries—politically powerful interest groups mobilized to demand immigration restrictions and protective tariffs.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **Convergence via Factor Prices:** Williamson emphasizes that real wages and factor prices (such as the wage/rental ratio) are superior metrics to GDP per capita for understanding economic convergence. Between 1854 and 1913, real wages across the Atlantic economy converged significantly due to the integration of global labor and commodity markets.
*   **Distributional Impact (Heckscher-Ohlin):** The author applies the Heckscher-Ohlin framework to explain that trade and migration created winners and losers:
    *   **New World (Labor-scarce):** Immigrants flooded the bottom of the labor market, increasing inequality and depressing the wages of the unskilled relative to land rents and capital.
    *   **Old World (Labor-abundant):** Emigration reduced the supply of labor, raising the relative returns to unskilled workers and reducing inequality.
*   **Policy Backlash:** The "backlash" was an endogenous response to these distributional shifts.
    *   **Immigration:** As inequality rose in the New World, domestic labor interests successfully lobbied for stricter immigration policies (e.g., U.S. quotas, literacy tests) to defend their economic position.
    *   **Trade:** In Europe, the "grain invasion" from the New World lowered land rents. Landed elites in protectionist countries (like Germany and France) successfully lobbied for tariffs, while countries like Britain and Denmark, where agriculture was a smaller share of the economy, largely maintained free trade.

### Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & Institutions:** The paper demonstrates that institutional changes (like migration quotas and tariffs) are not merely exogenous shocks but are often direct, calculated responses to the economic pressures created by global market integration.
*   **Geography:** The "Atlantic Economy" is defined by the geography of labor and resource abundance. The New World’s resource-rich/labor-scarce nature contrasted sharply with Europe’s land-scarce/labor-abundant reality, driving the migration flows that shaped the era.
*   **Demographic Transition:** Williamson links migration flows to demographic trends, noting that the age structure of migrants (predominantly young adult males) significantly impacted the labor force participation rates and per-worker productivity of both sending and receiving nations.
*   **The Malthusian Epoch vs. Modern Growth:** While the late 19th century reflects a shift toward modern growth, the study highlights that factor price disparities—a key feature of pre-modern economies—were being actively smoothed out by the "open economy forces" of the Industrial era, setting the stage for the modern integrated global economy.
