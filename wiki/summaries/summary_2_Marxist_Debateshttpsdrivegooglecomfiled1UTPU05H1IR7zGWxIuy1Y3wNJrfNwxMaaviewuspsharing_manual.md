# 2: Marxist Debates

> **Source:** Wood, E.M. 2002, "2: [Marxist Debates](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1UTPU05H1IR7zGWxIuy1Y3wNJrfNwxMaa/view?usp=sharing)," (excerpts) in _The Origin of Capitalism: A longer View_. Verso.


## Overview
This chapter from Ellen Meiksins Wood’s *The Origin of Capitalism* (2002) provides a critical survey of the historiographical debates surrounding the transition from feudalism to capitalism. Wood examines how various Marxist scholars have attempted to explain the emergence of the capitalist mode of production, specifically focusing on the tension between internal economic pressures and external trade-based explanations. The text serves as a roadmap for understanding the "Brenner Debate" and the evolution of political Marxism, moving away from deterministic or "commercialization model" interpretations.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
* **The Commercialization Model Critique:** Wood argues against the traditional Marxist (and non-Marxist) assumption that capitalism is the natural result of increased trade and urban growth. She posits that the mere presence of markets does not equate to the presence of capitalism.
* **The Primacy of Social Property Relations:** The central mechanism of capitalism, per Wood, is not the growth of commerce but a specific change in social property relations. This shift occurs when producers are separated from their means of production and forced to sell their labor power to survive, and when market dependence becomes the primary driver of economic activity.
* **The Role of Agrarian Capitalism:** Wood emphasizes that the "transition" began not in the cities with merchants, but in the countryside with the restructuring of landlord-tenant relations (specifically in England). This created a class of agrarian capitalists and a landless proletariat, a unique development that paved the way for industrialization.
* **Rejection of Teleology:** The chapter rejects the idea that capitalism was an inevitable outcome of "progress" or the maturation of commercial practices. Instead, it frames the rise of capitalism as a historically contingent transformation of power dynamics.

## Connections to Course Themes
* **Economic History:** The text challenges traditional stage-theories of economic development. It asks us to consider whether economic growth is a prerequisite for capitalism or if capitalism is a specific social system that forces a unique form of economic growth.
* **Institutions:** Wood’s analysis is fundamentally institutional. She focuses on the legal and social framework of land ownership and labor obligations. In the context of our course, this maps onto discussions of how property rights and the enforcement of contractual obligations dictate the trajectory of an economy.
* **Malthusian Epoch:** While this text focuses on the transition *out* of feudalism, it implicitly addresses the Malthusian trap by discussing how early modern agriculture shifted from subsistence-focused production to market-dependent, profit-maximizing production. It challenges the idea that subsistence constraints were purely biological, highlighting that they were also institutional and social.
* **Demographic Transition:** By analyzing the forced separation of the peasantry from the land, the text provides insight into the creation of the labor supply necessary for the demographic and economic shifts that later characterized the Industrial Revolution.
