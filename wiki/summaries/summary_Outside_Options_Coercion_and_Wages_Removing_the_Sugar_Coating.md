# Outside Options, Coercion, and Wages: Removing the Sugar Coating

> **Source:** Dippel, Christian, Avner Greif, and Daniel Trefler. 2020. "Outside Options, Coercion, and Wages: Removing the Sugar Coating." _The Economic Journal_  130(630): 1678–1714. [PaperLinks to an external site.](https://drive.google.com/file/d/19Z071GHVKbaLjvFRv7iuqdmDPDWy3dkn/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
This paper examines the political economy of labour markets in 14 British West Indies colonies between 1838 (the year of slave emancipation) and 1913. The authors challenge the view that free labour markets emerged naturally post-emancipation. Instead, they argue that the white planter elite utilized their political influence to institute "legal coercion"—the strategic use of legislative, judicial, and police powers—to artificially suppress the "outside options" of freed labourers (such as squatting on smallholdings). By reducing these outside options, planters effectively forced workers back onto plantations at lower wages. The study provides robust empirical evidence linking the strength of the plantation system to higher rates of incarceration and lower wages.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Coercion-Outside Option Mechanism:** The paper posits that in economies with a large informal sector (smallholding), firm owners (planters) increase profits by actively reducing the value of the informal sector. If workers have a viable outside option, they can demand higher wages or refuse plantation work. Coercion makes the outside option unattractive, effectively binding the worker to the plantation.
*   **Legal Coercion:** This manifested in three forms:
    1.  **Restriction of Land Access:** Anti-squatting laws, harsh trespass statutes, and limiting access to Crown land.
    2.  **Asymmetric Employment Contracts:** Laws that criminalized absenteeism and breach of contract, often enforced by planter-controlled magistrates.
    3.  **Regressive Taxation:** Biased import duties on food and property taxes that specifically penalized smallholders.
*   **Planter Political Power ($N$):** Planter power is measured by the share of plantation crops in total exports. As this share declined, planter lobbying power diminished, leading to reduced legal coercion and higher wages.
*   **Identification Strategy:** The authors use two exogenous instruments to identify the decline of the plantation system: 
    1.  The availability of fertile, non-sugar-suitable hinterland (which made it easier for ex-slaves to evade the plantation system).
    2.  The shift in British investment patterns (the decline of the Caribbean as an investment destination due to growing opportunities elsewhere).

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Institutions:** This paper provides a granular, micro-level look at how elites build and maintain institutions (legal systems, tax codes) to secure economic rents. It highlights the persistence of coercive institutions even after the legal abolition of slavery, demonstrating that "freedom" can be constrained by the institutional environment.
*   **Economic History:** The paper serves as a direct empirical challenge to the "Malthusian" or purely market-driven view of labour transitions. It illustrates that the transition from coerced to free labour is not linear and is heavily mediated by the political agency of the land-owning elite.
*   **Geography:** The paper connects geographic variation (the availability of "hinterlands" unsuitable for sugar) to long-run institutional and economic outcomes. Geography determines the "ease of exit" for workers, which in turn limits the ability of the elite to exercise political coercion.
*   **The Atlantic Economy:** The study focuses on the "First Globalization" period, showing how the decline of the Caribbean’s importance in the global sugar trade—a macro-economic trend—directly triggered a domestic political shift that improved the welfare of the formerly enslaved population.
