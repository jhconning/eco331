### Summary of `sl6_Culture`: The Role of Culture in Economic History

**Part 1: Defining Culture and the Economist’s Skepticism** The lecture begins by distinguishing culture ("learned rules of behavior and heuristics," per Henrich) from formal institutions. Economists are notoriously skeptical of using culture as an explanatory variable because it is prone to ad hoc reasoning, endogeneity, and the "winners write history" bias (e.g., using Asian culture to explain both their poverty in 1950 and their booming success in 1990). The "Needham Puzzle" introduces this complexity: China possessed immense technological advantages (gunpowder, paper) and an effective state, yet did not industrialize first. While some argue Confucian culture became stagnant—citing Emperor Qianlong's dismissal of British goods—historians note Qianlong was actually secretly fascinated by Western science, suggesting the barrier may have been institutional rather than a genuine cultural disinterest.

**Part 2: The Protestant Ethic vs. Human Capital vs. Institutions** Max Weber famously argued that the Calvinist belief in predestination created a unique "Protestant Work Ethic" that sparked capitalism. The slides challenge this purely cultural view using modern economic tools. Becker and Woessmann counter that the Protestant advantage was actually just a shock to human capital: Martin Luther wanted people to read the Bible, which accidentally created a highly literate workforce that was perfectly positioned for economic growth. Simultaneously, Jared Rubin argues the Reformation fundamentally altered institutions: by stripping Protestant Kings of the religious legitimacy the Catholic Church used to provide, monarchs were forced to turn to Parliaments for legitimacy, inadvertently creating the robust property rights that spurred investment.

**Part 3: Islam and the Institutional Roots of the "Long Divergence"** During the Middle Ages, the Islamic world was vastly wealthier and more scientifically advanced than Europe. Timur Kuran argues that the Middle East's subsequent economic stagnation was not caused by an intrinsic cultural aversion to commerce, but by the unintended consequences of Islamic law. The prohibition on interest (*riba*) slowed complex finance, but the bigger culprits were inheritance and partnership laws. Because Islamic law mandated estates be divided equitably among extended family upon death, capital was constantly fragmented, and commercial partnerships were legally forced to dissolve if a single member died. This kept Middle Eastern businesses small and short-lived, while Europe was busy inventing structurally immortal, multi-generational joint-stock corporations. Furthermore, Chaney highlights that the rise of religious *madrasas* boosted the political power of religious elites, leading to a stark collapse in scientific book production.

**Part 4: The Deep Roots and Long-Term Persistence of Culture** The final section pivots to how historical shocks create cultural norms that persist for centuries after the initial cause disappears. Voigtländer and Voth show that German towns exhibiting severe anti-Semitic pogroms during the 1348 Black Death were the exact same towns that proudly voted for the Nazi party 600 years later. We see persistence in civic trust as well: people living on the former Habsburg Empire border still inexplicably trust modern government more than their neighbors across the street, due to efficient Habsburg bureaucracy from the 1800s.

The slides conclude by exploring Joseph Henrich's "Kinship Trap" thesis. Henrich argues that the Catholic Church's medieval obsession with banning cousin marriage destroyed massive, insular family clans. This inadvertently rewired Western European psychology: without a massive clan to rely on, Europeans were forced to become individualistic, build impartial institutions (like courts and guilds), and learn to trust "strangers" in impersonal markets. This is mirrored in Italy today, where areas with weaker historical Church influence still have higher cousin marriage rates and lower civic trust. Finally, Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn demonstrate that societies whose ancestors practiced agriculture using the heavy plow (which required male upper-body strength) still exhibit lower female labor force participation and more traditional gender norms today, proving that ancient technologies leave a permanent cultural footprint.

### 6 Discussion Questions for the Classroom

1. **The Needham Puzzle:** If Emperor Qianlong was secretly studying British scientific instruments while publicly telling the British that "we possess all things and have no use for your manufactures," what does this tell us about the danger of taking historical "cultural" statements at face value? Can a ruler's political bluff easily be mistaken by historians as a cultural failure?
2. **Weber vs. Human Capital:** If Becker and Woessmann are correct that the "Protestant advantage" simply came down to the fact that Luther forced people to learn how to read the Bible, does that completely destroy Max Weber's argument that religious *values* matter in economic growth, or just change the mechanism?
3. **The Unintended Consequences of Fairness:** Timur Kuran argues that Islamic inheritance laws were deeply progressive and egalitarian for the 9th century (mandating that wealth be split among extended family and women), but those same laws became severe commercial bottlenecks by the 18th century because they prevented the accumulation of capital. Can you think of any modern laws or institutions designed for "fairness" today that might be inadvertently stifling long-term economic growth?
4. **Henrich's Kinship Trap:** Joseph Henrich argues that the Catholic Church accidentally created the psychological foundation of modern capitalism—individualism and trusting strangers—merely because they wanted to break up rival family armies and inherit their estates. If true, does this mean "Western culture" isn't a unique set of inherent values, but simply a psychological byproduct of a medieval power grab?
5. **The Plow and the Gender Gap:** The research shows that physical technologies from centuries ago (the heavy plow vs. the hoe) still dictate gender norms today, even among first-generation immigrants living in modern American cities. If our current cultural beliefs are just "hangovers" from dead technologies, how long does it take for culture to catch up with our new economic reality?
6. **The Economist's Dilemma:** Economists are terrified of using "culture" as an explanation because winners write history (e.g., Asian "Confucian" culture was blamed for their extreme poverty in the 1950s, but praised as the secret to their economic miracle in the 1990s). Given how slippery culture is, do you think we should even try to use it as an economic variable, or should we stick strictly to analyzing institutions and geography?
