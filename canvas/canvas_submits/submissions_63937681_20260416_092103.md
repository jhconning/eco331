#### Assignment Description
The following poem is estimated to have been written in the 17th or 18th century. Though its author is now unknown, it circulated widely through oral tradition (quoted several times in British Parliamentary debates) and in print via pamphlets and publications.

**  
The Goose and the Common  
**  
The law locks up the man or woman   
Who steals the goose from off the common   
But leaves the greater villain loose   
Who steals the common from off the goose.

The law demands that we atone   
When we take things we do not own   
But leaves the lords and ladies fine   
Who take things that are yours and mine.

The poor and wretched don’t escape   
If they conspire the law to break;   
This must be so but they endure   
Those who conspire to make the law.

The law locks up the man or woman   
Who steals the goose from off the common   
And geese will still a common lack   
Till they go and steal it back.

          Anonymous

Bonus (2 point for an extra paragraph): Some legal scholars and economists have argued that we are living through a "Second Enclosure Movement" regarding information and technology. Do any of the themes of this poem apply to the modern economy? Analyze one or more examples.

The "Enclosure Movement" of the 17th and 18th centuries marked a transformative era in British economic history, replacing long-standing customary rights with exclusive legal titles. This process dismantled the medieval framework of communal grazing and open-field farming, effectively redefining the "common" from a shared resource managed through traditional usufructuary rights into private property. As noted in the poem "The Goose and the Common," property emerged not as a natural state but as a legal construct engineered by those with the legislative authority to "conspire to make the law". By converting communal land into private parcels sanctioned by Parliament, the state inverted the concept of theft: it became a crime for the poor to take a goose from the land, while it was a legal achievement for a lord to take the land from the goose.

This transition shifted property from a localized social bond to a state-enforced marketable commodity, prioritizing capital accumulation and economic efficiency over community stability. By replacing consensus and custom with deeds and fences, the British legal system paved the way for industrial capitalism, driving the "poor and wretched" into the wage-labor market. Ultimately, this history reveals that the definition of "ownership" is dictated by whoever holds the power to craft the law.

  
Bonus: These historical themes find a modern parallel in the "Second Enclosure Movement," a term used by scholars like James Boyle to describe the expansion of intellectual property rights. Just as 18th-century lords fenced off physical commons, today's corporations use patents, copyright, and Digital Rights Management (DRM) to enclose software code, information, and genetic material. The seed industry, for instance, employs patent law to restrict farmers from the age-old practice of replanting seeds. Similarly, the digital shift from ownership to licensing deprives users of the right to modify or repair their own tools. In both eras, the central critique remains constant: a legal system that favors industrial "lords and ladies" by privatizing once-shared or naturally occurring resources.

**What is the true definition and concept of "property"?**

Generally speaking, property is the legally sanctioned right to exclude others from a resource, which is frequently referred to as a "bundle of rights" rather than a physical item. But how did this concept emerge? It all traces back to 18th and 19th-century Britain, where a dramatic transformation that mirrored the Industrial Revolution and the rise of capitalism took place. Previously, land rights were shared under the feudal system; while a lord owned the property, peasants had "customary rights" to graze cattle, gather firewood, or farm specific plots. This way of life and society existed before "enclosures" were introduced. "Enclosure," the process of fencing off common lands and transforming them into private, individual properties, served as the starter for the evolution of the concept of "property" from a complex, restrictive system of customs and feudal land-based rights to a more defined, market-oriented system of individual ownership. This shift was not viewed as simple legal maintenance and reform, but rather as social upheaval because it transformed land from a source of social stability and subsistence into a commodity for profit.

**Are property rights a natural fact, a customary tradition, a legal fiction constructed by those with the power to 'make the law,' or something else?**

Looking back at the poem and our recent readings, it is clear that "property" was a legal fiction created and manipulated by those in positions of political power, rather than a "natural fact." Before the major transition, customs dominated decisions. Land was open and accessible to everyone. It followed an open concept in which peasants farmed small, dispersed plots and shared communal grazing areas to reduce individual risks like crop failure. Everything was fine until those in power became greedier and decided to shift from communal to private property and ownership. The wealthy primarily used Parliamentary Acts to "legally" seize common land and consolidate it into capitalist farms. While supporters hailed this as "progress" that ended famine, critics such as Marx saw it as “class warfare."
