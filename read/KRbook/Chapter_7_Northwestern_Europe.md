Why Did Northwestern Europe

Become Rich First?

■ During the 19th century, parts of the world took off

economically, becoming far richer than at any

point in human history

■ This process began in Northwestern Europe—

specifically, Britain and the Dutch Republic

■ Why did growth begin in this tiny corner of

Europe? It wasn’t just that Britain industrialized

first. We must explain *why* this was the case!

■ Also, why didn’t other parts of the world (China,

Middle East, Southern Europe) experience similar

growth despite a “head start”?

good and bad

■ “Fractured land hypothesis”: topographical

Proximity to the Steppe

■ Another geographic feature that shaped institutional development: proximity to the steppe

■ China: built a unified, central state to protect against the threat of invasion by steppe

nomads (Ko, Koyama, and Sng 2018)

■ Europe: not near the

steppe, faced invasion from

many directions (Huns,

Avars, Magyars, Arabs,

Moors, and Vikings)

■ Contributed to European

fragmentation: a unified

state could not defend in all

directions

Why Does Fragmentation Matter?

■ Empire can be good for growth

– Suppresses piracy, limits internal trade

duties, standardizes monetary system,

infrastructure investment

– E.g., “Pax Mongolica” and “Pax

Islamica”

■ But weaker, fragmented polities can also

be good for growth

– More checks on executive authority

– More interstate competition à

economic, political, scientific, and

technological breakthroughs

Why Wa s There No Medieval

European Take-off?

■ Communes (city-states) engaged in trade,

independent of strong rulers

■ Medieval growth, when it occurred, was

based on trade and expansion of markets

(Smithian growth)

■ Cities like Venice, Florence, and Genoa

became fabulously wealthy

■ But the Commercial Revolution (11th-13th

centuries) did not lead to a take-off. Why not?





Why Wa s There No Medieval

European Take-off?

■ Medieval growth was subject to *reversals* and

was not sustained

– Merchant oligarchs placed barriers to

entry

■ Most importantly, the medieval technological

base was limited

– Although innovation did occur

– Clocks, spectacles, navigation, printing

■ But the *rate of innovation* was too slow to

overwhelm demographic and other retarding

factors





Divergence within Europe Just before

■ By the end of the medieval period, Europe

was no longer obviously behind the rest of

the world

■ In the 16th-17th centuries, Europe’s economic

center moved away from the Mediterranean

to northwestern Europe (England, Low

Countries)

■ One reason: European Marriage Pattern

became more prominent in Northern Europe

after the Black Death, increased real wages

■ Though EMP cannot fully explain pattern of

Opening of the Atlantic

■ Opening of Atlantic trade gave Atlantic-facing

economies an advantage

■ Dutch and English ended up taking advantage of new

opportunities

■ But Spain fell behind

– Inflow of precious metals led to high inflation

– Profits of Atlantic trade controlled by the Crown

– Profits funded wars and consolidated autocratic

power (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2005)

– Political institutions encouraged rent-seeking

policies (monopoly rights to guilds, tax

exemption for nobility, forced loans)

The Dutch Parlement

■ Dutch Republic: powerful parlement that ruled

without a Crown

– Reformation kick-started a wider political

rebellion against Spanish rule (Dutch

Revolt)

– No centralized authority to extract

resources, trample over property rights, or

infringe on personal liberties

– Parlement mostly comprised of urban,

commercial interests

Dutch Parlement Building

■ Contributed to Dutch “Golden Age”: growth in

finance, urbanization, trade, real wages





The English Parliament

■ British Parliament: emerged in 13th century but

weak relative to the Crown until the 16th century

■ Tudors (Henry VII, Henry VIII): used Parliament

to bolster weak legitimacy, especially after the

Reformation removed the Church in the 1530s

(Greif and Rubin 2021)

■ Ultimately led to conflict: Civil Wars of 1640s and

Glorious Revolution (1688) resulted in

Parliamentary supremacy

– William and Mary accepted the Bill of

Rights, which limited their power

Fall Out from the Glorious Revolution

■ After the Glorious Revolution, English

Parliament held the purse strings

■ Credible commitment to repay lenders, led to

massive expansion of sovereign debt and the

creation of the Bank of England (North and

Weingast 1989)

■ Also lowered interest rates the state paid on

debt

■ Glorious Revolution laid the foundations of the

British borrowing and interest rates, 1693–1739

party system and cabinet government

■ Ultimately provided the political and institutional

stability that was important for Britain’s

subsequent economic growth





Britain on the Eve of Industrialization

■ After the Glorious Revolution, Parliament became a forum

where land could be reallocated towards more productive uses

– Investment in road and river transport dramatically

improved

– Parliamentary regulation helped keep internal trade

relatively unimpeded

■ Major caveat: this was *not* a move towards greater democracy

– Only a small proportion of adult males could vote

– The power of the rich was entrenched, the 18th-century

British state was designed to favor the propertied classes

(Enclosure Acts, penal laws against the poor)

An Enclosure Act





What Wa s Different About British

Institutions?

■ It is less useful to focus on features such as corruption or lack of democratic

representation: Britain shared these features with almost all pre-modern states

■ More insight is gained from considering which factors were critical in allowing

Britain to attain a high degree of political stability and prosperity

■ The British system was successful in constraining the independent power of the

monarchy, while balancing the interests of landowning elites with those of

mercantile and financial elites

– Able to mobilize tremendous resources for warfare without crushing the

domestic economy

– Not a democracy, but it did provide scope for “voice” through regular

elections





Chapter Summary

■ By around 1700, northwestern Europe had many of the necessary preconditions

for sustained economic growth

– Per capita incomes and real wages were high by pre-industrial standards

– Markets were relatively well developed and extensive

– Institutional framework conducive to the expansion of internal trade

– State institutions strong enough to provide a measure of law and internal

peace

■ But these factors alone were not enough for modern economic growth

■ The Dutch Republic took the lead during its “Golden Age,” but it did not

industrialize

■ Britain was the first to have the combination of growth of industry and structural

change that characterized its Industrial Revolution (late 18th-19th centuries)
