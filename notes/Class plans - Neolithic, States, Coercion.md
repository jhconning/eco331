# **CLASS 1: THE NEOLITHIC REVOLUTION**

## _From Foraging to Farming: Progress or Trap?_

### **I. Introduction: The Standard Story (5 min)**

**The traditional narrative:**

- Hunter-gatherers: poor, nasty, brutish, short lives
- Agriculture discovered ~10,000 BCE → civilization begins!
- Surplus → cities → writing → technology → progress
- Farming = unambiguous improvement

**Today's challenge**: Was it really progress?

---

### **II. What Changed in the Neolithic Revolution? (10 min)**

**The transition** (~10,000-5,000 BCE, varying by region):

- From mobile foraging → sedentary agriculture
- From wild plants/animals → domesticated crops/livestock
- From small bands (25-50) → villages (hundreds) → cities (thousands)

**Key crops and locations:**

- **Fertile Crescent**: wheat, barley, sheep, goats (earliest, ~10,000 BCE)
- **China**: rice, millet, pigs (~9,000 BCE)
- **Mesoamerica**: maize, beans, squash (~8,000 BCE)
- **Andes**: potatoes, quinoa, llamas (~8,000 BCE)
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: sorghum, yams (~5,000 BCE)

**Population explosion**:

- World population ~5 million (10,000 BCE) → ~100 million (5,000 BCE)

---

### **III. The Case Against Agriculture: Diamond's Critique (15 min)**

#### **A. Health declined**

**Skeletal evidence shows:**

- **Shorter stature**: Hunter-gatherers averaged 5'9" (men), 5'5" (women); early farmers 5'3" and 5'1"
- **More diseases**: Crowd diseases (measles, smallpox, TB) require dense populations
- **Nutritional deficiencies**: Reliance on few crops (vs. diverse foraging diet)
- **Dental problems**: Cavities, abscesses from grain/carbohydrate diet
- **Bone stress markers**: Evidence of harder physical labor

**Comparison of skeletons** (Greece, Turkey):

- Pre-agricultural: healthier bones, better teeth, taller
- Post-agricultural: shorter, more disease markers, harder labor

#### **B. More work, less leisure**

**Forager lifestyle** (ethnographic evidence from modern foragers):

- ~15-20 hours/week "work" (hunting, gathering)
- Rest of time: socializing, storytelling, play, rest
- Flexible schedules, no bosses

**Farmer lifestyle**:

- ~40-60 hours/week (plowing, planting, weeding, harvesting, storage)
- Backbreaking labor (hoeing, grinding grain)
- Rigid seasonal schedules
- Can't just "move on" if crop fails

#### **C. Greater inequality**

**Forager societies**:

- Highly egalitarian (can't accumulate much when mobile)
- Food sharing norms
- Weak hierarchy

**Agricultural societies**:

- **Storable surplus** → some accumulate more than others
- **Hereditary inequality** emerges
- Elites, slaves, class distinctions
- "For 90% of people, agriculture meant harder work and worse nutrition"

#### **D. Increased violence**

**Evidence**:

- Pre-agricultural skeletons: ~5% show violence
- Early agricultural sites: ~15-25% show violence (wounds, weapons)
- Fortifications appear with agriculture

**Why?**

- Fixed assets worth fighting over (land, stored grain, livestock)
- Population pressure on good land
- Wealth disparities create incentives for raiding

#### **E. Environmental degradation**

- Deforestation, soil erosion, salinization
- Collapse of early civilizations (Mesopotamia) partly due to environmental damage
- Compare to foragers' light footprint

---

### **IV. The Puzzle: If Agriculture Was Worse, Why Did It Spread? (10 min)**

**Diamond's answer: It's a trap!**

1. **Population growth locks you in**:
    
    - Agriculture supports 10-100x more people per square mile
    - Once population grows, can't go back to foraging (not enough wild food)
    - "Like an arms race or drug addiction"
2. **Ratchet effect**:
    
    - Each generation makes small choices (plant a little, forage a little)
    - Gradually commit to sedentism
    - By the time costs are clear, you're trapped
3. **Competitive advantage**:
    
    - Agricultural societies have **more people** → more soldiers
    - Can militarily dominate foragers
    - Over time, farmers displace or absorb foragers
4. **Not individual choice**:
    
    - No individual chose agriculture knowing the full costs
    - Emerged gradually from many small decisions
    - Collective outcome no one intended

**The irony**:

- Progress for the **species** (more humans exist)
- Regress for the **individual** (worse health, harder work)
- Evolution doesn't optimize for happiness!

---

### **V. Counter-Arguments and Complications (8 min)**

**Not everyone agrees with Diamond:**

**A. Selection bias?**

- Maybe only marginal populations adopted agriculture initially
- Those already doing poorly had less to lose
- Successful foragers may have resisted longer

**B. Climate change forced it?**

- Younger Dryas period (~11,000 BCE): climate instability
- Wild food sources became unreliable
- Agriculture = risk management strategy

**C. Dietary diversity**

- Early farmers didn't immediately abandon foraging
- Mixed economies persisted for millennia
- Only later did societies become dependent on few crops

**D. Social and cultural benefits?**

- Permanent settlements → stronger social bonds, cultural accumulation
- Ritual, art, monumental architecture
- Food security (when it worked)
- Is leisure everything? (Philosophical question)

**E. Regional variation**

- Not all agricultural transitions looked the same
- Some regions (Pacific Northwest) developed sedentism without agriculture
- Others (pastoral nomads) found middle paths

---

### **VI. Implications and Themes (7 min)**

**For the rest of the course, remember:**

1. **Technological change isn't always progress** (for individuals)
    
    - Can create traps or collective action problems
    - Winners ≠ majority
2. **Path dependence matters**
    
    - Early choices constrain later options
    - Hard to reverse course once populations grow
3. **Population vs. per capita welfare**
    
    - More humans ≠ better life for each human
    - Whose welfare are we optimizing?
4. **Inequality has deep roots**
    
    - Storable surplus → stratification
    - Agriculture created conditions for exploitation
5. **States emerge from agriculture**
    
    - Next class: How surplus + sedentism + inequality → states
    - Coercion becomes possible when people can't flee

**Preview next class**: _"If agriculture made life worse for most people, why didn't they just leave? Sometimes they did—but increasingly they couldn't. Next time: how states emerged to control populations and prevent exit."_

# **CLASS 2: THE ORIGIN AND NATURE OF STATES**

## _Three Perspectives on State Formation_

### **I. Recap and Bridge (5 min)**

**Last class**: Agriculture = more people, harder work, worse health, more inequality

**Today's question**: After agriculture, **states** emerged. Why?

- States = centralized authority, taxation, coercion, hierarchy
- Appeared 5,000-6,000 years after agriculture began
- Not automatic or inevitable—many agricultural societies remained stateless

**Three competing views**: Predatory, Efficiency, War-Making

---

### **II. The Predatory View: States as Control and Extraction (20 min)**

#### **A. Olson: The Stationary Bandit**

[Use your outline from before—10 minutes]

**Key insight**: States are better than anarchy, but still fundamentally extractive

#### **B. Scott: Legibility and the Grain Hypothesis**

**Scott's question**: Why did states form where and when they did?

**The "legibility" requirement**:

States need to **see and control** populations:

- Who lives where?
- What do they produce?
- How much can we extract?

**Not all crops are equal for state formation:**

|Crop Type|Visibility|Taxability|State-Friendliness|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Grain** (wheat, rice, barley)|High: fixed fields|High: seasonal harvest, storable|**VERY HIGH**|
|**Roots** (cassava, potatoes)|Low: underground|Low: harvest anytime, less storable|Low|
|**Tree crops** (olives, fruit)|Medium|Medium|Medium|
|**Foraging/hunting**|Very low: mobile|Very low|Very low|

**Why grain enabled states:**

1. **Visible**: Can't hide a wheat field
2. **Seasonal**: Harvest happens at predictable time → easy to tax
3. **Storable**: Can warehouse and redistribute
4. **Divisible**: Easy to measure and portion
5. **Concentrated**: High calories per unit area

**Geography matters**:

- Early states: river valleys with grain (Mesopotamia, Nile, Yellow River, Indus)
- Stateless zones: mountains, forests, swamps where populations could evade

**The "domestication" of humans**:

- Agriculture domesticated plants and animals
- States domesticated _people_—made them sedentary, legible, taxable
- Walls around early cities: keep threats out or keep taxpayers in?

#### **C. Domar's Trilemma: The Problem of Exit**

[Use outline from before—5 minutes]

**Connection to Scott**:

- Grain agriculture makes populations **visible**
- But if land is abundant, people can flee
- Solution: **legal bondage** (we'll explore this next class)

**Implication**: State formation requires both:

1. Taxable production (grain)
2. Captive population (geography or coercion)

---

### **III. The Efficiency View: States as Cooperative Solutions (10 min)**

[Use your North-Thomas outline from before]

**Key tension with predatory view**:

- Predatory: States extract
- Efficiency: States provide

**Both can be true**: States that _only_ extract don't survive; states that only provide can't fund themselves

---

### **IV. The War-Making View: Competition and Extraction (8 min)**

[Use your Tilly outline from before]

**Addition for early states**:

- Mesopotamian city-states constantly at war
- Egyptian unification driven by military consolidation
- Chinese Warring States period → Qin unification
- Competition _selects for_ extractive capacity

---

### **V. Comparing and Synthesizing (12 min)**

[Use your comparison table and synthesis from before]

**Key application: Early Mesopotamia (Uruk, Ur, ~3500-2000 BCE)**

Through each lens:

1. **Predatory (Scott + Olson)**:
    
    - Irrigated wheat/barley → highly legible
    - Surplus worth extracting
    - Temple and palace elites as stationary bandits
    - Population trapped in narrow fertile strip
2. **Efficiency (North-Thomas)**:
    
    - Irrigation requires collective action
    - Flood control, canal maintenance
    - Dispute resolution, weights/measures
    - Genuine gains from coordination
3. **War-Making (Tilly)**:
    
    - City-states compete militarily
    - Need resources for armies, walls
    - Successful states expand, unsuccessful ones absorbed

**All three matter**:

- Geography creates **possibility** (legible grain production)
- Coordination needs create **legitimacy** (public goods)
- Competition creates **necessity** (military extraction)

---

### **VI. Why No States Everywhere? (5 min)**

**Counter-examples matter**:

**Amazon basin** (until recently):

- Slash-and-burn agriculture: mobile, non-legible
- Root crops (cassava): hidden, not easily taxed
- Low population density
- Dense forest: high monitoring costs
- Result: No large states (but complex societies existed!)

**Central African forests**:

- Similar pattern: yams, mobile agriculture
- States only on savanna edges (where grain could grow)

**Eurasian steppes**:

- Pastoral nomadism: herds move
- Hard to tax mobile populations
- Tribal confederations, not states (until Mongols)

**Papua New Guinea highlands**:

- Sweet potatoes: root crops, dispersed gardens
- Remained stateless until 20th century

**Lesson**: States are _conditional_ on specific combinations of:

- Crop type (taxability)
- Geography (exit options)
- Population density
- Military pressure

# **CLASS 3: COERCED LABOR AND THE STATE**

## _Slavery, Serfdom, and the Control of Labor_

### **I. Introduction: The Puzzle of Unfree Labor (5 min)**

**The question**: Why do coerced labor systems exist?

**Standard economic intuition**:

- Free labor markets should be efficient
- Slavery/serfdom seem inefficient (low incentives, monitoring costs)
- Yet these systems persisted for millennia

**Today's agenda**:

1. Types of coerced labor
2. When and why they emerge (Domar's framework)
3. How states enable and enforce them (Scott's perspective)
4. When they break down

---

### **II. Forms of Coerced Labor (8 min)**

**A spectrum, not a binary**:

|System|Degree of Coercion|Mobility|Ownership|Examples|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Chattel slavery**|Extreme|None|Full property rights|Ancient Rome, Caribbean, US South|
|**Debt bondage**|High|Very limited|De facto ownership|South Asian bonded labor|
|**Serfdom**|High|Restricted|Bound to land, not person|Medieval/Early Modern Europe, Russia|
|**Corvée labor**|Medium|Limited (periodic)|State claims labor|Ancient Egypt, Inca _mit'a_, French _corvée_|
|**Indentured servitude**|Medium|None (for term)|Temporary ownership|Colonial Americas, British Australia|
|**Conscription**|Medium|None (for term)|State claims person|Universal in wartime|
|**Wage labor + legal restrictions**|Low-Medium|Restricted|Nominally free|Pass laws (South Africa), company towns|

**Common features**:

- **Restriction on mobility**: Can't leave
- **Compulsory work**: Must labor for master/lord/state
- **Minimal compensation**: Below market rates
- **Backed by force**: Violence or legal coercion

---

### **III. Domar's Trilemma: The Core Logic (15 min)**

#### **A. The Impossibility Theorem**

[Use your previous Domar outline, expanded slightly]

**The formal logic**:

Assume:

1. Land is abundant (free/cheap frontier available)
2. Labor is free (workers can choose where to work)
3. Landlords exist and don't work themselves

**Then**: Why would workers pay rent to landlords?

- If land is free on the frontier, workers go there
- Landlords can't extract surplus
- **Contradiction!**

**Solution—must eliminate one**:

**Option A: Eliminate landlords (frontier society)**

- American frontier thesis (Turner)
- Homesteading, family farms
- No feudal class emerges
- Example: 19th century American West

**Option B: Eliminate free labor (coercion)**

- Bind workers legally to land/master
- Russian serfdom, plantation slavery
- Landlord class preserved through force
- Example: Russia 16th-19th century, American South

**Option C: Eliminate free land (closed frontier)**

- Land scarcity forces workers to accept terms
- Market for labor emerges
- Example: Western Europe after population growth

#### **B. Historical Applications**

**Case 1: Russia (16th-17th centuries)**

**Setup**:

- Muscovy expanding south and east
- Vast new territories (steppes, Siberia)
- Service nobility (_pomestie_ system) gets land grants
- But land without labor is worthless!

**The problem**:

- Peasants fleeing to frontiers (Cossack territories)
- Landlords losing labor force
- Military service system threatened

**The solution**:

- Gradual restrictions on peasant mobility
- 1497: Two-week moving period (St. George's Day)
- 1649 Ulozhenie: **Unlimited recovery period for runaways**
- Effectively binds peasants to land

**Domar's prediction confirmed**: Free land + landlords → must bind labor

**Case 2: American South (17th-19th centuries)**

**Setup**:

- Abundant land (after indigenous dispossession)
- Desire for plantation agriculture (tobacco, cotton, rice)
- Labor-intensive crops

**Initial attempt**: Indentured servitude

- White Europeans, 4-7 year terms
- Problem: They complete terms and head to frontier!

**The "solution"**: Chattel slavery

- Permanent, hereditary bondage
- Racial justifications develop to support it
- Even more extreme than serfdom

**After emancipation (1865)**:

- Land still abundant, labor now free
- How to preserve planter class?
- Sharecropping + legal restrictions (Black Codes, vagrancy laws)
- Domar's logic: Try to restrict mobility through law

**Case 3: Western Europe (post-Black Death, 14th-15th centuries)**

**Setup**:

- Black Death kills 30-50% of population
- **Labor suddenly scarce, land abundant** (temporarily)
- Domar predicts: Either landlords lose power OR coercion increases

**What happened**:

- Initially: Attempts to bind labor (Statute of Labourers 1351 in England)
- But: Ultimately failed! Serfdom weakened
- Peasants gained bargaining power
- Why? Couldn't close frontiers (within regions), and coordination problems among landlords

**By 16th century**: Western European peasants largely free

- Land/population ratio returned to pre-plague levels
- But institutional legacy: stronger peasant rights

**Contrast**: Eastern Europe (Prussia, Poland) went opposite direction

- "Second serfdom" in 15th-16th centuries
- Why? Grain exports to Western Europe + open eastern frontiers
- Needed to bind labor to meet export demand

---

### **IV. Scott's Perspective: States Enable Labor Coercion (12 min)**

#### **A. The State-Labor Coercion Connection**

**Scott's argument** (_Against the Grain_, Chapter 5: "Population Control"):

States and coerced labor are **mutually reinforcing**:

1. **States need labor control**:
    
    - For taxation (corvée, conscription)
    - For public works (pyramids, walls, canals)
    - For military (soldiers, porters)
2. **Elites need state power**:
    
    - Can't enforce slavery/serfdom without state backing
    - Need courts, police, slave catchers
    - Need legal codes defining unfreedom

**Examples**:

**Ancient Mesopotamia**:

- Temple and palace estates used large slave labor forces
- War captives enslaved
- Debt bondage common
- State enforced work obligations

**Ancient Egypt**:

- _Corvée_ system: peasants owed labor to state
- Pyramid building, canal maintenance
- State tracked population through censuses
- Limited mobility (desert boundaries helped)

**Classical Athens** (paradox!):

- Democracy for _citizens_
- But ~30% of population were slaves
- Silver mines, agriculture, crafts
- State-backed slave patrols

**Roman Empire**:

- Massive slave population (estimates 20-30%)
- War captives, conquered peoples
- Latifundia (large estates) dependent on slave labor
- State's military expansion _generated_ the slave supply

#### **B. The Technology of Control**

**How states make coercion feasible**:

1. **Legal infrastructure**:
    
    - Slave codes, serf laws
    - Property rights in humans
    - Punishments for escape/resistance
2. **Documentation**:
    
    - Population registers
    - Tax rolls
    - Bills of sale
    - Travel permits (internal passports in Russia)
3. **Physical barriers**:
    
    - Walled settlements
    - Checkpoint systems
    - Geographic bottlenecks
4. **Enforcement apparatus**:
    
    - Slave patrols (US South)
    - Bailiffs (Medieval Europe)
    - Police tracking runaways

**Scott's point**: Without states, large-scale coerced labor is difficult

- Small-scale slavery existed in stateless societies
- But plantation slavery, serfdom across regions requires state power

#### **C. Resistance and Escape**

**Coerced labor always generated resistance**:

**Flight to frontiers**:

- Russian serfs → Cossack territories
- American slaves → Canada (Underground Railroad), maroon communities
- Southeast Asian slaves → highlands (Zomia)

**Open resistance**:

- Slave revolts (Spartacus, Haitian Revolution)
- Peasant uprisings (Wat Tyler, Pugachev's Rebellion)
- Usually brutally suppressed

**Everyday resistance** (James C. Scott's "weapons of the weak"):

- Work slowdowns
- Tool breaking
- Feigned ignorance
- Theft of master's property

**The state's response**: More surveillance, harsher punishments

- Fugitive slave laws (US)
- Unlimited recovery periods (Russia)
- Mutilation, branding to mark escapees

---

### **V. When Do Coerced Labor Systems Break Down? (8 min)**

**Factors that weaken coercion**:

#### **A. Demographic shifts** (Domar logic)

- Labor scarcity increases worker bargaining power
- Example: Post-Black Death Europe

#### **B. Technological change**

- Mechanization reduces labor requirements
- Makes supervision costs of slavery less attractive
- Example: Some argue US slavery would have declined economically (though Civil War ended it first)

#### **C. Military defeat**

- War ends slavery (US Civil War)
- Revolution overthrows serfdom (Russia 1861, though gradual; 1917 revolution)

#### **D. Moral/ideological shifts**

- Enlightenment ideas, human rights
- Abolitionist movements
- Religious arguments (Quakers, etc.)
- But Scott notes: Moral arguments often _follow_ economic shifts, not cause them

#### **E. State capacity changes**

- If state weakens, can't enforce coercion
- If state strengthens differently (conscription army instead of feudal levies), less need for serfdom
- Example: French Revolution abolishes feudalism partly to create citizen army

#### **F. Integration into market economy**

- Wage labor becomes more efficient
- Free workers more flexible, better incentivized
- Landlords switch from coerced to market labor
- Example: Prussian Junkers gradually shift to wage labor in 19th century

**Scott's skeptical note**:

- Formal abolition doesn't always mean real freedom
- Debt bondage, sharecropping, company towns can be "unfreedom" with different names
- State coercion shifts forms (from corvée to conscription to taxation)

---

### **VI. Broader Implications (7 min)**

**What we've learned about states and labor**:

1. **Labor coercion is deeply tied to geography**:
    
    - Open frontiers create pressure for bondage
    - Closed spaces allow market labor
    - Domar's trilemma predicts institutional choices
2. **States enable and depend on labor control**:
    
    - Can't have large-scale coercion without state power
    - States need labor for projects, wars, taxes
    - Mutual reinforcement
3. **Efficiency isn't the only logic**:
    
    - Coerced labor often economically suboptimal
    - But distributional consequences matter more
    - Elites prefer less-efficient systems that preserve their position
4. **Path dependence in labor institutions**:
    
    - Early choices (slavery vs. free labor) shape centuries of development
    - Hard to reverse once established
    - Legal, cultural, economic structures lock in
5. **The modern world still has echoes**:
    
    - Prison labor, human trafficking
    - Migrant labor with restricted mobility
    - Conscription (draft)
    - Debt bondage in global supply chains

**Connection to development**:

- Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson: "Reversal of Fortune"
- Places with indigenous labor abundance → coercive institutions → poor today
- Places with labor scarcity → settler institutions → rich today
- Initial factor endowments → institutions → long-run outcomes

---

### **VII. Synthesis: Three Classes Together (5 min)**

**The arc of the story**:

**Class 1**: Neolithic Revolution

- Agriculture → surplus, sedentism, population growth, inequality
- Made _possible_ states and coercion
- But individually costly ("worst mistake"?)

**Class 2**: State Formation

- States emerged to control and extract from agricultural populations
- Required legible crops (grain) and captive populations
- Predatory, efficient, and war-making logics all operate

**Class 3**: Labor Coercion

- States and elites use coercion to control labor
- Geography determines when coercion is necessary (Domar)
- State power makes large-scale coercion feasible (Scott)

**The big picture**:

- Human history from 10,000 BCE to 1800s CE: increasing hierarchy, coercion, extraction
- Most people worked harder, had less freedom, faced more violence than their foraging ancestors
- "Progress" for civilization, but not for most individuals
- Only recently (and incompletely) has this reversed

**For the rest of the course**:

- These patterns repeat: expansion → labor scarcity → coercion
- Or: population growth → labor abundance → freedom
- Or: competition → extraction → state building
- Watch for Domar's trilemma and Scott's legibility everywhere

### **Assessment Ideas:**

**Short Essay** (after Class 3): _"Choose one coerced labor system (Russian serfdom, American slavery, Inca corvée, etc.). Using Domar's framework and Scott's insights, explain: (1) Why did this system emerge? (2) How did the state enable it? (3) Why did it eventually end or transform?"_

**Comparative Analysis**: _"Compare the Neolithic Revolution in TWO different regions (Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, Andes). Why did both adopt agriculture? Did state formation follow similar patterns? What role did geography play?"_

**Discussion Questions** (use throughout):

- "If you were a peasant in 16th century Russia facing serfdom, what would be your options?"
- "Was the Neolithic Revolution 'worth it' from a utilitarian perspective?"
- "Can we have states without coercion? What would that require?"
- "Are modern democracies fundamentally different from ancient states, or just better at hiding extraction?"

### **Visual/Media Suggestions:**

- **Maps**: Show where early states formed (grain valleys) vs. where they didn't (mountains, forests)
- **Timeline**: Neolithic Revolution → state formation (lag of 5,000 years!) → spread of agriculture and states
- **Comparison charts**: Health data (foragers vs. farmers), labor systems (slavery → wage labor spectrum)

This three-class sequence gives students a foundational understanding of how agriculture → states → coercion, with specific mechanisms (legibility, Domar's trilemma, war-making) that they'll see repeated throughout economic history!
