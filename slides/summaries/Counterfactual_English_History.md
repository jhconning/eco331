**Counterfactual History**: 

**The Smithian baseline.** Start with geography and commerce. England's exceptional endowment — a deeply indented coastline, navigable rivers penetrating far inland, proximity to the North Sea trading system, and above all the absence of internal tolls and tariff barriers that fragmented Continental markets — created powerful Smithian growth forces largely independent of agrarian class structure. Adam Smith himself would note that market integration, the division of labor, and the accumulation of commercial capital do not require free peasant labor as a prerequisite; they require secure exchange and low transaction costs. Even in a serf economy, lords with access to coastal and riverine trade routes have strong incentives to produce for markets rather than purely for subsistence. England's wool trade — already deeply integrated into Flemish and Italian commercial networks before the Black Death — would likely persist and deepen regardless of how labor was organized internally. The institutional form of surplus extraction matters less, in this view, than whether surplus is being generated and channeled into commercial circuits at all.

**The demographic and epidemiological recovery.** Clark's patient Malthusian framework suggests that English population dynamics after the Black Death were going to generate developmental pressure regardless of serfdom. The key Clarkian mechanism is not wages per se but the slow cultural and biological selection for bourgeois values — patience, literacy, propensity to save — that he argues operated across many centuries and was relatively insensitive to short-run institutional variation. Even in a counterfactual serf England, the literate administrative classes, the church, and the merchant stratum continued to reproduce these traits. The "survival of the richest" argument does not require free peasant labor markets to operate; it requires only that economically successful traits be transmitted intergenerationally, which happens through culture and family as much as through market wages.

**The coal and energy argument.** Pomeranz and Wrigley both emphasize that England's coal endowment — its location, accessibility, and proximity to the early industrial districts — was a geographical windfall largely orthogonal to agrarian institutions. Wrigley's "organic economy" ceiling would eventually be hit by any English economy, however organized; and England's coal was sitting in the ground regardless of whether peasants were serfs or free laborers. The key insight is that the energy transition from wood and muscle to mineral fuel generates such enormous productivity gains that it can potentially dissolve institutional constraints that previously appeared binding. A serf economy hitting Malthusian limits has particularly strong pressure to exploit accessible coal for heating and industrial processes — arguably stronger than a labor-abundant free economy, since energy substitutes for the scarce factor. The steam engine, on this view, might emerge later and in a somewhat different form, but the underlying coal-and-geography logic is hard to suppress permanently.

**The cultural and ideational channel.** McCloskey would be most skeptical of the entire institutionalist framing. Her argument is that what mattered was a specific change in *rhetoric* — the social legitimation of bourgeois commercial activity, innovation, and "creative destruction" — that happened in northwestern Europe for reasons not reducible to property rights regimes or labor market structure. The Protestant Reformation, the print revolution, the culture of improvement and experiment: these are, on her account, the real causes, and they operated at the level of ideas and social valuation rather than factor markets. A serf England would still have had the Reformation (Henry VIII's dynastic motivations were largely independent of agrarian class structure), would still have developed a literate commercial culture in its towns, and would still have participated in the broader northwest European ideational shift toward honoring innovation and trade. The bourgeois virtues, once rhetorically validated, generate their own institutional changes — they don't merely respond to prior institutional conditions.

**The synthesis counterfactual.** Putting these threads together, an economist skeptical of strong institutional determinism might argue that England's developmental advantage was *overdetermined* by factors more durable than any single labor market settlement. The geography was not going anywhere. The coal was not going anywhere. The commercial integration into North Sea networks was not going anywhere. The Protestant Reformation, almost certainly, was not going anywhere. A serf England might have developed more slowly, with surplus channeled through lords rather than improving tenant farmers, and with industrialization arriving a generation or two later — but the fundamentals press toward development regardless. The result is not the Poland-England equivalence of the strong institutionalist story but something closer to a *delayed and differently organized* English breakthrough: perhaps 1820 rather than 1760, perhaps steam power first deployed on great noble demesnes rather than by Manchester mill owners, perhaps more state-directed and mercantilist in character than the historically laissez-faire British version. The destination is recognizable; the path and timing differ.

The honest intellectual tension here is that this more optimistic counterfactual is harder to falsify precisely because it relies on forces — geography, culture, energy endowments — that are slow-moving and structural. The institutionalist has the advantage of a sharp causal mechanism and a clean comparison case (Eastern Europe). The skeptic's reply is that Eastern Europe lacked England's geography, coal, and Atlantic position, and that conflating the institutional divergence with the developmental divergence mistakes correlation for cause. That debate, of course, remains very much alive in economic history today.
