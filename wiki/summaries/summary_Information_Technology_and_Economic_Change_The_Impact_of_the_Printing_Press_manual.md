# Information Technology and Economic Change: The Impact of the Printing Press

> **Source:** Manual PDF

## Overview
This paper investigates the economic impact of the movable type printing press in early modern Europe. While previous macroeconomic research struggled to find evidence of the printing press driving aggregate productivity growth, Jeremiah E. Dittmar utilizes city-level data to demonstrate that the technology was a significant driver of urban growth. Between 1500 and 1600, cities that adopted the printing press in the late 1400s grew approximately 60% faster than comparable cities that did not, establishing the printing press as a foundational technology for the rise of European capitalism.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **Localized Spillovers:** The economic benefits of the printing press were localized due to high transport costs for books and the need for face-to-face interaction. Printing cities became hubs for human capital accumulation and intellectual exchange.
*   **Commercial Skill Development:** The printing press facilitated the spread of "commercial arithmetics" and manuals on double-entry bookkeeping. These texts provided the merchant class with the tools necessary for modern business practices, such as calculating interest and managing long-distance trade.
*   **Agglomeration Effects:** Printing workshops acted as meeting places for scholars, merchants, and craftsmen, eroding traditional social barriers and fostering an environment conducive to innovation and information sharing.
*   **Exogenous Variation:** To address the selection bias of why certain cities adopted the press, the author uses "distance from Mainz" (the birthplace of printing) as an instrumental variable. The results show that proximity to Mainz was a strong predictor of adoption but had no correlation with city growth *before* the invention, confirming that the growth advantage was caused by the technology itself.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & Innovation:** The paper challenges the "Malthusian" narrative that technology had negligible impact in the pre-industrial epoch. It argues that information technology—specifically the reduction of access costs to knowledge—was a catalyst for economic vitality.
*   **Institutions and Geography:** The author notes that printing was an urban phenomenon. It interacts with geography (specifically ports) where the combination of print-enhanced commercial skills and waterborne transport created significant synergies for growth.
*   **The Demographic Transition:** The study emphasizes that in pre-industrial Europe, cities were the primary engines of economic change. Rapid urban growth, fueled by the printing press, serves as a proxy for broader economic prosperity during this period.
*   **Culture & Knowledge:** The "printing culture" fostered an urban, bourgeois public sphere. By providing the tools for numeracy and business literacy, the printing press enabled the "social ascent of new professionals," a vital step in the transition toward modern economic institutions.
