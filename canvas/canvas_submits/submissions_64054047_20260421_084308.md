The argument for free land and its relationship to free or slave labor was built on the idea of a limiting economic factor. Solow builds on the theories of Domar and discusses the case of Wakefield, highlighting the case that when land is abundant, there is an unlimited expansion of labor, allowing productivity to rise and no rents to landlords, while when land is limited, there is always a diminishing return, allowing for surplus for landlords who benefit from limited land (Solow, 1993, p. 36). This reflects many of the ideas we discussed earlier in class, especially the early establishment of civilizations in areas with abundant land. When land was readily available, they created coercive mechanisms that allowed them to control the population until it grew to match land availability; once land was limited, they could conquer to expand and find more land and more people to work it. This economic constraint helps explain why colonial landlords struggled to find free workers: workers were limited, and when there was so much land to be worked, they needed to ensure they could keep a surplus, which would have been much harder under a wage system. 

Despite the same constraint, the North and South took different paths: the North pursued a Yeoman republic, while the South maintained a system of slavery and indentured servitude. Solow expands on Domar’s model by emphasizing that “Domar’s model is an abstraction” and that slavery’s relationship to free land is not a predetermined causal effect but dependent on tons of other factors, like profitability, and more complex socio-political decisions (Solow, pp. 37). As a result, the difference between the North and South emerged due to the relationship between socio-political decisions and Domar’s framework. Solow discusses how the political interests of Thomas Jefferson and Hamilton led to different visions and different paths for the North and South to follow. Jefferson’s vision was built upon the possibility of what land could do, as he believed the land allowed for independence and ambition. While Hamilton wanted an industrial society, this required an intervention to prevent an agricultural future (Solow, pp. 37-38). This is reminiscent of our earlier class discussions on the role that institutions play in development, since institutional pathways can dramatically change how countries deal with the same issues. We see that, in this case of “free land” and the colonies, despite their shared connection to Britain, the Atlantic trade network, and the realities of a multi-ethnic society, they did not follow the same developmental pathway (Solow, p. 40). As a result, the economic constraints of limited labor were a universal presence in any European colony, but they were not the sole deciding factor in the development of slavery; it was how institutions and governance interacted with the economic constraint that led to different colonies that were all dealt the same cards to have different legacies of development and coerced labor.

**According to Solow, why did the presence of "free land" make it difficult for a colonial landlord to hire free workers?**

According to Solow, the availability of "free land" made it difficult for a colonial landlord to hire free workers because, in areas where land was cheap and plentiful, voluntary European labor was slow to adapt and establish since no one wanted to work for another. Given the abundance of resources available, farmers preferred to farm for themselves rather than for a "landlord." As a result of this, labor became the most scarce and valuable factor of production. Wages increased as a result of labor scarcity, leaving nothing for a nonworking landlord to benefit from. There was no way to collect "rent" because the worker could have quit at any time. The scarcity of labor benefited the workers more than the landlord because, as long as the capital required to start a farm was low and land was abundant, any employee could eventually save enough money to become an independent farmer. This was the economic constraint of the "free land" concept.

**So how and why did this economic constraint lead to a "republic of independent yeomen" on family farms in the North but to a "landed aristocracy and slaves" in the South and Brazil?**

This is actually due to Domar's "trilemma" theory. He claims that a society can only have two of three things at once: free land, free peasants, and a non-working landowning class. The "republic of independent yeomen" on family farms in the North and the "landed aristocracy and slaves" in the South and Brazil are just two examples of this concept at work. For the "republic of independent yeomen" on family farms in the North, the two things that were satisfied out of the three were free land and free peasants. Since landlords could not profitably employ free labor, the agricultural structure shifted to family-sized farms where the owners worked for themselves. Furthermore, without political intervention to enslave the population, economic forces could not support a large class of non-working landowners, resulting in a more egalitarian society of "yeomen." As for the "landed aristocracy and slaves" in the South and Brazil, these regions had a desire to produce large-scale export crops and support a wealthy landowning class, which required breaking the concept of the "trilemma." The only way to accomplish this was to abolish worker freedom. In order to profit from the land, the "government" (or the ruling class) had to limit the labor force's freedom of movement, effectively tying them to the land through slavery or serfdom, and according to Solow, it was only with this assured, coerced labor supply that capital investment was attracted and long-term trade links with Europe were established, resulting in the formation of a wealthy "landed aristocracy."

It’s known as Domar's Trilemma, where in an agricultural society, you can have free land, free labor, or a non-working elite class, but you can only ever have 2 at once (these 3 things cannot all occur at the same time).

This economic constraint explains why the North and South ended up with such different social structures. There weren't high-value export "staples" like sugar that required massive amounts of labor to be profitable in the North. Since landlords couldn't make a profit off hired labor, the region shifted to a "free land and free labor" system, which naturally created a society of free, landowning farmers (independent yeomen) on family-sized farms. However, in the South and Brazil, crops like sugar, tobacco, and rice were super profitable, but they required a permanent labor force that wouldn't disperse. Because free workers would just leave to start their own farms, the elites used the power of the state to take away the "free labor" and build a land aristocracy. They solved the problem of labor scarcity through introducing slavery and depending on coercion, which resulted in an aristocracy that couldn’t have existed with a free workforce.
