# Did Culture Make Some Rich and Others Poor?

## Overview
In this chapter, Mark Koyama and Jared Rubin explore the persistent debate regarding the role of culture in explaining the Great Divergence. They define culture broadly—as the set of beliefs, values, and norms shared by a group—and investigate whether specific cultural traits served as preconditions for the transition to modern economic growth. While early scholarship (notably Max Weber) emphasized religious doctrines, modern economic historians focus on how cultural traits like trust, individualism, and attitudes toward work and education interact with institutional frameworks to create differential economic outcomes.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
Koyama and Rubin categorize the influence of culture through several primary mechanisms:

*   **The Weberian Hypothesis and its Evolution:** The authors revisit the "Protestant Ethic" debate, acknowledging that while Weber’s original thesis regarding Calvinism and capital accumulation has faced significant empirical scrutiny, the underlying idea—that religious transformations can alter economic behavior—remains relevant.
*   **Cultural Traits and Cooperation:** The chapter highlights the importance of "generalized trust." In societies with high levels of trust, transaction costs are lowered, enabling more complex market exchanges and specialized production. They contrast this with "limited-morality" societies, where cooperation is confined to immediate kinship groups, often hindering long-term capital investment.
*   **The Transmission of Human Capital:** The authors argue that cultural values regarding the "value of children" and literacy—often rooted in religious practices such as the Reformation's emphasis on reading scripture—directly influenced the supply of human capital, a critical engine for the Industrial Revolution.
*   **Cultural Persistence and Change:** A central argument is that culture is not stagnant. It evolves in response to institutional incentives. When institutions change (e.g., the expansion of secure property rights), cultural norms regarding innovation and risk-taking often follow, creating a feedback loop between formal rules and informal beliefs.

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** Koyama and Rubin link cultural shifts to the transition out of the Malthusian trap. They suggest that cultural attitudes toward marriage age and fertility (e.g., the European Marriage Pattern) were essential for preventing population growth from completely eroding the gains from productivity improvements before the 19th century.
*   **Institutions vs. Culture:** The chapter serves as a bridge between the "Institutions Hypothesis" (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson) and the "Culture Hypothesis" (Greif, Henrich). The authors argue that these are not mutually exclusive; rather, institutions provide the "rules of the game," but culture determines how individuals play within those rules.
*   **Geography:** The authors address the role of geography as an indirect influence on culture. For example, certain geographies favored the development of "big-man" societies or kin-based structures, which in turn inhibited the development of the broader societal trust necessary for modern markets.
*   **The Demographic Transition:** The chapter underscores that the shift to modern growth required a cultural pivot toward high-investment child-rearing. This shift, which prioritized child quality (education/health) over child quantity, is identified as a vital cultural mechanism that sustained the Demographic Transition.
