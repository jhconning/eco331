--- Slide 2 ---
A Large Part of The World was Colonized by Just a Few
“Opening of the Atlantic” a key turning point in global economic history
England, France, Spain, Portugal, & Netherlands colonized a large fraction of the world’s landmass
Violence and exploitation practices were common
What role did this play in Europe becoming rich? Other parts of the world not catching up?


--- Slide 3 ---
Exploitation
The colonial powers exploited the natural resources of their colonies
Precious metals (South America) 
Rubber (Congo)
Sugar (Caribbean, Brazil)
Spices and sugar (Indonesia)
Oil (Middle East)
Slaves
Cheap inputs and labor from the colonies allowed for dramatic economic expansion in Western Europe
The legacy of colonization is still with us in many respects. But was it responsible for modern economic growth?
“In the Rubber Coils”

--- Slide 4 ---
How Colonization Mattered
New World crops fundamentally changed Eurasian diets
Nunn and Qian (2010): potato was responsible for about one-quarter of Old World population growth and increase in urbanization between 1700 and 1900
Pomeranz (2000): key to Europe’s rise was new land (“ghost acreage”) in the New World, which relieved population pressures and brought in new natural resources
Slave/coerced labor thought to play a role in Britain’s industrialization via cotton and sugar production: discussed more in Chapter 8

--- Slide 5 ---
Marx’s Argument
Karl Marx, in Das Kapital:
“The discovery of gold and silver in America, the extirpation, enslavement and entombment in mines of the aboriginal population, the beginning of the conquest and looting of the East Indies, the turning of Africa into a warren for the commercial hunting of black-skins, signaled the rosy dawn of the era of capitalist production.”
For Marx, colonization provided the initial capital required for the construction of the modern economic system, especially via trading companies
”Primitive accumulation” provided the resources that fueled economic growth in European countries


--- Slide 6 ---
Modern Versions of the Exploitation Thesis
Eric Williams (1944): sugar plantations in the West Indies supplied capital for British industry and calories for workers 
Joseph Inikori (2002): 
Goods produced by enslaved people, particularly sugar and cotton, were vital inputs to Britain’s industrializing economy
Africa and the Americas were important markets for British manufactures

--- Slide 7 ---
Modern Versions of the Exploitation Thesis
Beckert (2014):  
Colonial exploitation and slavery was required to supply the key input for industrialization: cotton
Colonial activities by western states deindustrialized Asian economies
Colonial powers prevented colonized countries from imposing protection and supporting their own industries

--- Slide 8 ---
Problems With the Exploitation Thesis
Economic growth is not zero-sum. Exploitation made individuals in Western countries extremely rich.   But the scale of modern economic growth cannot simply be accounted for by transfers from colonized countries to colonizers
Spain and Portugal conquered vast colonial empires, but their economies stagnated after 1600
The links between British colonial activates in the Caribbean and the development of an innovative economy in the Midlands and northwest of England after 1760 were indirect

--- Slide 9 ---
King Cotton? 
Textiles were among the leading sectors of the Industrial Revolution
The price of cotton fell by 85% between 1780 and 1850
Beckert (2014) argues that slave-produced cotton textiles were critical to early industrialization
In contrast, Mokyr (2009) argues “If there had been no cotton, there still would have been an Industrial Revolution, even if its exact shape would have differed.”



--- Slide 10 ---
Slave Trades
Slave trades: the most odious aspect of colonization
Colonial powers brought over 12 million slaves to the New World
By 1850, Africa’s population was only half of what it would have been had the slave trades never taken place
Beyond the social and psychological costs of slavery, what were its economic costs in Africa?
Number of slaves in the transatlantic slave trade by carrier

--- Slide 11 ---
The Legacy of the Slave Trade
Nunn (2008): countries with high “slave exports” have lower GDP, greater ethnic fractionalization, and worse political institutions today
The gap between high- and low-slave export African countries is growing
Suggests slave trades might be responsible for both the relative poverty of sub-Saharan Africa and differences of income within Africa
Why have the effects of slavery persisted?

Slave “exports” vs 2018 log GDP per capita

--- Slide 12 ---
The “Residue” of the Slave Trades
Trust
Slaves captured in raids by neighboring groups and kidnapping by close friends, neighbors
Led to culture of mistrust that persists to the present (Nunn and Wantchekon 2011)
Key reason why parts of Africa have had difficult time developing
Ethnic fractionalization
Slave trade broke apart inter-ethnic alliances, led to fractionalization
Less economic growth, more conflict
Africa’s ethnic boundaries prior to colonization

--- Slide 13 ---
The Resource Grab
European colonizers mostly set up institutions in their colonies to maximize resources extracted
Limited rights for natives, low investment in public goods
Post-colonial governments found these extractive institutions useful as well
Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001): this is a key reason “bad” political institutions persist in former extractive (but not settler) colonies
Europeans only settled where settler mortality was low
Settler mortality vs. Institutional Quality

--- Slide 14 ---
More Examples of Extractive Colonial Institutions
Sokoloff and Engerman (2000): reversal of fortunes in Americas due to places with good agricultural suitability getting extractive institutions that strongly favored a small landholding elite
Dell (2010): those living in the boundaries of the South American mining mita (forced labor) have less consumption and worse health today
Lowes and Montero (2021): those living in Congo rubber concession zone have much worse education, wealth, and health outcomes today

--- Slide 15 ---
Colonial Taxation
Colonial tax systems that gave power to local authorities were often retained after independence
Banerjee and Iyer (2005): decades after Indian independence, places in which rights were given to local landlords continued to have greater inequality, lower agricultural productivity, and less investment in health and education
Iyer (2010): areas formerly under British rule have much less access to schools, health centers, and roads, likely due to weaker public good provision under the British
Tax Collection Institutions and Irrigation

--- Slide 16 ---
Poor Political Design
Colonial powers ruled people they knew little about, leading to poor political design
Dippel (2014): forced co-existence of Native American tribes, who typically made decisions at “sub-tribe” level led to poor local governance, more poverty today
Michalopoulos and Papaioannou (2016): “Scramble for Africa” meant arbitrary borders created across ethnic group lines, triggered post-independence political violence, political instability, discrimination, and ethnic wars
Partitioned African Ethnic Groups

--- Slide 17 ---
Silver Linings of Colonialism?
The colonial experience was highly heterogeneous
Some economies stagnated under colonial rule, others developed rapidly
Economic development obviously does not excuse injustices that took place
Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012): real wages grew considerably in British colonial Africa 
Growth rate in Accra (1900-1960) comparable to London (1840-1900)
Feyrer and Sacerdote (2009): former island colonies tend to be better off the longer they were under colonial rule
NONE of these, studies, however, compare the colonial experience against the counterfactual where colonization did not occur!

--- Slide 18 ---
Railways
Railroads: among the most important goods provided by some colonial powers
Built largely for military and (self-serving) economic reasons
By 1930, 67,247 km of track laid in India (fourth largest in world)
Increased income, trade, and market integration throughout British India (Donaldson 2018)
Ghana: places with colonial railroads are more developed today despite many of the railroads going into disuse after independence (Jedwab and Moradi 2016)
Indian Railway Network, 1930

--- Slide 19 ---
Other Public Goods and Education
Dell and Olken (2020): Dutch Cultivation System in Java had unintentional effect of increasing public good investment
Need infrastructure to get raw sugar to refinery
Cultivation regions currently have more access to electricity, better schools
Huillery (2009): around 30% of current performance in education, health, and infrastructure in French West Africa explained by colonial investment
Note: these were not benevolent policies of colonizers … they were unintentional consequences
Dutch Cultivation System

--- Slide 20 ---
Missionaries
Cagé and Rueda (2016): places near Protestant missions with printing presses have numerous contemporary socio-economic outcomes that are much better
Valencia Caicedo (2018): proximity to (Catholic) Jesuit missions is associated with higher education attainment and income today
Literature does not claim that it was something about Christian values or theology that enabled some of the colonies to eventually escape the poverty trap … it was likely an unintended consequence of missionary activity aimed at religious conversion

As part of the colonization process, Christian churches sent missionaries to far-off lands with the goal of converting the local populations
Woodberry (2012): missionary activity correlated with democracy, likely due to increased mass education

--- Slide 21 ---
Chapter Summary
Did colonization make parts of Europe rich? Did it keep the colonized poor?
The debate for whether it made Europe rich centers on colonization’s effect on industrialization; this is discussed in more detail in Chapter 8
The weight of the evidence is that colonization has slowed catch-up growth in many parts in the formerly colonized world
Bad institutions persisted long after independence
Cultural legacies of colonization’s most pernicious features (e.g., slave trade)
There were some silver linings of colonialism (education, public goods), though it is difficult to know how those regions would have grown in the absence of colonialism
