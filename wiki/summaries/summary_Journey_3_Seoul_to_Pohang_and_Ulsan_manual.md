# Journey 3: Seoul to Pohang and Ulsan

> **Source:** Manual PDF

### Overview
This text explores the divergence in industrial development trajectories between the "North-East Asian" developmental states (Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan) and the "South-East Asian" experience (specifically Malaysia). It posits that North-East Asian success was driven by a state-led, history-informed approach—often inspired by the German "Historical School" and Friedrich List—that focused on infant industry promotion, technology absorption, and, crucially, strict "export discipline." In contrast, the text argues that Malaysia’s attempt to emulate this model under Mahathir Mohamad suffered from a misunderstanding of structural economic mechanisms, particularly the failure to force private entrepreneurs to compete and innovate within the global market.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The "River of Development" Metaphor:** Drawing on Friedrich List, the text argues that developing nations are essentially "descending the same stretch of the river," necessitating state intervention to protect and nurture manufacturing until firms can compete globally.
*   **Export Discipline:** This is identified as the single most critical mechanism for success. States like South Korea (under Park Chung Hee) and Japan (via MITI) did not just subsidize firms; they used those subsidies as "bait" to force entrepreneurs to export. This exposed domestic firms to international quality standards, forcing efficiency and technological upgrading.
*   **The Role of Entrepreneurs:** The text argues that governments should not attempt to replace the private sector (as often happened in Malaysia) but rather "catch and control" the "animal spirits" of entrepreneurs. By using rents, licenses, and credit as leverage, the state forces profit-seeking individuals to fulfill national developmental objectives.
*   **Institutional Learning:** Industrialization is described as a "vocational college" process. Companies like POSCO and Hyundai (HMC) were explicitly used as schools to absorb and master foreign technology—often by splitting technological challenges into smaller pieces and avoiding dependency on equity joint ventures that might restrict export potential.
*   **The Failure of "Crony Capitalism" without Discipline:** The text distinguishes between the "crony capitalism" of South-East Asia (concessions without obligations) and the "developmental state" of North-East Asia (concessions contingent on export performance).

### Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & The Malthusian Epoch:** The text contrasts the modern industrial success of North-East Asia with the limitations of raw-commodity-dependent economies. It highlights how these states successfully escaped the trap of low-productivity agriculture by transitioning to manufacturing, emphasizing that agricultural productivity was a *prerequisite* for financing early industrialization.
*   **Institutions:** The text emphasizes the importance of a professional, "generalist" bureaucracy (like MITI or the EPB) that focuses on problem-solving rather than rigid adherence to neo-classical economic theory. It highlights that the most successful states held "efficiency-oriented" economists at a distance until they had already reached the global technological frontier.
*   **Geography and Culture:** While acknowledging cultural factors (e.g., Mahathir’s tea-drinking etiquette), the text explicitly rejects "cultural theories" as explanations for economic outcomes. Instead, it argues that structural economic causes—export discipline, competition, and technological sovereignty—are the primary drivers of success, regardless of location or tradition.
*   **The Demographic Transition:** Although not the primary focus, the text references the role of labor-intensive textiles as an early development phase, which aligns with standard models of moving surplus rural labor into higher-value-added manufacturing settings to drive sustained GDP growth.
