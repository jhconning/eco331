## Theme 1: Geography and Development

**1. Diamond's Big Thesis: Is continent shape destiny?**
Jared Diamond argues that the *horizontal* orientation of Eurasia allowed crops, animals, and technology to spread easily along similar latitudes, while Africa and the Americas had *vertical* orientations with different climate zones blocking diffusion. But critics argue this is too deterministic. Can you think of cases where geography *didn't* determine outcomes? What role did human agency play in counteracting geographic constraints?

**2. The Regression Problem: Is geography correlated with income, or does it *cause* income?**
Geography-based explanations face a serious econometric challenge: many geographic variables are correlated with each other and with historical institutions. Jeff Sachs argues being landlocked or in a malaria zone slows growth. But how do we know geography is the *cause* rather than a *correlate* of some deeper factor? What would a clean causal test look like?

**3. Geography, Disease, and State Formation**
The tsetse fly and malaria belt regions are less likely to have developed large-scale states (Alsan, 2014; Gallup and Sachs, 2001). If disease prevented state formation, and state formation is necessary for property rights and development, does this mean some regions were trapped in a geography-to-institutions loop? Or can states be built regardless of disease environment?

**4. Can Infrastructure Break the Geographic Trap?**
Roman roads remained the backbone of European trade for over a millennium after Rome's collapse. More recently, countries invest heavily in ports, railroads, and internet connectivity to overcome geographic disadvantages. Does this mean geography is *ultimately* not fate? And if so, why haven't more infrastructure investments succeeded in landlocked or tropical regions?

## Theme 2: Malthusian Trap and Escape

**5. Technology in the Malthusian Trap: Progress That Didn't Help**
Galor's central puzzle is that for roughly 300,000 years, technological progress raised *population* but not *living standards*. Why didn't people simply stop having children and enjoy higher incomes? What was the mechanism that prevented that from happening? And what finally changed in the 19th century to break this pattern?

**6. The Preventative Check: Did Europe's Marriage Pattern Matter?**
Greg Clark argues that the high-pressure demographic regimes of poor countries today resemble pre-industrial Europe — birth rates remain high even as death rates fall from modern medicine, leading to *lower* per-capita income. But in early modern England, delayed marriage lowered birth rates and raised incomes. Was Europe's "European Marriage Pattern" a cultural accident, or was it a rational response to scarcity? Could similar demographic transitions be engineered today through policy?

**7. The Black Death as an "Accidental Escape Mechanism"**
Voigtlander and Voth argue that the Black Death moved Europe from a low-income to a high-income Malthusian equilibrium — not by lowering fertility, but by raising the death rate, which raised wages, which raised urbanization, which raised mortality again, creating a self-sustaining high-wage trap. This is a deeply paradoxical argument: *catastrophe led to prosperity*. Is this argument convincing? Does it apply to any modern cases?

**8. Quality vs. Quantity: Galor's "Quantys and Qualys"**
Galor's unified theory pivots on a quality-quantity tradeoff in human reproduction. "Quantys" maximize children; "Qualys" invest heavily in fewer. Technological change rewarded Qualys — those who invested in skills — giving them an evolutionary and economic edge. Eventually Qualys dominated, fertility fell, and sustained growth began. Is this a biological story, a cultural story, or an economic incentive story? Can this framework explain why the demographic transition happened in England before Africa?

## Theme 3: Institutions and Development

**9. North's "Rules of the Game": What Makes Informal Institutions So Sticky?**
Douglas North distinguishes formal rules (laws, constitutions) from informal constraints (norms, customs, social expectations). He argues informal constraints are far more resistant to change than formal rules. Why would this be? Can you think of examples where formal rule changes failed because informal norms didn't change with them? What does this imply for development policy that tries to transplant institutions?

**10. Engerman and Sokoloff: Did Factor Endowments Determine the Americas' Institutional Futures?**
In 1700, the Caribbean colonies (Haiti, Barbados) were among the *richest* places in the world per capita — far wealthier than the future United States. A century later, the U.S. was pulling ahead. Engerman and Sokoloff argue that crop suitability (sugar vs. grain) determined whether colonies developed slave-based extractive institutions or broad-based inclusive ones. Does geography therefore determine institutions? Or is there room for different choices within the same crop environment?

**11. Inclusive vs. Extractive Institutions: Are Extractive Institutions *Ever* Growth-Promoting?**
Acemoglu and Robinson contrast "inclusive" institutions (broad property rights, competitive markets, constraints on elites) with "extractive" institutions (concentrated power, rent extraction). But critics note that some extractive regimes *did* industrialize rapidly — Stalinist USSR, early-phase South Korea under Park, or China today. Does the inclusive/extractive distinction fully capture what drives development, or is it too binary?

**12. Korea: A Perfect Natural Experiment?**
North and South Korea share the same geography, culture, and history up to 1950. Today South Korea has a per-capita income 20 times higher than the North. This seems like the strongest possible evidence that institutions drive development. But is the comparison fully "clean"? What other factors (geopolitical position, access to technology, U.S. aid, colonial industrial legacy) might also explain the divergence?

## Cross-Cutting Questions (Geography × Malthus × Institutions)

**13. Which Comes First: Geography or Institutions?**
Santiago's synthesis nicely captures the core issue: Engerman and Sokoloff show that geography (crop suitability) shaped the type of institutions that emerged, which then determined long-run development. But if geography shapes institutions, is the geography-institutions debate actually a debate, or is geography always the deeper cause? Is there any case where institutions clearly evolved *independently* of geographic starting conditions?

**14. Can Strong Institutions Overcome Bad Geography — and Vice Versa?**
Jeff Sachs argues some countries face nearly insurmountable geographic disadvantages (malaria, landlocked, poor soils). Acemoglu and Robinson respond that with the right institutions, these constraints can be overcome. Botswana (landlocked, low rainfall) has dramatically better institutions and higher income than most of its neighbors. Is Botswana proof institutions trump geography, or an exception that proves the rule?

**15. Why Does the Malthusian Escape Happen in Some Places and Not Others?**
The Malthusian trap lasted ~300,000 years and then broke very rapidly in Britain around 1800, with much of the world following over the next two centuries, and some regions still struggling. Given that geography, technology, and biological drives are roughly similar across humanity, what made Britain the first to escape? Geography? Institutions? Cultural traits? Chance? And why haven't all places escaped?

**16. Colonial Institutions and Path Dependence: Are Poor Countries Trapped?**
Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson argue that colonial powers set up extractive institutions in regions with high settler mortality (mostly tropical), which persisted after independence. Samiul Alam asks: if we can see the "fingerprints" of colonial institutions in modern outcomes, can reform undo them — or does institutional history suggest these paths are essentially irreversible without a systemic shock? What historical cases speak to whether institutional traps can be broken?

**17. The Modern Malthusian Trap: Does Geography Still Matter for Escaping Poverty?**
Galor argues that the Malthusian trap was broken globally by the demographic transition and industrialization. But NILGUN YURDAKUL points out that landlocked African countries still grow more slowly — does geography continue to create "Malthusian-like" conditions where rising populations outpace gains from technology? Or do institutions now fully mediate whatever geographic constraints remain? What would Galor predict for a landlocked, resource-poor country with weak institutions?
