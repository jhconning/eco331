Establishing Economic Property Rights  
by Giving Away an Empire
Douglas W. Allen    Simon Fraser University
Abstract
The first land grant act for a transcontinental railroad and the Homestead Act 
were both passed in 1862. Each gave away massive federal lands, created incen-
tives to rush construction and settlement, and (at least partly) dissipated land 
values. I argue that these apparently wealth-reducing actions rationally utilized 
railroads and settlers to establish meaningful federal ownership over the frontier 
and allowed for future federal land sales to take place. This paper examines the 
tenuous US sovereignty in the West, the institutional details of the land grants, 
and (using newly digitized records) the relationship among railroads, home-
steads, and cash sales to test the theory that “giving away an empire” was a ra-
tional strategy to establish economic property rights through quick occupation.
Between 1850 and 1872 the federal government gave an empire 
of land to promoters who promised to build railroads across the 
sparsely settled territory of the West. (Fogel 1964, p. 3)
1. Introduction
Economic property rights are necessary for the creation of wealth, but these 
rights must be established and maintained, and methods vary according to cir-
cumstances. In this context, the American West has provided numerous oppor-
tunities for understanding why and how such rights came to exist. 1 One import-
ant method of establishing rights to land is through occupation, and here again 
the West provides an excellent setting, given the use of land grants to railroads 
and homesteaders.

Without considering their role in establishing property rights, these massive 
land giveaways make little sense. Between 1850 and 1871 (70 percent between 
1862 and 1866), around 80 grants, making up an area just larger than Texas, were 
issued by Congress to seven railway systems 2—creating a transcontinental rush 
that induced rail construction 20 years prematurely. Likewise, homestead grants 
encouraged settlers to rush, and this was exacerbated by placing homesteads near 
the railroad land grants.3 Why was there a desire for rushing, why was a cumber-
some and dissipating empire of land used to induce this rushing, and why were 
settlers allowed to rush and dissipate valuable lands close to railroads?4
The hypothesis here is that railroad and homestead land grants were a single 
federal strategy to rapidly populate the frontier to establish effective property 
rights over their fragile legal claims in light of both internal and external threats 
to sovereignty.5 Hence, it was not just a matter of coordinating railroads and set-
tlers to move to the frontier but rather of the federal government actively accel-
erating development beyond what narrow economic interests might warrant, 
even accounting for the complementarity between rail and settlement, so that the 
West would remain part of the United States.
In the mid-19th century, the Union’s economic rights to the frontier—the ex-
pectation that decisions made with respect to the lands would be carried out—
were tenuous. The aggressive rush to develop and settle the West frustrated the 
land claims of others. 6 Rushing was a costly option to establish economic own-
ership, but in light of the alternatives at the time it was rational and allowed for 
future land sales. Thus, “giving away an empire” was a matter of exploiting pri-
vate incentives that were compatible with the state’s objective of sovereignty. The 
state wanted the West occupied by settlers, and settlers and railroads were com-
plements. By giving land away to both settlers and railroads, the state induced a 
massive and immediate settlement of western lands.

2. Essential Details of Land Grants
2.1. Railroad Land Grants
The historical details of railroad land grants are as dense as a map of tracks 
on the Eastern Seaboard but can be briefly summarized. Both railroads and land 
grants were common, but separate, at the turn of the 19th century. Although fed-
eral funds were made available to Ohio in 1802–3 for internal railroads, and al-
though various rights-of-way were granted to private railroads east of the Missis-
sippi, until 1845 Congress was reluctant to involve itself in any significant way in 
the new technology (Haney 1968, p. 16). Likewise, although military land grants 
had been given to soldiers after the Revolutionary War, Mexican-American War, 
and War of 1812, early grants for railroads were for narrow rights-of-way; acre-
age for depots, timber, and gravel; and workshops and stations. By later stan-
dards, the lands granted by 1830 were quite insignificant (Haney 1968, pp. 164–
68; Sanborn 1899, pp. 9–13).
The first major railroad land grant was made in 1850 when the Illinois Cen-
tral, Mobile and Chicago, and Mobile and Ohio railroads were granted six even- 
numbered sections of land per mile on either side of track if the rail from Chicago 
to New Orleans/Mobile were completed within 10 years.7 Like subsequent grants, 
this one respected lands that were already sold, preempted, or otherwise encum-
bered, and the federal government substituted other public lands within 15 miles 
of the track for them (An Act Granting the Right of Way, and Making a Grant of 
Land to the States of Illinois, Mississippi, and Alabama, 9 Stat. 466 [1850]; Ellis 
1948, p. 254). Table 1 shows some basic aspects of railroad land grants across the 
seven major western systems.
During the 1850s, Congress struggled with the form and constitutionality of 
aid to private rail firms, particularly across the public lands to the Pacific. When 
the Civil War removed the Southern objectors to railroad land grants, a united 
Congress passed a series of acts that accounted for the bulk of all land grants. 
The Pacific Railroad Acts start in 1862 (12 Stat. 489) with a grant for the Union 
Pacific. Initially no firm rushed to take up the offer, and a series of acts followed, 
most notable of which were in 1864 (13 Stat. 356) and 1866 (14 Stat. 66), that 
sweetened the pot on most margins: longer completion times, shorter lengths of 
completion for patenting, and release from government bonds. The last of the 
railroad land grants was made in 1871, and the seven major systems accounted 
for about 83 percent of the net acreage transferred (Greever 1951, p. 83).
Despite their differences, the major land grants were similar in key respects. 
Land was given in alternate sections on either side of the track for 6–40 miles, 
which created a wide swath of checkerboard private railroad holdings through a 
state or territory (Greever 1951, p. 84). 8 All grants required railroads to provide

2.2. Homesteading
The Homestead Act was passed in 1862 (12 Stat. 392), the same year as the 
first private railroad land grant. 10 The act allocated a quarter section (160 acres) 
of unappropriated public land based on first possession after the land had been 
federally surveyed and made available for homesteading. Only citizens who had 
“never borne arms against the United States Government” were eligible, and 
once claimed a $10 registration fee was paid, an affidavit of “allegiance to the 
Government” was sworn, and the homesteader was to remain on the land, build a 
house, and cultivate the land for 5 years before title was transferred (12 Stat. 392). 
The survey requirement meant that the state could direct where settlement would 
take place, and therefore the location of homesteading—like the location of rail-
road land grants—was a reflection of its intentions. 11
Like the railroad land grants, homestead grants created a rush to claim areas 
designated for settlement. As a result, the costs of rushing dissipated the value of 
the land, and for the marginal settler there would have been full dissipation. Un-

3. A Conceptual Framework of Land Grants
Figure 1 provides a simple conceptual framework for considering the role of 
land grants in establishing property rights. Although justified later, it is assumed 
that the federal government has ruled out the use of force and has decided to 
establish sovereignty through occupation. The federal government does this by 
choosing two instruments: the subsidy received for a homestead and the size of 
a railroad land grant. The railroad, seeing the size of the land grant subsidy, then 
decides whether to build by a given date. Once the railroad has committed to this 
date, the settler decides when to arrive and lay claim to the homestead, given the 
homestead subsidy. The model that follows solves this through backward induc-
tion.

3.4. The Rationale for Homesteading and Railroad Land Grants
The above analysis assumes that the frontier was perfectly owned and the in-
terest in occupation at t 4 was exogenously given. However, the frontier was not 
perfectly owned, as challenges came from Native Americans, foreign powers, and 
the Confederacy (more below). If settlers lost battles to hostile groups, and if the 
federal government was unable or unwilling to defend the lands with force, then 
it risked losing its legal claims to the frontier. In 1862 the federal government 
wanted occupation as soon as possible.
The analysis also assumes that the homestead subsidy could not exceed the 
price of the land. In the 1860s the federal government was fiscally constrained 
by the war, war debt, and reconstruction. This fiscal constraint meant that dollar 
payments to settlers and enforcement of legal dominion through military force in 
the West was infeasible.
Early occupation by homesteaders and railroad grants solved both problems. 
Large numbers of dense settlements thwarted the claims of others based on force, 
without the state incurring large military expenditures. Given that the federal 
government was unconstrained in the amount of land it could give a railroad, 
large grants could always be made to induce the settlement of homesteaders 
across the various regions. Once ownership was established through occupation, 
the federal government could sell the remaining federal lands and leave further 
rail development up to the private decisions of the railroad.
4. Evidence
This section provides evidence for the model in terms of historical context, 
institutional detail, and econometric testing. It is interesting, however, that the 
broad historical facts are consistent with the model. First, although railroad land 
grants were initiated in 1862, there were no takers. However, the state did not 
make adjustments to homesteading but rather adjusted on the unconstrained 
margin: the size of the railroad grant l. Once this was increased in 1864, railroads 
started to compete to build, and homesteaders followed.
Second, the model is broadly consistent with the historical evidence on timing, 
which suggests that settlers’ arrival dates without homestead land grants were 
around t1 ≈ 1890 and with just homesteading were at t 2 ≈1885 (see, for example, 
Fogel [1960] on these dates). The frontier was so isolated that the low land val-
ues created small, insufficient homestead rushes. As a result, homesteading alone 
would have been insufficient to establish the sovereignty required in the 1860s. 
However, a railroad grant of l* induced completed construction by t 3 = 1869, 
which created an incentive for homesteaders to arrive at t4 = 1864. Hence, it was

4.1. Tenuous Sovereignty and Congressional Awareness
A necessary condition for the hypothesis is a tenuous US sovereignty over the 
West in the mid-19th century. The evidence for this is extensive and requires only 
a brief summary. The 1840s and 1850s saw a massive increase in the size of the 
United States, mostly brought about by negotiations with foreign powers.23 How-
ever, despite the establishment of legal rights to the lands west of the Mississippi, 
in practice sovereignty was tenuous in midcentury because of threats from Native 
Americans, foreign powers, and the Confederacy.
Confrontations with Native Americans escalated dramatically in the mid-19th 
century. The speed of land acquisition, the rush of gold miners, the migration 
of Mormon settlers, the steady push of eastern tribes into the West, the limited 
western federal military presence, the resistance to movement by Native Ameri-
cans and concentration on reservations, the ineffectiveness of tribal organizations 
to enter into common-law treaties, the corruption among federal land agents, 
and the constant encroachment of squatters transformed native life in 2 decades 
and led to an escalating number of violent confrontations that could not feasibly 
be dealt with militarily.24 War resulted: 190 battles in the 1850s, 786 in the 1860s, 
530 in the 1870s, and 131 in the 1880s (Anderson and McChesney 1994, p. 58).
Added to this chronic hostility was the threat of foreign nations. Border dis-
putes with Britain continued until 1859, and both France and Britain made dip-
lomatic overtures to aid Texas, California, and even the Confederacy (Axelrod 
1993, p. 155). At various times in the mid-19th century, worry over threats from 
Mexico, Spain, and even Russia surfaced, and as late as 1849, when the Office of 
Indian Affairs was created, the federal government could not effectively deal with 
sovereignty issues by force. Finally, one cannot underestimate the impact of the 
Civil War on the Union’s expectation of sovereignty in the West. In 1862 it was 
far from clear which side would win, and the possibility of a powerful new nation

4.2. Institutional Details of Land Grants
The institutional details of the homesteading and railroad land grants are con-
sistent with a goal of fast and concentrated occupation. The first, and most ob-
vious, fact is that both the Homestead Act and the first act establishing railroad 
land grants were passed in the same year.29 It was not just any year: 1862 was pos-
sibly the nadir of Union expectations of survival, and Congress was focused on 
matters of national security.30 Had the acts been passed in widely different years, 
or at a time of security, the hypothesis would be refuted.
In addition to simultaneous creation, the details of the railroad land grants 
were complementary to homesteading. For example, railroad lands were given in 
alternating sections of 640 acres and then sold in quarter sections. By giving the 
railroads every other section of land, the value of the subsidy to the railroad was 
clearly reduced. For any amount of land, the same acreage could have been trans-
ferred through contiguous sections next to the track. However, such continuous 
land holdings may have had multiple uses: ranching, mineral, timber, or indus-
trial, but most of these uses would have resulted in a low population density. By 
forcing the sale of noncontiguous lands, the railroads were constrained to sell the 
land for agricultural uses.31
Furthermore, each railroad grant stated that “all lands hereafter granted in 
aid of railways be sold by State or corporation to actual settlers, in quantities no 
greater than one quarter-section, to any one person” (Cong. Globe, 40th Cong., 
2d Sess., p. 4429 [1868]). This clause for railroad land grants aligned it with the 
1862 Homestead Act, which stated that the lands were to go to “actual settlers,” 
private individuals who would farm and live on the land (An Act to Secure 
Homesteads to Actual Settlers on the Public Domain, Pub. Law No. 37-64, 12 
Stat. 392). Like homesteads, the railroad lands were to be sold in small 160-acre 
quarter sections and to only one person. This was intended to have a family living 
on every quarter section sold, just like homesteads. Along a similar line, when the 
land grant railroad came upon a squatter, the settler’s possession was recognized 
to maintain occupation, and the railroad was given similar sized lands outside the 
land grant swath.
Two features of railroad grants encouraged fast construction. First, payment of 
the grant was not made until construction of a given amount of track was com-
pleted and inspected (patented), which had the obvious benefit of removing a 
major moral hazard on the part of the railroad. Had payments been made first, 
railroads would have delayed construction until it was privately profitable to lay

4.3. Railroads, Homesteading, and Land Sales
According to the model, in a territory of tenuous sovereignty, homesteading 
should have occurred near the railroad, homesteading should have taken place 
before the railroad was completed, and any land sales should have lagged home-
steading in both time and space. This last implication is a critical test of the prop-
erty rights hypothesis. In principle, land sales and homesteading could occur at 
the same time and in the same area because all of the land for settlement was 
available for sale. However, when legal property rights to land are tenuous, in-
dividuals are unwilling to purchase land. Thus, homesteading should have taken 
place first and on the edge of the frontier where ownership was least certain, and 
therefore lagging cash sales of federal land are a litmus test of newly established 
economic property rights.
Until recently, data on land settlement were limited and of low quality.37 How-
ever, the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) has digitized and made available all 
of its historical land patent files. The BLM files contain information on land par-
cels that were transferred from the federal government to various parties. Among 
other details, each digitized BLM patent record specifies whether the land was 
given away as a homestead or sold, provides the location of the property accord-
ing to the PLSS address, and shows the year the land title was transferred. 38 The 
BLM records were matched with PLSS records for each state in question. The 
PLSS files are ArcMap shapefiles that contain the PLSS address for every section 
in the state. The two files were matched using the section’s address, and so re-
cords that do not match are parcels that remained untaken. This matching not 
only allows each BLM record to be geo-referenced and mapped, but it also creates 
a data set that contains every section of land in a state: those transferred to private 
parties and those untaken.
I test the model using the land patent files from 1862 to 1890 along and around 
the First Transcontinental route (Figure 4).39 The First Transcontinental Railroad 
was made up of the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific Railroads. Construc-
tion began on the ends (Omaha, Nebraska, and Sacramento, California) in 1864 
and concluded in 1869 when the two sections met at Promontory Summit, Utah 
Territory.

4.3.3. Homesteading and Cash Sales along the Transcontinental Route
Examining homestead and cash sale settlements along the entire transconti-
nental route allows the exploitation of exogenous differences in populations 
across the five states involved. In particular, the two eastern states (Nebraska and 
Wyoming) are strikingly different from the other three (California, Nevada, and 
Utah) in terms of the expectation of federal sovereignty in the 1860s. Whereas the 
situation in Wyoming was similar to that in South Dakota, Nebraska, and Kan-
sas, the three western states had positive exogenous population shocks prior to 
homesteading. California experienced the gold rush of 1849, Nevada had a silver 
rush in 1876–77, and in Utah Mormon settlement started in 1847.50 Although the 
question of sovereignty existed for all of the western states in 1860, to the extent 
that some states had resident settler populations already, they were more secure. 
In such states there should have been less homesteading relative to cash sales, the 
railroads should have been less important for homestead settlement, and home-
steads should not necessarily have preceded cash sales in time because miners 
would have found cash sales preferable to homesteads since they would gain full 
rights to the land immediately and without having to cultivate it. 51 In addition, 
homesteading did not simply move from east to west. Homesteading should 
move from the borders of secured territory into less secured territory. Since fed-
eral rights were relatively secure on the western edge of the railroad (Sacramento) 
as well as on the eastern edge (Omaha), it is predicted that homesteading should 
have preceded cash sales from the outside toward the middle.

One final implication of the model is that as land values increased, railroad 
land grants should have been reduced in size and at some point not be used at all. 
When land values were high, settlers were willing to immediately homestead, and 
therefore railroad land grants were not needed. Oklahoma’s unique history pro-
vides a natural experiment to test this implication.

Oklahoma was originally the quasi-sovereign Indian Territory established by 
President Andrew Jackson, who “exchanged” it for the southern lands of people 
referred to at the time as the Five Civilized Tribes. These tribes were forcibly re-
moved from 100 million acres in the South and began their Trail of Tears journey 
west for $68 million and 20 million acres in the Indian Territory (Wickett 2000, 
pp. 3–4). The process was not peaceful, and some fighting lasted until 1842. In 
the 1860s other tribes from the western plains were also relocated to the territory, 
and most settled in the eastern portions, despite having lands across the territory. 
The Indian Territory ended up having the largest concentration and total popula-
tion of Native Americans compared with any other state.
Over the last decades of the 19th century, as the surrounding states became 
settled, the unoccupied lands in the western half of the Indian Territory were 
continuously encroached by squatters, ranchers, and various outlaws. 55 Squat-
ters organized themselves into groups of boomers who attempted to take Native 
American lands en masse, which led to confrontations that had to be resolved 
through force. Throughout the 1870s and 1880s, the confrontations increased.56
In the 1880s, several acts of Congress (for example, the Dawes Act), forced 
the 30 tribes then living in the territory to sell the western, generally unoccupied 
lands. This gave the federal government legal title to the western part of the terri-
tory, but it was clearly a case of tenuous ownership given the forced sale, ongoing 
western hostilities, and potential for conflict with the tribes. Once again, the solu-
tion was quick occupation through homesteading. Between April 22, 1889, and 
July 9, 1901, seven large blocks of land constituting about half of the state were 
opened to homesteading.57
Because these giveaways of land took place relatively late in the frontier settle-
ment process, during a time when neighboring states like Kansas were essentially 
settled and railroads already crisscrossed the West, the lands were already valu-
able enough for immediate rushing; that is, individuals were willing to purchase 
the lands at a positive price. As a result, by all accounts, the rush was immediate, 
and the Oklahoma land rushes are now well entrenched in popular culture as the 
exemplar of homesteading behavior. What was absent from Oklahoma were rail-
road land grants. None were given, as the model predicts.
5. Conclusion
The conclusion to be drawn from this analysis is that despite the fre-
quent obituary notices, the “land-grant legend” is still alive. While

The question regarding land in aid of railroads that was never asked or an-
swered was why the most valuable land was used to create a dissipating rush onto 
the frontier. Here I have argued that previous researchers missed the objective of 
the federal government (to establish economic property rights) and failed to see 
how the use of two types of land grants allowed individual incentives among set-
tlers and railroads to fall into a rushing equilibrium that was incentive  compatible 
with the objective of the federal government. Had western lands been secure, 
there would have been no railroad land subsidy or homesteading.58
Instead, earlier economists criticized the use of land in aid of railroads be-
cause the value of the railroad was linked to the value of the land. Although they 
showed that the rates of return to railroads from building transcontinental lines 
in exchange for millions of acres of land were close to 0 on average, they failed to 
see that this was the outcome of a rushing equilibrium. Indeed, this dissipation 
was the major cost of allocating the land to railroads on a first-possession basis.
Even though homesteading and railroad land grants were costly in terms of 
dissipations created through rushing, from the point of view of the United States 
(particularly the northern states), the costly exercise was rational. Indeed, the 
rush was so successful and complete that within 30 years there was no doubt as to 
the economic owner of the West, and the speed and overwhelming success led to 
the ironic creation of legends of overpayment.
Aside from matters of ownership, it makes no sense to increase the risk of op-
erations to the railroad or to dissipate the value of western lands. However, rush-
ing reinforced legal claims through occupation, and using land as the prize of first 
possession provided railroads and settlers the right incentives to build and move 
west quickly.
Various forms of evidence have been shown consistent with this theory. The 
institutional details of both types of land grants, and the specific details of the 
Union Pacific, created incentives for a rushed occupation. Likewise, a detailed 
analysis of the pattern of homesteading and cash sales along the Union Pacific 
shows that the two types of land grants were complementary and used together 
and that cash sales lagged in time and space. Finally, the case of Oklahoma pro-
vides a type of falsification test: when high land values made homesteading arrive 
immediately, no railroad grants were used.

Data
The federal land patent data are from the BLM. 59 The BLM files contain the 
records of every parcel of federal land that was transferred to private individuals, 
corporations, tribes, or any other agency. The records include the legislative type 
of transfer, such as homestead grants, railroad grants, cash sales, Indian allot-
ments, and so on (there are hundreds of types).
These files also contain the PLSS locations for each land patent. The PLSS iden-
tifies a plot of land through aliquot part, section, township, and range number. 
As lands are subdivided, the aliquot parts become very complicated. Hence, in 
the BLM records, for example, one Nebraska plot is described as SW1/4 NW1/4 
16 T17N R8E, which is the southwest 40 acres in the northwest quarter section 
of section 16 in township 17 north, range 8 east. Since 98.8 percent of the home-
steads consisted of 160 acres, the aliquot parts are ignored, and the geographic 
center of the section is used as the location of each quarter. The unit of analysis is, 
therefore, the section, and a given section often has multiple homesteaders in it.
The BLM files are divided into multiple CSV files and are linked by an identifi-
cation number. The files were merged, and then all homestead, Indian allotment, 
military warrant, railroad grant, and state or territory allotment transfers were se-
lected. For the years 1860–90, these comprise almost 100 percent of the transfers. 
The new file was then matched with state PLSS files to determine which sections 
were empty and to geo-reference the data.
Once the file was entered into ArcGIS, various shapefiles were added to build 
the final data set. The important railroad information was created by Atack 
(2016). The elevation data used to create the variable for poor terrain are from the 
Natural Resources Conservation Service, and the soil quality data are from the 
productivity index grid.60
For this project, the Contiguous USA Albers projection was used to generate 
distance measurements between the centers of each section to the nearest object 
specified (for example, a railroad or stream). For latitudes and longitudes, the 
North American Datum 1983 geographic coordinate system was used to calcu-
late the decimal degrees. The definitions of variables are found in Table A1, while 
some summary statistics are provided in Table A2.
