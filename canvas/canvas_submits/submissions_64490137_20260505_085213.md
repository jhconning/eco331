Allen’s “Standard Model” worked unevenly outside Western Europe for a plethora of nuanced reasons beyond policymakers’ failures to implement it correctly. Primarily, though, the “Standard Model” presupposes conditions that many late industrializers did not yet meet. In chapter 8, Allen shows that countries like (Imperial) Russia or those in Latin America adopted tariffs, railways, and banks (or were alternatively reliant on foreign investment), but saw only limited industrial transformation because of varied success in applying mass-education, poor rural populations, fragmented domestic markets, and thin human capital. As such, the nominal prerequisites for the model were present and were applied gradually, but the social, technological, economic, and institutional infrastructure needed to make them productive were not present or were underdeveloped. 

  


Galor’s ideas vis-a-vis industrialization helps in establishing this point. According to him, industrialization was not just dependent on policy and development, but a demographic and human-capital transition, where fertility falls, education rises, and quality of labor outstrips quantity. Many of the late industrializers had high fertility, low literacy, and antiquated and anachronistic agrarian societies (as previously mentioned Russia and Latin American countries), which limited labor mobility and acquisition and expansion of skills. Without these fundamental requirements, the building blocks of industrialization as defined by Allen’s model could, at best, produce shallow and temporary (or slow) results. It is also worth it to mention that late industrializers were precisely late* industrializers, and therefore emerged in an environment already populated by multiple dominant industrial powers and (often mercantilistic/colonial) trade patterns which encouraged the export of raw materials and primary products and the import of manufactured goods. This environment therefore discouraged manufacturing, further slowing industrial progress.

The limited success of Allen’s “Standard Model” in the rest of the world cannot be explained by policy failure alone. While late industrializers attempted the same four pillars such as market integration, tariffs, banking systems, and education, the broader context described in KR Chapter 9 shows that the global environment had already changed. Early industrializers like Britain developed under favorable conditions with access to colonies, limited competition, and greater freedom to protect domestic industries. By the time other countries adopted similar strategies, they faced intense global competition, constraints from imperial powers, and less control over trade policy. As a result, even when policies were implemented effectively, they often produced weaker outcomes.

At the same time, Galor’s idea of “metamorphosis” points to a deeper structural issue. Industrialization required not just institutions but also a transformation in demographics and human capital. This included lower fertility rates, higher investment in education, and a shift toward skilled labor. Many late industrializers did not experience this transition. High population growth stretched resources thin and limited the ability to invest in education, while schooling systems often failed to produce a workforce suited for industrial economies. Without this demographic and educational shift, the components of the Standard Model could not work as intended.
