### Joseph Inikori and the Slavery/Industrialization Debate

Joseph E. Inikori’s work—most notably his 2002 book *Africans and the Industrial Revolution in England*—is a landmark in the economic history of the Atlantic world. It revitalized and expanded the debate over how much credit the Atlantic slave trade and plantation slavery deserve for sparking the British Industrial Revolution.

## 1. Summary of Inikori's Argument

Inikori challenges "internalist" explanations of the Industrial Revolution (which argue that British growth was primarily driven by domestic factors like local institutions, British culture, or internal coal deposits). Instead, he places the **Atlantic economy and African labor at the absolute center of British industrialization**.

* **From "Profits" to "Structural Linkages":** Inikori argues that looking solely at the *profit margins* of slave traders misses the point. Instead, he emphasizes the massive structural linkages the Atlantic system created.
* **The Export Market:** African labor in the Americas produced cheap raw materials (like cotton) and created a massive captive market for British manufactured goods. Inikori argues that the demand from the Americas (fueled entirely by slave labor) was the critical stimulus that forced British manufacturing to mechanize to keep up with demand.
* **Re-export Substitution:** He shows how English merchants initially traded Asian goods for enslaved people, but eventually substituted British-manufactured goods into these Atlantic markets, directly stimulating domestic British industries.
* **Shipbuilding and Finance:** The sheer volume of the Atlantic trade drove the expansion of the British shipping industry, maritime insurance, and the financial institutions (like credit and banking) that would later underwrite industrial factories.

## 2. Where He Fits in the Historiography

Inikori's work is best understood as a sophisticated defense and evolution of the **Eric Williams Thesis**.

### The Williams Thesis (1944)
In *Capitalism and Slavery*, Eric Williams (later the Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago) famously argued two things:
1. The profits from the triangular trade and plantation slavery provided the capital accumulation that bankrolled the British Industrial Revolution.
2. Abolition occurred not primarily out of humanitarianism, but because the slave system had become economically obsolete and redundant for British capitalists.

### The Cliometric Critique (1970s-1980s)
In the decades following Williams, economic historians (like Stanley Engerman and Patrick O'Brien) used quantitative methods to attack his thesis. They calculated that the profits from the slave trade constituted a very small percentage of total British national income (often estimated at less than 2% of British GDP). Therefore, they argued, slavery's profits were too small to have been the primary engine of the Industrial Revolution.

### Inikori's Synthesis (2000s)
Inikori rescues the Williams thesis from the cliometric critique. By shifting the focus from *capital accumulation (profits)* to *market expansion (structural linkages)*, Inikori shows that even if the direct profits were a small percentage of GDP, the Atlantic economy was the indispensable "growth sector" of the 18th century. Without the captive markets and raw materials produced by enslaved Africans, British industry would have faced demand bottlenecks and never scaled to the point of a "revolution."

*(Note: Modern scholars like Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson have also built on this, arguing that Atlantic trade strengthened the British merchant class, which in turn forced institutional reforms that protected property rights, indirectly sparking the IR.)*

## 3. Class Discussion Questions

Here are several questions to prompt student discussion, moving from basic comprehension to deeper synthesis:

1. **The Nature of "Capital":** The critics of the Williams thesis argued that the *profits* of the slave trade were too small to fund the Industrial Revolution. How does Inikori respond to this? If the driving force wasn't just raw cash (profits), what were the other mechanisms linking slavery to industrialization?
2. **Internal vs. External Drivers:** Robert Allen focuses heavily on internal British factors—like high wages and cheap coal—as the driver of mechanization. How does Inikori's focus on the Atlantic export market challenge or complement Allen's view? Can both be true?
3. **The "Counterfactual" Question:** Based on Inikori's reading, what would the British economy have looked like in 1850 if the Americas had never been colonized and the Atlantic slave trade had never existed? Could an Industrial Revolution have happened purely on domestic demand?
4. **Morality and Economic History:** Why do you think the debate over the economic contribution of slavery to modern capitalism is so highly contested? How does validating or rejecting the Williams/Inikori thesis change how we view the foundations of the modern global economy?
5. **The Role of the Consumer:** Inikori points out that enslaved people produced commodities (sugar, tobacco, cotton) that transformed British consumption patterns (the "Industrious Revolution"). How did the exploitation of labor in one hemisphere change the daily habits and labor supply of workers in another hemisphere?
