# Establishing Economic Property Rights by Giving Away an Empire

> **Source:** Allen, Douglas W. 2019. "Establishing Economic Property Rights by Giving Away an Empire." _The Journal of Law and Economics_  62(2): 251–80. [Paper](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1x3eA_cwjyCss7dl_wsWVnKPja2sRkEkB/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
In this paper, Douglas W. Allen challenges the traditional view of 19th-century American land grants as "wealth-reducing" or inefficient government subsidies. By analyzing the 1862 Homestead Act and the simultaneous transcontinental railroad land grants, Allen argues that these policies were a rational, coordinated federal strategy. Facing tenuous sovereignty over the Western frontier due to potential threats from foreign powers, the Confederacy, and indigenous resistance, the federal government used land grants to induce rapid occupation. By subsidizing both infrastructure (railroads) and settlement (homesteaders), the state successfully created "economic property rights"—the practical ability to enforce ownership—where legal titles alone were insufficient.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Problem of Tenuous Sovereignty:** In the mid-19th century, despite possessing legal title to the West, the U.S. federal government lacked the military resources to enforce its rule. Sovereignty was threatened by hostile powers and local conflict.
*   **The Rushing Equilibrium:** The government intentionally created a "rush" for land. By pairing railroad construction with the homesteading of 160-acre plots, the state induced individuals to occupy the frontier decades earlier than market forces alone would have dictated.
*   **Complementary Subsidies:** The two programs acted as complements: railroads provided the necessary market access, while homesteaders provided the physical occupation required to secure the territory.
*   **Backward Induction/Rational Strategy:** Allen demonstrates that the state’s decision was a cost-minimizing mechanism. When land values were low, large land grants were necessary to lure settlers. As land values increased (as seen in later cases like Oklahoma), the need for railroad subsidies vanished, and homesteading proceeded on its own.
*   **Empirical Proof:** Using digitized Bureau of Land Management (BLM) patent records, Allen shows that homesteading consistently occurred before land sales and was spatially concentrated near railroads, suggesting that the "dissipation" of land value was an intentional cost of establishing state control.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Institutions:** The paper highlights that legal institutions (titles) are meaningless without the institutional capacity to enforce them. The "giving away of an empire" was an institutional innovation designed to bridge the gap between abstract law and practical control.
*   **Economic History:** The research provides a revisionist look at the "land-grant legend," refuting claims that the railroad land grants were purely wasteful. It places the US transcontinental expansion in the context of state-building, comparable to other nations (like Canada) that faced similar challenges in securing remote territories.
*   **Geography:** Geography played a primary role in the state's calculations. Larger land grants were allocated to areas with lower land values (e.g., deserts, mountainous regions) to ensure that even the most "expensive" to settle regions were occupied quickly.
*   **The Malthusian Epoch/Demographic Transition:** The paper touches on the "rushing" behavior, where the scarcity of secure, productive land drove the urgency of settlement. It illustrates the role of population density—induced by government incentives—in transitioning the frontier from a lawless "common" to a productive, privately-owned economy.
