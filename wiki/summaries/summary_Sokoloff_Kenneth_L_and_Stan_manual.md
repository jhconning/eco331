# Institutions, Factor Endowments, and Paths of Development in the New World

> **Source:** Manual PDF

## Overview
This paper, by Kenneth L. Sokoloff and Stanley L. Engerman, investigates the "Great Divergence" in economic performance between the United States and Canada compared to the rest of the New World. While many early observers predicted that the Caribbean and Latin American colonies would be the primary engines of economic prosperity due to their resource wealth, the North American colonies eventually achieved sustained long-term growth. The authors argue that this divergence was not merely a product of national heritage or religion, but rather the result of initial factor endowments that shaped the evolution of long-lasting economic and political institutions.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Role of Factor Endowments:** The authors argue that initial conditions—specifically climate, soil quality, and the density of native populations—determined the economic structure of each colony.
    *   **Plantation Economies:** Regions with climates suitable for sugar and other lucrative staples (e.g., the Caribbean, Brazil) developed economies based on large-scale slavery, leading to extreme inequality in wealth and human capital.
    *   **Mining/Conquest Economies:** Regions with rich mineral resources and dense native populations (e.g., Mexico, Peru) utilized existing social hierarchies to concentrate wealth and power among a small European elite.
    *   **Smallholder Economies:** Regions like the Northern United States and Canada, which lacked both economies of scale for plantation crops and large indigenous labor forces, developed into societies with more homogeneous populations and more equitable distributions of wealth.
*   **Institutional Persistence:** Initial inequality created a path-dependent process. Elites in unequal societies established legal and political institutions—such as restrictive voting rights, land tenure systems, and limited access to education—that reinforced their status and prevented the majority of the population from participating in the economy. Conversely, the more equitable North American colonies fostered institutions that promoted broader access to opportunity, such as universal suffrage and widespread, publicly funded primary education.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History:** The paper contributes to the literature on long-run economic growth by shifting the focus from "culture" to "endowments." It demonstrates that economic outcomes are not fixed at birth but are the result of institutional choices made in response to geographic realities.
*   **Institutions:** The authors highlight how "extractive" institutions (common in Latin America) protect the privileges of a few, while "inclusive" institutions (common in the U.S. and Canada) promote widespread economic participation. This serves as a foundational text for understanding institutional economics.
*   **Geography:** The paper demonstrates how geography dictates the "comparative advantage" of a region, which in turn necessitates specific labor systems (e.g., slavery vs. free labor) that set the trajectory for social and political inequality.
*   **Demographic Transition/Human Capital:** The authors identify the "common school movement" in the U.S. and Canada as a critical driver of development. Because these societies were more homogeneous and less unequal, they were able to invest more effectively in human capital, which in turn fueled further industrialization and economic divergence.
