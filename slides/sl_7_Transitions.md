## Fall of the Roman Empire
![bg right:40% w:600 Image of West East Roman](attachments/West_East_roman.png)
- **Power Vacuum (c. 476 CE):** Collapse of the Western Roman Empire eliminated centralized governance and the protection of the Roman legions.
- **Economic Contraction:**
  - De-urbanization. Rome's population fell from 1 million to a few tens of thousands.
  - Collapse of long-distance trade and disappearance of formal monetary economy.
- **Adaptation:** Local populations needed security from invasions (Germanic tribes, later Vikings and Magyars).

---
## The Rise of Feudalism
![bg right:40% w:600 Image of Feudalism Structure](attachments/feudalism_structure.png)
- **A Response to Chaos:** Feudalism was a decentralized military and political system that emerged to fill the power vacuum.
- **Oaths and Loyalty:** Kings granted land (fiefs) to lords and vassals in exchange for military service (knights). 
- **Chains of Obligation:** Personal loyalty and localized control replaced imperial bureaucracy and formal Roman law. 

---
### Manorialism: The Economic Foundation
- **Economic Fragmentation:** The massive, commercial Roman *latifundia* gradually evolved into local, self-sufficient medieval manors.
- **Serfdom:** Independent peasants traded their labor and their mobility in exchange for physical protection from the local lord.
  - Peasants became *serfs*, legally tied to the land.
- **Local Focus:** Subsistence agriculture prioritized over market production. Focus on the *three-field system*.
- **The Starting Line for Capitalism:** This fragmented, highly coercive, non-market framework is the starting point from which Western European capitalism would eventually emerge.

## Land Tenure and Labor Services
- Crown was only _alloidal_ land owner.  All others 'held' land from the Crown.
- Lords (tenants in chief) had rights granted by the King
- Sub-tenants rented land from the Lord. Could be free or unfree.
- Serfs (villeins) not free to leave for other locations or occupations without penalties.
  - Charged rent, fines, labor-services. 
  - Could accumulate property and pass tenancy on to heirs.
  - firm rights to cropland and commons, passed down through generations.
    - Lord could not evict tenant without cause.
- Complex system of rights and obligations, varied by region and time.
- Over time serfdom ended and relationship that of customary tenants.

### Differentiation and Market activity

- villeins: serfs or unfree tenants. Tied Could not move away, marry, inherit or sell without the lord's permission.
- freeholders: had free tenure. Owed fixed rent to the lord but few feudal services. They could sell or inherit without lord's permission.
- cottars: Occupied cottages and cultivated small land lots.
- Free and unfree tenants often had significant holdings (30 acres or more)
- Often hired laborers to fulfill labor services.
- Might offer land to cotter (though lord regulated)

---
### Manorial Rights
- Waste rights : fells, mountains, marsh.  Technically ownership vested in the lord, but Lord's ability to change use by enclosure restricted by common rights of tenants (e.g. to take wood, peat, pasture).  
- Lord: Game, Fisheries, Mills, minerals.  
  - Mill tolls
- Woodland: rights to timber vs underwood.  

---
### Manorial Courts
- Infringement of lord's priviliges
  - fishing/hunting without permission
  - cutting timber/underwood w/o license
  - encroachment or intakes on lords' soil
  - not grinding corn at lord's mill
- Offenses to Public order and stability of community
  - breaching king's peace (fighting, pilfering, slanders, vagabondage)
  - keeping under-tenants or harboring 'inmates' (e.g. allowing building of cottages, etc)
- Offenses against common rights and neighborhood:
  - 'stray' livestock or grazing where not allowed
  - failure to maintain fences, gates, water channels, etc.
  - burning waste on wrong dates, etc.

## Contrasting views on the transformation of Institutions
- **The Commercialization (neo-Smithian) Model** 
  - Stresses factors such as population, the rise of markets, and the development of new technologies as driving institutional change.
  - Some economic historians, including North and Thomas (1973) went as far as to argue that serfdom was a contractual arrangement (service for protection), but the value of the services and the terms of the contract changed in response to demographic and other pressures.
  - Feudal and other institutions held back people's natural tendency to want to improve and engage in trade and specialization. Over time, as markets thickened and technologies improved, it became in the interest of groups to remove these restrictions and new institutions emerged that paved way for capitalism.

---
## Contrasting views on the transformation of Institutions
- **Marxist Perspectives**
  - Give more prominence to class conflict and the 'balance of class forces'.
  - For example would argue that from 11th to 14th century, landlords gained the upper hand and converted many formerly more free peasants into serfs.  Norman Conquest of 1066 strengthened the power of the nobility and the Church further.
  - Malthusian would say high pre-Black death population led to low wages, population collapse led to higher wages. 
  - Many marxists would argue low wages were explained by landlord exploitation rather than Malthusian factors. 


---
### Robert Brenner and the Political Marxists
  - Famous 'Brenner Debate' followed 1976 critique of neo-Smithian and neo-Malthusian views of the transition to capitalism.
  - **_Political Marxists_** such as Brenner and Wood go as far as to argue that class conflict is largely 'autonomous' or independent of demographic and technological factors. 
  - There is no 'natural' tendency to accumulate and improve, indeed under Feudalism Lords found it more profitable to squeeze peasants and build miltary might. Peasants had little incentive to accumulate and sell to the market, because markets were limited and distorted.
  - Where landlords were strong and organized they could extract surplus from peasants via extra-economic means -- via compulsions and limits on mobility such as serfdom. Where peasants could resist, peasants were more free and better off.


---

  - **Neo-Malthusian view**: high popn in England pre-Black death led to low wages and serfdom. Low population post Black Death led to higher wages and end of serfdom.
  - **Brenner critique:** The Malthusian pattern is NOT true across Europe. 
    - France: pre-Black death had high popn but strong peasant property and low rents. 
    - Eastern Europe: serfdom imposed on relatively free peasantry _after_ Black death.
  - Hence (according to Brenner) where landlords were strong and organized they could impose serfdom. Where peasants could resist, peasants were more free and better off.
  - Capitalism and 'capitalist imperatives' emerged first in England because landlords eventually prevailed in dispossesing peasants from the land, forcing them to become 'free' wage laborers, with no choice but to participate in markets.

---
### Other Marxist Perspectives  
- Not all Marxists agree with Brenner's view that class conflict is mostly 'autonomous.'
- Famous marxists such as Rodney Hinton saw the Black plague and other factors including commercialization as helping to precipitate the 'feudal crisis' after the 14th century. 
- Yet they do still place 'conflict' at the center of their arguments and argue the feudal system would have eventually faced crisisbecause it was based a largely 'non-entrepreneurial' landlord class extracting surplus from peasants via coercive means.

### Neo-smithian interpretation

* North and Thomas and neo-institutionalists fit into the "neo-Smithian" or commercialization interpretation of history.
  
* Costs of creating/enforcing PRs may exceed benefits to any group or individual. 
* But as benefits of PRs increase (driven by technology, Smithian expansion of commerce and trade, etc) benefits rise to exceed costs in creation of new PRs.
* May be resistance and delay, but inexorable trend towards more efficient PRs.
  - **Land policy in early modern Spain.**  Agriculture efficiency might have improved with more exclusive PRs to protect crop lands from _Mesta_ sheperds guild which ahd custormy rights to drive sheep across Spain (and paid royal fees).
    - Social return > private return to change.
    - Crown did not want to risk losing the Mesta guild's support.
    - Why didn't farmers just bribe sheperds to stay off their land?

## The Commercialization or neo-Smithian model

**Ellen Wood's critique**:
- This explanation of origin of capitalism presupposes it is natural outcome of human practices.  Required only maturation and removal of external obstacles.
  - Smith's "truck, barter, and trade." From the beginning, rational self-interest leading to profit maximization.  
* Conflict over institutional change may play a role, but "[in this view] capitalism had existed, at least in embryo, from the dawn of history, if not in the very core of human nature (Wood)."
- External forces help overcome obstacles:
  - technological improvements.
  - opportunities for trade expanded. 
  - expansion of autonomous cities

## Open Access vs. Private Property

- Open Access/Commons: any one can enter and use.
  - Captures the average product of labor (APL)
  $$
  APL_c=\frac{F(T,L)}{L}
  $$

- Encloser/Private owner: one owner has right to exclude others.
  - a cost of enclosing/protecting c
  - Can now charge a rent for access.
  - Or earn a profit (equivalent in competition to a rent), hiring workers.
  - Hires until $w=MPL_e$

- But market wage $w$ must match what workers can earn on the commons so 
  $$
  MPL_e= w = APL_c>MPL_c
  $$
- 'Too much' labor is allocated to the commons.

### Land insecurity (use-it-or-lose-it) tenure insecurity interpretation:
- The worker who enters the unenclosed commons earns an implied land rent
The average product a worker earns by remaining in the unenclosed sector is equal to the local wage in that sector (MPL_c) plus the value of any implied land-rents.

- In equilibrium the worker earns the same in the commons as a free worker in the enclosed sector.
$$
w_C+r_C\cdot \frac{\bar T}{L}=w_E
$$

* To preserve land-use-rights must use land and remain in community.

* Enclosed sector wage must compensate worker for leaving community and losing customary wage plus rents from surrendered land use-rights.
* Customary tenancy rights deliver are a form of land-rent.

### Evolutionary Theories of Property Rights

* Assume that property rights are costly to establish and enforce.
* At low levels of population density and few opportunities for tech/market improvement
  * Benefits of enclosure fall short of costs. 
  More efficient to keep lands unenclosed (customary tenures)
* As population pressures increase, and/or tech/mkt opportunities improves.
  * Benefits of enclosure increase relative to costs.
* Will private enclosure occur?
  * Potential efficiency gain but also rent-shifting gain to encloser.
  * Too much or too little enclosure.
  * will displaced be compensated for loss of informal rights.

## Customary Rights

- "Peasant rights in forests and woodland... grew out of persistent, everyday friction with their lords".

>The 'ancient customs' of Stillington (Yorkshire) specified that the inhabitants of each ancient messuage or cottage could take three wagonloads of turves from the common every year and as much bracken as they could gather between the rising and setting of the sun on 14 September.

> [but also sometimes] pitted the settled poor against the rootless and utterly destitute.

## Land Enclosures
- Refers to the fencing or hedging off areas of land for private use that had once been available for common use.
- The enclosure of open fields was of accompanied by the 'amalgamation' of strips of land into larger farms ('engrossment'). 
- Arable lands converted to pasture in 16C price rises.
- Enclosure led to more exclusive control and to the extinguishing of rights.
- Sometimes by agreement between landlord and tenants. But enclosed without consultation. 
- At its worst, enclosures forced tenant farmers off their land and removed the commons where a village would graze their animals
- Sometimes caused entire villages to disappear.

## Kett's rebellion (1549)
- 1540s crisis in agricutlure. Inflation, unemployment, rising rents, and declining wages.
- Enclosures of common lands, often led to loss of areas to graze animals and access to resources.
- Peasants had torn down enclosures, thought they had been acting legally, since there had been edicts against enclosures by Henry VIII.
- Peasants in Norfolk and Suffolk, led by yeoman Robert Kett, rebelled against enclosure of common lands and clergy.

---
### Kett's Rebellion (contd)
>29 grievances expressed "desire to limit the power of the gentry, exclude them from the world of the village, constrain rapid economic change, prevent the overexploitation of communal resources, and remodel the values of the clergy"
- 16000 rebels, multiple confrontations. 
- 3000 killed, 1000 hanged.

### Tudor Enclosures
- Tudor period (1485-1603). Rapid economic growth and state expansion.
  - Dissolution of the monasteries Henry VIII 1530s.
  - Many enclosures in the period but also commissions of inquiry and legislation to protect peasant rights:
    - Statute of Tillage 1486,  1515 Forest Act. 
    - 1536 Common Land Act (enclosures had to be approved by commission and compensation paid to affected)
    - 1547 "Poor Law" system of relief to the poor.
    - 1576 Statue of Artificers (required landlords provide cottages, prevent displacements).
- Rise of the Yeoman (successful peasant producers). Property rights strengthened. Expanded tenancies and holdings. Often initiated/benefited from enclosure.

## The English Agricultural Revolution
- 1520 estimate 80\% farmers subsistence, selling little to market.
- Yet by 1800 English ag yields were 2X level of middle ages and near highest in the world. 
- Output per worker 50\% higher than next highest European country.
- Institutional Revolution:
  - Most open fields had been enclosed
  - small peasant hoildings into large farms leased to tenants who cultivated them with wage labor.
  - Concentrated land ownership. High rents, stagnant wages (but pop growing fast).
- London rapid growth from 16C.  Great commercial power by 1800, reflecting growing British control over Atlantic trade. 
- First Industrial Revolution 1760-1830.

### Traditional/Triumphalist accounts of the Agricultural Revolution
- Historian Arnold Toynbee _Lectures on the Industrial Revolutio in England_ (1884).
  - an "Agricultural Revolution" (AR) was prior and necessary for the IR. 
  - AR was made possible by the enclosure movement. Swept aside backward and inefficient agriculture (peasant and/or feudal remnants).
  - Landowners consolidate holdings and experiment with modern agricultural techniques. 
  - Great landlord innovators ( Jethro Tull, Lord Townshend, Arthur Young, ...) and 'improving tenants' .
  - Innovations raised productivity: selective breeding of livestock, new systems of crop rotations involving turnips and clover.
  - Drainage and other improvements to land.

- All this raised productivity, allowed for surplus of food to support booming population and labor to the cities booming with trade. 

---

## Agrarian Fundamentalists (Allen's term)
The view that improved farming, modern agrarian institutions, increase in inequality and first indistrial revolution are connected. It's claims:
  1. **Technical Revolution was caused by 'modernization'**. 
    - Traditional and 'feudal' peasant farms and open fields had stifled progress. Replace small backward peasant properties with large 'modern' farms.
    - Enclosures and large farms created private property and capitalism.
2. **Growth of agricultural productivity was a pre-requisite for the Industrial Revolution**
   - Enclosures and rising productivity fed the manufacturing cities, provided freed labor and savings for industry.
3. **Increase in Inequality an inherent feature**
   - Tradeoff between equity and growth viewpoint.
   
---
### Agrarian Fundamentalism


- **Tory Triumphalists** such as Arthur Young.
  - claim enclosures maintained or increased farm employment.
- **Marxists** including Robert Brenner and Ellen Wood.
  - claim enclosures led to 'labor shedding.' 
  - Destruction of peasant property to create new  capitalist imperatives, 'necessary' for capitalism/IR.

Agrarian Fundamentalism prompted the collectivization of Soviet Agriculture, and influenced agricultural policies through out colonial and post-colonial world.

> For all those who contrast a traditional society with a modern one, for all those who argue that the traditional society must be overturned for development to ocur, for all those who see inequality as the necessary price of growth -- England is the classic case. (Allen, p2)

## Allen's view and findings
- “two agricultural revolutions,” one by yeomen farmers in the seventeenth century and another by landlords in the eighteenth,
- "large farms and enclosures were not necessary for the technical revolution in agriculture."
- Studied effect of enclosuree and farm size on cropping, employment and yields in South Midlands.  How easily did labor shift to manufacturing? What happened to wages, profits, and rents?
- the “great inventions” —clover/turnips, convertible husbandry, and New Leicester sheep— were scant contributors.

---
Food consumption:
- steadily rising output from 1520 to 1739
- stagnation during the period 1740-1800, with output up by just 10 percent
  - Food consumption per capita actually fell, from a peak no higher than the levels of the sixteenth.
- renewed acceleration from 1800 to 1850, increasing by 65 percent.

## Rise and Fall of the Yeoman
- Black death popn decline destabilized serfdom.
- Labor mobility and wages increased, rents collapsed.
- Early enclosure and conversion to pasture were seigniorial response to new order.
- 1 of 10 villages in midlands destroyed between 1450-1525.
- This eliminated small-scale agriculture and transition to capitalist agriculture.
- Reaction to enclosures actually strengthened consolidation of peasant agriculture, created secure forms of tenure. 
- Yeomen were owner-occupier farmers.  They were responsible for most productivity growth -- the agricultural revolution. 

- The later landlords revolution eliminated the yeomen, by concentrating ownership in great estates, creating large leased farms.
  - In 18th C many yeomen sold property to landlords, others lost their land when landlords changed terms. Fueled in part by growing mortgage market.  

---
### Open fields a barrier to productivity improvements?
- Allen argues larger portion of agricultural improvement happened in yeoman agricultural revolution.  Productivity doubled between 1500-1800. Enclosure played minor role.

In later landlord revolution period, did enclosure lead to new adoptions:
- In heavy-soil arable regions yes -- enclosed villages more likely to improve.
- In light-soil regions also more likely to adopt new crop rotations. 
- But these do not represent large productivity gains.
- In 1800 yiled in open and enclosed fields were similar. 

On employment:
- Enclosure had little if any effect when it did not change tillage/pasture. Where led to conversion to pasture, employment declined. In pastoral areas, the Marxist labor shedding is clsoer to the facts.

---
On rents:
- they increased a lot. Combined with finding that enclosure did not raise productivity, Allen argues that enclosure represented redistribution of income from farmers to landlords.
- Real rents increased about seven fold between 1450-1850.

## Enclosures and Agricultural Productivity

- Robert C. Allen's book _Enclosure and the Yeoman_ challenged the traditional view that enclosures were necessary for agricultural productivity.
- He argued that enclosure did not lead to increased productivity, and that open field systems, in which peasants had access to common land, had already achieved high levels of productivity.
- He suggested that productivity gains in agriculture were due to innovations in technology and methods of farming, not enclosure.


---

## Agrarian Fundamentalism

- Allen criticized the "agrarian fundamentalism" of Alwyn Young and other champions of enclosure who believed that removing peasant property rights and customary tenures was necessary for increasing productivity.
- According to Allen, the evidence from the South Midlands showed that yeoman farmers on open fields had raised agricultural productivity, disproving the view of the agrarian fundamentalists.

## Debated 'agricultural revolution'
- Agricultural Statistics are sparse, so much speculation in early period. Cereal Statistics in Britain not collected systematically until 1880s.
- **Greg Clark**: 'enclosure was mainly a capital investment induced by higher land rents relative to wages, with very little associated productivity gains.'
- Slow and steady vs. 'revolution'
>  [From 1500 to 1869] English agricultural output seems to have tripled,and [TFP]  increased by about 50%.   But this was achieved over a very long period with few abrupt changes so that the rate of growth of productivity per years was on average 0.15% ... in any twenty year period farmers would be unable to discern, given the noise of year to year fluctuations, that productivity was actually increasing.

## Push or Pull?

- What certainly happened is the structural transformation of the economy: people moved from rural areas to urban.  From agriculture to manufacturing.
- The agricultural revolution story, is a 'Push' story: rising ag productivity released labor for industry.
  - Allen pointed out that in landlords revolution, rural unemployment rose. So labor shedding, but not absorbed into industry.
- But maybe it was more of a 'Pull' story: rising wages in industry attracted labor from agriculture.

The second and third agricultural revolution
