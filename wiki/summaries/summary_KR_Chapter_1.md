# Koyama & Rubin: How the World Became Rich - Chapter 1

> **Source:** KR 1, "Why, When, and How Did the World become rich" ([1-16](https://www.amazon.com/How-World-Became-Rich-Historical-ebook/dp/B09VNRJZ31). lots of pictures, available via Amazon read sample)


## Overview
Chapter 1 of Koyama and Rubin’s work serves as the foundational introduction to the "Great Divergence." The authors pose the central question of economic history: Why are some nations incredibly wealthy while others remain impoverished? The chapter moves beyond simple geographic or purely institutional explanations, arguing that the transition to modern economic growth was not an inevitable outcome of history, but a complex process driven by the interaction of institutions, culture, and human agency.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
* **The Puzzle of Inequality:** The authors emphasize that for most of human history, living standards were stagnant. The "Hockey Stick" graph of GDP per capita represents a radical break from the Malthusian past.
* **Institutions vs. Culture:** Koyama and Rubin reject mono-causal explanations. They argue that while institutions (such as property rights and rule of law) are crucial, they are themselves the products of underlying cultural values, beliefs, and historical contingencies.
* **The Role of Incentives:** Economic growth requires incentives for innovation and investment. These incentives are mediated by the political and legal frameworks of a society.
* **Agency and Contingency:** History is not deterministic. Small, accidental changes in cultural or political environments can lead to massive long-term economic divergences.

## Connections to Course Themes

* **The Malthusian Epoch:** The chapter defines the pre-modern world by the Malthusian trap, where any gains in productivity were quickly swallowed by population growth, keeping living standards near subsistence levels.
* **Institutions:** The authors highlight that "inclusive" institutions—those that provide incentives for a broad cross-section of society to innovate—are the engines of modern growth, contrasting them with "extractive" institutions that benefit a narrow elite.
* **Culture:** Unlike pure institutionalists (like Acemoglu and Robinson), Koyama and Rubin emphasize the role of religious beliefs, social norms, and the state’s perception of economic actors. They argue culture dictates the viability of specific institutions.
* **Geography:** While acknowledging geography’s role in shaping early agricultural civilizations, the authors argue that geography is not destiny; it does not explain why certain regions (like Western Europe) managed to escape the Malthusian trap while others with similar geographical conditions did not.
* **The Demographic Transition:** The shift from "quantity" of children to "quality" of children (human capital investment) is identified as a critical component in moving from a Malthusian state to modern sustained growth.
