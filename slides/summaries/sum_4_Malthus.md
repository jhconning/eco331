# Malthus, Population, Human Capital
## Summary

### Pre-Industrial Demography
- Life expectancy in Roman Egypt ~27–28 years; 30% of infants died before age 1
- England 1700: LE ~38 years at birth — high by historical standards
- Even the wealthy were not spared (Queen Anne: 17 pregnancies, no surviving children)

### The Malthus Model
Two linked relationships determine a steady state $(y^*, N^*)$:

- **Top panel** (demography): birth rate $b(y)\uparrow$ and death rate $d(y)\downarrow$ with income $y$
  - When $b(y) > d(y)$, population grows
- **Bottom panel** (economics): $y\downarrow$ as population $N\uparrow$ (diminishing returns to land)
- **Result**: economy always returns to steady state — shocks are temporary

### Comparative Statics
| Shock | Immediate effect | Long-run result |
|---|---|---|
| Lower birth rate (preventative check) | Deaths > births → pop falls | Higher $y^*$, lower $N^*$ |
| Higher death rate (epidemic) | Deaths > births → pop falls | Higher $y^*$, lower $N^*$ |
| Technology advance | Births > deaths → pop rises | Same $y^*$, higher $N^*$ |
| Lower death rate (modern medicine) | Births > deaths → pop rises | Lower $y^*$, higher $N^*$ |

**Key insight**: In a Malthusian world, technology raises population, not living standards. Modern medicine alone can *lower* per-capita income.

### Earlier Onset of Agriculture
- Earlier agriculture → larger, more diverse populations → more specialization and technology
- Advantage was military and demographic (disease immunity, soldiers), **not** higher per-capita income
- Explains why some civilizations absorbed, conquered, or displaced others

### Historical Evidence
- English data 1200–1790s: Black Death clearly visible as income spike followed by population recovery
- Transition visible ~17th century: population rising *without* falling income per capita
- Sustained per-capita income growth only after ~1870s

### The Irish Famine — A Malthusian Case Study
1. **The Potato Trap**: 2–4× calorie yield vs. grain → population doubled to 8M+ (1750–1845); dangerous Lumper monoculture
2. **The Blight**: *Phytophthora infestans* (1845) → total crop failure; 1M dead, 1M+ emigrated
3. **Policy failure**: Ireland remained a net grain *exporter*; *laissez-faire* ideology and Poor Laws prioritized markets over relief
4. **Malthusian orthodoxy**: officials framed famine as a "natural check"; relief feared to worsen future overpopulation

### The Black Death and Multiple Equilibria (Voigtländer & Voth)
- Normally death rate falls with income. During 1350–1700, the relationship could **reverse**:
  - Plague → $y\uparrow$ → urbanization $\uparrow$ → mortality $\uparrow$ (cities were deadly)
  - $y\uparrow$ → war $\uparrow$ → mortality $\uparrow$
- This upward-sloping death rate segment creates **multiple equilibria**: a large shock could permanently move the economy to a higher steady state
- Wars also forced **state formation**: centralized bureaucratic states emerged from the pressure of near-constant conflict (Tilly)

### Escape from the Malthusian Trap — Galor's Unified Growth Theory
**Quantys vs. Qualys** (quantity–quality trade-off):
- *Quantys*: many children, little human capital investment (subsistence farmers)
- *Qualys*: fewer children, heavy investment in productivity (artisans, traders)
- When technology raises demand for skilled labor, Qualys gain an evolutionary advantage
- **Positive feedback**: tech → Qualy incomes ↑ → more Qualys → more tech

Population size and composition foster progress via innovation supply/demand, knowledge diffusion, division of labor, and trade.

### From Malthus to Solow
| Model | Key factor | Long-run $y$ |
|---|---|---|
| **Malthusian** | Land (fixed) + labor | Returns to subsistence; tech raises $N$, not $y$ |
| **Solow** | Capital (accumulated) + labor | Steady state above subsistence; one-time tech → permanently higher $y^*$ |

**Solow model**: $Y = A \cdot K^\alpha \cdot L^{1-\alpha}$; capital depreciates and must equip new workers → diminishing returns → convergence across countries with similar technology and savings rates.

**Neo-classical policy implication**: poor countries are poor due to market distortions — liberalize, privatize, deregulate.
