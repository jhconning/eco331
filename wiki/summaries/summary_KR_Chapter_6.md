# Koyama & Rubin Chapter 6: Was it Just a Matter of Colonization and Exploitation?

> **Source:** KR 6 "Was it Just a matter of Colonization and Exploitation?"


## Overview
This chapter explores the relationship between European colonial expansion and the divergence in global economic growth. It evaluates the "Exploitation Thesis"—the argument that the wealth of Western Europe was built upon the extraction of resources, slave labor, and colonial plunder—against evidence suggesting that while exploitation was massive and destructive, it may not be the sole cause of the Great Divergence. The chapter balances the significant long-term negative institutional impacts of colonialism with evidence of unintended "silver linings" such as missionary-led education and infrastructure development.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms

### The Exploitation Thesis
*   **Primitive Accumulation:** Following Marx, some scholars argue that colonial looting and slavery provided the initial capital necessary for the Industrial Revolution.
*   **Inputs for Industrialization:** Scholars like Williams, Inikori, and Beckert argue that slave-produced goods (cotton, sugar) were essential inputs for European industrial development and that colonial powers actively deindustrialized competing regions like Asia.
*   **Counter-arguments:** Koyama and Rubin note that economic growth is not a zero-sum game. While colonial wealth was significant, the scale of modern industrial growth is likely too vast to be attributed solely to colonial transfers. Furthermore, colonial powers like Spain and Portugal, who extracted vast resources, eventually experienced economic stagnation.

### Negative Legacies (The Persistence of Poverty)
*   **Institutional Extraction:** Following Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001), the authors argue that Europeans established extractive institutions in areas with high settler mortality, which persisted long after independence.
*   **Social & Cultural Erosion:** The transatlantic slave trade left a lasting "residue" of mistrust and ethnic fractionalization, which continues to inhibit economic cooperation and growth in sub-Saharan Africa.
*   **Poor Political Design:** Arbitrary borders (e.g., the "Scramble for Africa") and the forced co-existence of different ethnic groups have led to sustained political instability and violence.

### The "Silver Linings"
*   **Infrastructure:** In specific cases, colonial investments in railways (e.g., India, Ghana) or transport (e.g., the Dutch Cultivation System in Java) created positive long-term economic legacies, even when the original intent was self-serving.
*   **Human Capital:** Missionary activity, particularly through the introduction of printing presses and education, is associated with better contemporary education and health outcomes in many former colonies.

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **Institutions:** The chapter provides a critical analysis of why "bad" institutions persist. It highlights how colonial administrative frameworks—such as tax systems favoring local landlords—were often retained by post-colonial governments, creating path dependency in inequality and low public goods investment.
*   **Geography:** The text connects geography to economic outcomes via "settler mortality" and agricultural suitability. Regions suited for large-scale plantations (sugar, mining) were more likely to develop extractive, exclusionary institutions than regions suited for smallholder settlement.
*   **Malthusian Epoch & Growth:** The chapter links the "Opening of the Atlantic" to the transition out of the Malthusian trap. It introduces the concept of "ghost acreage" (Pomeranz)—the idea that the New World provided the natural resources and land necessary to relieve European population pressures and facilitate industrialization.
*   **Culture:** The legacy of the slave trade is presented as a cultural barrier to growth. The erosion of trust and the promotion of ethnic divisions demonstrate that economic history is deeply intertwined with social and psychological consequences that outlive the colonial state itself.
