# Economic Effects of an Autocracy

> **Source:** Roland, Gerard (2013) "Economic Effects of an Autocracy," in _Development Economics_ , 4th edition, Pearson. (p 454-457) (2 page [PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1JIQ7hMyFdJ6zDL7XS5WXo9IrgdSr8HSP/view?usp=sharing))


## Overview
This paper investigates the socioeconomic and political consequences of the Progressive Era's municipal government reforms in the United States between 1900 and 1940. Specifically, the authors analyze the shift from traditional mayoral systems to the "reform-style" council-manager or city commissioner forms of government. By leveraging newly digitized municipal financial data, de-anonymized census records, and electoral data for 455 U.S. cities, the study tests the competing historical claims that these reforms either "uplifted" the urban poor through better administration or disenfranchised them to benefit business elites.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The authors employ a difference-in-differences design with trajectory balancing to address the non-random, endogenous timing of reform adoption. Their key findings include:

* **Minimal Socioeconomic Impact:** Contrary to the hypothesis that reforms significantly harmed the economic well-being of the working class or immigrant populations, the researchers find only modest increases in earnings inequality. The effects on employment rates, local government job access, literacy, and occupational segregation were largely statistically insignificant.
* **Reduction in Political Participation:** The study confirms that the adoption of reform-style government led to a significant decrease in voter turnout for both presidential and congressional elections. This supports the historical consensus that Progressive institutions (such as at-large elections, non-partisan ballots, and stricter registration) were designed to, and successfully did, reduce the political influence of lower-income and immigrant voters.
* **No Change in Public Expenditures:** Despite claims that reform would lead to more efficient or equitable government spending, the authors find no systemic change in the allocation of funds across public goods (e.g., schools, police, sanitation, or health) in reformed cities compared to those that did not reform.
* **Methodological Contribution:** The authors demonstrate that "naive" statistical approaches (ignoring the endogeneity of reform timing) significantly overestimate the negative economic impact of these reforms, highlighting the importance of using robust weighting techniques when studying historical institutional change.

## Connections to Course Themes
* **Institutions:** The paper serves as a micro-level examination of how political institutions shape economic outcomes. It evaluates the "bundled" nature of institutional change, where governance structure reforms (designed for efficiency) often carried implicit exclusionary biases that weakened democratic participation.
* **Political Economy:** The study addresses the classic trade-off between administrative "efficiency" (often championed by business elites) and broad-based political participation. It provides empirical depth to the debate regarding whether centralized "clean" government actually improves the welfare of the most vulnerable citizens or merely shifts power away from them.
* **Demographic Transition and Inequality:** By tracking specific demographic groups (immigrants, African Americans, and white business elites) across 40 years of urban growth, the paper provides evidence on how structural institutional shifts intersect with existing social hierarchies. It suggests that while the reforms successfully altered the political landscape, they were, perhaps surprisingly, insufficient to radically shift the fundamental economic disparities between these groups.
