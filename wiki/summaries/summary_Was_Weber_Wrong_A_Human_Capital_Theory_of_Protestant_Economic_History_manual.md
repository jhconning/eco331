# Was Weber Wrong? A Human Capital Theory of Protestant Economic History

> **Source:** Manual PDF

### Overview
This paper challenges Max Weber’s seminal "Protestant Ethic" thesis, which posited that Protestantism fostered economic growth through a specific cultural work ethic. Instead, authors Sascha O. Becker and Ludger Woessmann propose an alternative "human capital" theory. They argue that Protestant economic prosperity was an unintended consequence of Martin Luther’s advocacy for universal schooling—specifically aimed at ensuring all Christians could read the Bible. This religious mandate for literacy inadvertently provided the Protestant population with the human capital necessary to thrive in the emerging industrial economy.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Human Capital Hypothesis:** The primary mechanism is the differential in education. Because Protestant doctrine emphasized the "universal priesthood of all believers," reading the Bible in the vernacular became a religious necessity. This led to higher literacy rates in Protestant areas.
*   **Empirical Strategy:** The authors utilize county-level data from 19th-century Prussia. To address the potential endogeneity of religion, they use the "concentric dispersion" of the Reformation—measured by the distance of each county to Wittenberg (where Luther launched the Reformation)—as an instrumental variable.
*   **Findings:** The study demonstrates that Protestantism had a strong, causal, and positive effect on literacy. When controlling for this human capital accumulation, the independent effect of Protestantism on economic prosperity effectively disappears. 
*   **The Weber Thesis vs. Reality:** The authors conclude that while Weber was correct in observing the economic superiority of Protestant regions, he likely misidentified the underlying channel. Education, rather than an intangible "work ethic," explains the majority of the economic variance between Protestant and Catholic regions.

### Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History:** The paper contributes to the understanding of the Industrial Revolution by identifying why certain regions gained an "educational advantage" that accelerated their transition to modern growth.
*   **Institutions:** The authors note that the Reformation acted as an exogenous shock that influenced long-term institutional outcomes, specifically regarding the state's responsibility for maintaining public school systems.
*   **Demographic Transition/Human Capital:** The paper is a significant contribution to the literature on the origins of modern economic growth, aligning with theories (like those of Oded Galor) that emphasize the transition from physical to human capital as the fundamental driver of the divergence in global prosperity.
*   **Culture vs. Economics:** By treating "religion" as a proxy for a specific cultural shift toward literacy rather than a set of nebulous personality traits (thrift, industry), the authors bridge the gap between cultural economics and human capital theory, providing a testable mechanism that remains relevant in contemporary cross-sectional studies of Germany.
