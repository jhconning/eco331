# Introduction: Agrarian Fundamentalism and English Agricultural Development

> **Source:** Allen, R.C., 1992."[Introduction](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Thq3A1vozlpe64hhnulob6qwhAcAbX82/view?usp=sharing)" in _Enclosure and the Yeoman: the agricultural development of the South Midlands 1450-1850_. Oxford University Press.


## Overview
This text, the introduction to Robert C. Allen’s *Enclosure and the Yeoman* (1992), challenges the long-standing "Agrarian Fundamentalist" consensus regarding the English Agricultural Revolution. The dominant narrative traditionally held that the transition from small-scale peasant holdings (open fields) to large-scale, enclosed capitalist farms was the essential catalyst for England's agricultural productivity and its subsequent Industrial Revolution. Allen argues that this view is historically inaccurate and misattributes the drivers of economic growth.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
Allen proposes a revisionist history centered on the distinction between two distinct agricultural revolutions:

1.  **The Yeomen’s Revolution (17th Century):** Allen argues that the primary gains in agricultural productivity—specifically the doubling of crop yields—were achieved by small-scale, owner-occupying "yeoman" farmers in the open fields. These farmers were highly innovative and productive.
2.  **The Landlords’ Revolution (18th Century):** This consisted of enclosures and the amalgamation of small farms into large, tenant-run estates. Allen contends that this transition did not significantly boost yields; rather, it primarily served to redistribute income from farmers to landlords through higher rents. 
3.  **Refutation of "Labour Release":** While fundamentalists argue that enclosure "freed" labor for manufacturing, Allen provides evidence that, in the 18th century, these displaced workers were often not successfully re-absorbed by industry, leading to poverty rather than a shift toward a manufacturing proletariat.
4.  **Methodological Approach:** Unlike previous qualitative accounts, Allen utilizes statistical analysis of farm performance data, probate inventories, and Ricardian surplus calculations to prove that large farms do not possess inherent economies of scale over smaller, more intensively cultivated peasant farms.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & The Malthusian Epoch:** The text engages with the Malthusian framework by questioning whether population growth or technological improvement drives subsistence levels. Allen highlights that the "dismal" outcomes of the 19th century were not Malthusian inevitabilities but the results of institutional changes (enclosure) that increased inequality.
*   **Institutions:** The core of the critique is institutional. Allen analyzes how the legal system (Tudor courts) originally protected smallholders, and how subsequent changes to property rights (mortgages, enclosures) systematically dismantled this peasant class, favoring large landowners.
*   **Culture & Geography:** The author utilizes geographic diversity (heavy arable vs. light arable vs. pasture districts) to control for environmental variables, demonstrating that institutional "modernization" (enclosure) was often an unnecessary and inequitable substitute for genuine innovation.
*   **The Demographic Transition:** By analyzing the fate of the rural population post-enclosure, the text explores the transition from a traditional agrarian society to an industrial one, challenging the assumption that this shift was a linear, beneficial path toward economic development.
