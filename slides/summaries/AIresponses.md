# Summary of Student Submissions: Counterfactual History (The Statute of Laborers)

Based on the 54 submissions regarding the counterfactual economic history where the Statute of Laborers successfully suppressed wages and hardened serfdom after the Black Death, the students demonstrated a strong grasp of the institutional mechanisms that drove England's economic development. 

## Common Themes in Student Criticisms & Elaborations

The students' critiques consistently expanded upon the AI-generated responses by applying specific economic models and historical mechanisms discussed in class:

1.  **Incentives for Innovation (Induced Innovation):** By far the most common theme. Students astutely pointed out that cheap, coerced labor completely removes the incentive for landowners to adopt labor-saving technologies. They correctly identified that historically, England's unusually *high* wages forced entrepreneurs to substitute capital for labor (e.g., the steam engine, spinning jenny, new agricultural techniques).
2.  **Labor Mobility and Urbanization:** Students repeatedly highlighted that restricting peasants to manors would choke off the labor supply needed for urban growth. Without migration to cities like London, proto-industrial manufacturing (like textiles) and trade hubs could not develop.
3.  **Weak Domestic Consumer Demand:** Many students noted that keeping wages artificially low meant the vast majority of the population (the peasantry) would have no purchasing power. This would prevent the emergence of a "middling sort" or middle class to buy manufactured goods, stalling commercialization.
4.  **The "Second Serfdom" Comparison:** Several students successfully drew the comparative parallel to Eastern Europe (like Poland or Prussia), where a successful seigneurial reaction led to an extractive, agrarian, export-oriented economy rather than an industrialized one.
5.  **Malthusian Trap and Demographics:** Some students integrated the Malthusian model, noting that depressed incomes would lead to a low-level subsistence equilibrium, potentially halting the demographic transition and the "European Marriage Pattern."
6.  **The Cost of Enforcement and Rebellion:** A few critical thinkers pointed out a major flaw in the counterfactual: successfully enforcing such a rigid system would have required massive state capacity, resources, and violence, likely triggering constant uprisings (like the 1381 Peasant Revolt) that would drain the economy anyway.

## Highly Representative and Interesting Responses

Here are several standout student responses that creatively engaged with the prompt and added sophisticated analysis:

### **Samiul Alam**
*   **Highlight:** Strongly applied theoretical frameworks from class, explicitly mentioning **Acemoglu and Robinson (KR)** and extractive vs. inclusive institutions. 
*   **Key Insight:** Pointed out that high wages act as a "push" factor for capital substitution. Without this, the rationale for labor-saving devices disappears. He also noted that the collapse of serfdom was a necessary precondition for the inclusive institutions formalized later in the Glorious Revolution.

### **Leila Nouri**
*   **Highlight:** Explicitly tied the scenario to **Robert Allen’s theory** of the Industrial Revolution.
*   **Key Insight:** She correctly critiqued her AI's response for missing the specific mechanic of tenant farming. She noted that in actual history, lords who couldn't find cheap labor were forced to lease land to tenant farmers running farms for profit, creating a competitive agrarian system. She also hammered home that without high British wages driving demand, the Industrial Revolution would never have happened.

### **Samantha Chlafer**
*   **Highlight:** Excellent integration of the **Malthusian model** and demographic theories.
*   **Key Insight:** Discussed how the suppressed wages would alter the income elasticity of demand and prevent the European marriage pattern from taking hold. She argued that population growth would stagger differently, keeping the economy trapped in a cycle of high inequality and subsistence.

### **Noah**
*   **Highlight:** Brought up a highly practical critique regarding **state capacity and enforcement costs**.
*   **Key Insight:** He pointed out that maintaining such a coercive system (entrenched serfdom) in the face of widespread discontent would require massive financial and material resources to police. The constant threat of peasant uprisings and the strain on the state's finances would themselves cripple economic growth.

### **Leighkel Graham**
*   **Highlight:** Successfully applied the concept of the **"lock-in effect."**
*   **Key Insight:** She observed that the elites (landlords) would become locked into an extractive system because it benefitted them in the short term, thereby sacrificing the nation's long-term technological and economic dynamism. She also echoed the high costs and instability that would come from enforcing such draconian labor laws.

### **Chaouki Bousrih**
*   **Highlight:** Focused heavily on the **incentive structures for invention**.
*   **Key Insight:** Pointed out that land needed for new factories and industrial experimentation would be monopolized by lords using coerced agricultural labor, stifling the transition to manufacturing at its roots.
