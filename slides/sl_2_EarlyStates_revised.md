### Technology and Technological Progress

* **Technology:** the recipes for transforming resource inputs into useful outputs.
* **Technological progress:** getting more for less
    * more, better and more useful outputs from inputs
    * or similar outputs with less inputs
    * may be embodied in new methods, tools, forms of organization
* **Human Capital**: factors that influence worker productivity (training, problem-solving skill, health and longevity)

## A Puzzling Primate
### Yet Humans are Collectively Strong

* **One species of humans** thrives in arctic, desert, tropical, and high-altitude environments

## Cumulative Culture
### Building Knowledge Across Generations

* **Culture** = information stored outside the genome
  * recipes, tools, techniques, beliefs, social norms
  * transmitted from individual to individual, generation to generation

* **Cumulative** = each generation builds on what previous generations learned
  * Stone tools: simple rocks → hand axes → composite tools
  * Fire use: controlling fire → cooking → thermal technology

---

## Stone Tool Progression: Evidence of Cumulative Culture

![w:600 Image of Sl2 Stone Tool](attachments/sl2_stone_tool.png)

Over hundreds of thousands of years: simple rocks → hand axes → composite tools. Each generation built on knowledge of prior generations.

---
## Selective Cultural Learning
### How Knowledge Gets Transmitted

**Not all information is learned equally:**

* **Prestige-biased learning**: copy successful, high-status individuals
* **Content-biased learning**: copy behaviors that seem beneficial
* **Conformist bias**: tend toward behaviors others are doing

These mechanisms allow **rapid cultural evolution** and diffusion of innovations

---
## Culture-Gene Coevolution
### How Culture Shapes Human Biology

* Culture and genes evolve together in a **feedback loop**:
  * **Gene → Culture**: Genetic predispositions for learning, imitation, language
  * **Culture → Gene**: Cultural practices create selection pressures

* This creates a **coevolutionary process** where cultural practices shape which genes increase in frequency over generations

---

### Self-Domestication: Humans Evolving Through Culture

![w:700 Image of Sl2 Dog Human](attachments/sl2_dog_human.jpg)

* Cultural norms reward **cooperation**, punish **aggression**
* Selection toward prosocial traits
* Similar to how animals are domesticated through selective breeding
* But humans did it to themselves through **cultural practices**

## Lactase Persistence
### Culture and Gene Frequency

![bg right w:620 Image of Sl2 Lactase](attachments/sl2_lactase.png)

* **Culture**: Domestication of dairy animals (herding societies)
* **Genetic consequence**: Selection for ability to digest lactose into adulthood
* **Geographic pattern**: High gene frequency in pastoral populations
* Demonstrates how cultural practices create biological adaptation

## Megafauna Extinction in the Americas

* By time humans reach Americas, hunting methods are highly evolved. 
* Local large animal populations (that had not co-evolved with human predators) are rapidly wiped out.
* 30+ mammals hunted or otherwise led to extinction including: Woolly mammoths, giant armadillos, horses.
  
  ![bg right w:600 Image of Extinct Animals](attachments/extinct_animals.jpg)

---
## Why Africa Was Different

* In Africa, large animals **co-evolved** with human hunters over millions of years
* Animals learned to fear humans; developed escape behaviors
* Result: Africa retained its megafauna (elephants, rhinos, lions)

* In the Americas and Australia, animals had **no evolutionary experience** with human predators
* They were naive prey → rapid extinction

## James Scott's Grain Thesis

![bg right:35% w:400 Image of Scott Books](attachments/scott_books.png)

James C. Scott argues:

* Agriculture didn't require states
* **States required agriculture**
* Grain is visible, storable, divisible, taxable
* States form where grain makes populations "legible"
* Tubers, forest products: can be hidden, left in ground

<br>

* Longer term implications for state formatiorn and development

## The Grain Thesis

Why grain? From the state's perspective:

* **Visible**: grows in fields, can be surveyed
* **Storable**: can be accumulated in granaries
* **Divisible**: can be measured and taxed precisely
* **Predictable harvest**: timing known in advance

This is what Scott means by "legibility" — states need to see and count their subjects and their produce

## Why Adopt Agriculture?

Three possibilities (not mutually exclusive):

1. **Pushed**: Climate change, population pressure, declining wild resources forced the transition

2. **Pulled**: Elites emerged who benefited from controlling surplus and coerced others into farming

3. **Trapped**: Once adopted, populations grew; couldn't return to foraging even if worse off

The transition was **gradual, halting, and sometimes reversed**

## Three Views of State Formation

| **Predatory State** | **Public Goods State** | **Stationary Bandit** |
|---------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Scott, early Hobbes | Social contract theory | Olson |
| State extracts surplus through coercion | State solves coordination problems | Bandit settles down, invests in tax base |
| Walls keep people *in* | Walls keep enemies *out* | Both: protection + extraction |
| Slavery, bondage central | Defense, irrigation central | Ruler's interest aligned with subjects |

---

## Olson: Roving vs. Stationary Bandits

**Roving Bandit:**
* Raids, takes what he can, moves on
* No incentive to leave anything behind
* Maximizes short-term extraction

**Stationary Bandit:**
* Settles, claims territory, becomes "ruler"
* Has incentive to let subjects prosper (larger future tax base)
* Provides some public goods (protection, order)
* "An invisible hand guides him to produce the public good"

---

## Olson's Logic

The stationary bandit's incentive:

* If he takes 50% of output, he wants output to be as large as possible
* So he provides order, property rights, basic public goods
* His interests partially align with subjects' interests

**But:** Still extracts as much as he can without killing the goose
**And:** Subjects would prefer no bandit at all

This is a **theory of autocracy**, not democracy or voluntary cooperation

## The Open Frontier Problem

Scott's key insight:

* When land is abundant, people can **flee** oppressive rulers
* States lack the power to prevent "leakage"
* Early legal codes obsessed with runaways, escapees

* **Ester Boserup**: Only when population becomes dense and land scarce can elites control by owning land alone
* Until then: bondage, slavery, walls, forced resettlement

---

## Chayanov and Peasant Logic

A.V. Chayanov showed:

* Peasant families produce enough for sufficiency, then stop
* No incentive to produce surplus without compulsion
* "Beyond sufficiency... there was no reason to increase the drudgery of agricultural production"

**Implication:** Surplus doesn't exist naturally — the state creates it through extraction

---

## Mesopotamian Warfare

Seth Richardson's research on early Mesopotamian wars:

* Wars were **not primarily about territory**
* They were about **capturing populations**
* Petty wars to conquer smaller communities and bring them to the "grain core"
* The prize was captives — especially women and children
* "The state with the most people was generally richest"

---

## "No Slavery, No State"

Scott argues bondage was central to early states:

* War captives as labor force
* Debt bondage trapped locals
* Corvée labor for public works
* Women captives for reproduction

* As late as 1800, perhaps three-quarters of world's population in some form of bondage
* Athens: slaves were two-thirds of population

**Douglas North** sports analogy:
- Institutions: Rules of the Game. Organizations: Teams. Individuals: players.

Institutions: e.g. State Laws, system of property rights.

Organizations form to coordinate and advance individual interests. 
Compete with other organizations.

Constrained by institutions but also attempt to change institutions.

## Summary: Why Did States Emerge?

No single answer — likely all factors played a role:

1. **Geography**: Grain-favorable environments made taxation possible
2. **Climate**: Holocene stability enabled sedentary agriculture  
3. **Coordination needs**: Irrigation, defense required organization
4. **Coercion capacity**: Surplus extraction required bondage, control
5. **Population dynamics**: Density enabled hierarchy; open frontiers limited it

The debate continues: Were early states net benefits or net costs to most people?
