## Discussion Questions (12)

### Culture and Intelligence

1. **Henrich argues that humans are not born "smarter" than other animals, yet we dominate every environment on Earth. What is the difference between individual intelligence and collective/cultural intelligence? Can you think of modern examples where a group accomplishes something no single individual could?**

2. **If humans and chimpanzees were placed on a remote island with no tools or prior knowledge, humans would likely struggle more than chimpanzees—yet humans have spread to every continent. What explains this paradox? What role does documentation and knowledge transmission play?**

3. **Prestige-biased learning (copying successful, high-status people) was crucial in the ancestral environment. How does prestige-biased learning work in the modern age of social media? Are we copying the right people? How has technology changed who we see as "successful"?**

### Self-Domestication and Modern Society

4. **Henrich argues humans self-domesticated through cultural selection: societies punished aggression and rewarded cooperation, gradually selecting for more prosocial traits. If ostracism and threat of death enforced these norms in the past, what enforces cooperative behavior today? Has self-domestication continued, reversed, or stalled in modern wealthy societies?**

5. **Gabriel Marinescu asks: if life expectancy was 30-40 years and norm violations could mean death, self-domestication made sense. But since the 19th century, life expectancy has doubled and norm violations rarely mean death. Does self-domestication still operate the same way? What have we gained and lost?**

### The Malthusian Paradox

6. **Galor shows that for 300,000 years, technological progress increased population but not living standards—the Malthusian Epoch. Agricultural technology should have made people richer, yet it made many poorer with worse health. Why did societies adopt agriculture if it reduced living standards? What was worth the trade-off?**

7. **Hao Chen observes that modern technology (AI, automation) increases productivity but displaces workers. Is this a modern echo of the Malthusian pattern—where technology drives population/economic changes without improving average living standards? What would Galor predict about AI and inequality?**

8. **Ziyang Shi asks: despite massive technological innovation since the Industrial Revolution, why do some countries remain poor while others become rich? Galor highlights cultural traits like "long-term orientation" and "social capital." Are these cultural traits fixed, or can they change? What evidence would show they can/cannot change?**

### Geography, Institutions, and Culture

9. **Galor argues that geographic and institutional factors created different cultural traits across regions (e.g., long-term orientation in regions with crops requiring seasonal planning). If culture flows from geography and institutions, how much can societies change their culture through policy? Can you become "long-term oriented" through education?**

10. **Dominik Gozdz raises an important point: throughout history, wars have eliminated the most capable young people—those best at cultural learning. Wouldn't this disrupt the selection for "good cultural learners" that Henrich emphasizes? How might warfare have reshaped cultural evolution?**

### Knowledge and Human Spread

11. **Leighkel Graham asks: if documentation and written knowledge hadn't existed throughout history, where would human civilization be? If knowledge exists only in people's minds, and wars kill people, how fragile is cumulative culture? What happens when cultural transmission breaks down (Bhawana's question)?**

12. **Multiple students ask about inequality: Why has global economic development been so uneven? Is it colonial history (Ziyang), geography (Matt), institutions (Sheridan/Dominik), or cultural traits (Galor)? How do we disentangle these factors? Which is most changeable?**
