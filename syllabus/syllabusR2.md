# Eco 331: Economic History

## Course Description

Billions today enjoy much higher living standards and longer, healthier lives than their ancestors. Yet the institutional, technological, and economic transformations that made this growth possible -- the rise of capitalism, nation-states, industrialization, and global trade – are all products of only the last few centuries. New forms of property and corporate organization and technological innovation reshaped the environment, culture, and society, generating new wealth and opportunities, but often along a path that has included expropriation, displacement, and environmental damage. Understanding the timing, causes, and consequences of economic transitions and their uneven global impact is crucial for comprehending the modern world and its future possibilities. We will examine the role of geography, politics and institutions, culture, demography, colonialism and exploitation, and other factors shaped this and earlier “transitions,” including the Neolithic revolution, the rise and fall of coerced labor, demographic transitions, escape from the Malthusian trap, and more. While these are ‘big questions’ that invite causal explanations and economic theorizing, we will strive to ground our explorations in case studies and empirical historical research from around the world, from the ancient Levant and China, to Europe, and the New World.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the semester, students taking this Economic History course are expected to be able to:

1. **Analyze and evaluate major debates and theories in economic history**, including the causes and consequences of key transitions such as the shift to agriculture, the rise and fall of feudalism, the emergence of capitalism, and the Industrial Revolutions.
2. **Apply economic theory and quantitative methods to historical case studies**, using empirical evidence to assess competing hypotheses and draw informed conclusions about the past.
3. **Gain a critical perspective on the rise and spread of global capitalism**, understanding its transformative impact on wealth creation and technological progress, as well as its disruptive consequences, including colonialism, property dispossession, displacement, forced migration, and environmental damage.
4. **Explain how and why forms of migrancy, diaspora, and/or borders have transformed over time**, impacting people and resources in a global context
5. **Engage in respectful dialogue and demonstrate cross-cultural understanding** when discussing diverse historical experiences and perspectives.

### Participation and Discussion Questions

Grades for this activity will be based on your contributions thoughout the semester to the following sub-activities:

1. **Posted questions and comments:** Due the night before each class, you will be asked to contribute questions comments based on the readings for the upcoming class: (1) One or more questions you have about the reading topic.  (2) One or more critical observations you have about the reading topic (this could include a short paragraph expanding on a piece of history related to the reading or topic). You will receive points each week based on whether you completed this on time. 
2. **Online threaded discussions:** We will hold online threaded discussions from time to time via the Canvas website. These will be graded on both participation (did you post at all) and based on the constructive engagement and quality of your contributions.
3. **Class Participation:** Contributions to class discussion, office hour visits, and other measures of constructive engagement.

I will post a few graded online problem sets, primarily as an aid to help you study for the midterm and final.

I recommend regular attendance and active participation in class. If you should miss a lecture, you are responsible to keep fully informed about class material discussed and all course changes, should they occur. I will ask that all phones and electronic devices be put away during class (unless they are being used exclusively for note-taking) in order to avoid distractions and disturbing others. 

Submitted written work must be original, with clear references indicating reference works consulted and any quoted text in quotation marks with proper attribution.  Large language models such as ChatGPT and other AI assistants can be useful complements to learning about historical topics, if used with caution (they sometimes 'hallucinate' and provide made up information).  I _encourage_ you to explore these new tools to guide your explorations, but I do ask that you mix in your own efforts and ideas and indicate when and how you've used these tools.
