# 1: The Commercialization model and its legacy

> **Source:** Wood, E.M., 2002. "1: [The Commercialization model and its legacy,](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1TdrFR3lsrdkqA9vhX0V7eqFun4PrpSj4/view?usp=share_link)" (excerpts) in _The Origin of Capitalism: A longer View_. Verso.


## Overview
The Commercialization Model posits that the transition from a stagnant, Malthusian economy to modern economic growth was fundamentally driven by the expansion of trade, market integration, and the subsequent specialization of labor. This framework suggests that "Smithian growth"—characterized by productivity gains through the division of labor—created the necessary surplus to support urban centers and capital accumulation, eventually serving as the catalyst for the broader socio-economic shifts observed in pre-industrial and early industrial societies. The text explores how historical commercial expansion, particularly in Europe, acted as the primary vehicle for breaking the constraints of subsistence-level production.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Division of Labor:** Drawing on Adam Smith’s core insight, the text argues that as markets expand, the division of labor increases. This specialization leads to incremental improvements in tool efficiency and process innovation, moving economies beyond the limits of individual self-sufficiency.
*   **Transaction Costs and Institutions:** The model highlights that commercialization is not merely a technical phenomenon but an institutional one. Lowering transaction costs through better enforcement of contracts, standardized weights and measures, and protected property rights allowed for the scaling of trade routes.
*   **Urbanization and Human Capital:** Commercial hubs served as nodes for both material and intellectual exchange. The concentration of merchants and skilled artisans fostered an environment where human capital could be accumulated and transmitted more effectively than in agrarian, isolated settings.
*   **Surplus Extraction:** The expansion of markets allowed for the systematic extraction of rural surpluses, which fueled urban demand for manufactured goods, creating a feedback loop between the agricultural and non-agricultural sectors.

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** The text contrasts the Commercialization Model with Malthusian constraints. While the Malthusian model suggests that population growth inevitably absorbs productivity gains, the commercialization perspective argues that trade-induced specialization enabled a "bottleneck breakthrough," where capital accumulation began to outpace population growth.
*   **Institutions:** The legacy of commercialization is deeply linked to the development of legal and financial institutions. The model emphasizes that the "great divergence" in economic growth was not just a matter of geography or resources, but of the institutional frameworks—such as joint-stock companies and banking systems—that facilitated commercial risk-taking.
*   **Geography:** The text notes that geography played a non-deterministic but facilitative role. Coastal regions and riverine networks acted as natural conduits for the commercial expansion described, suggesting that geography influenced the timing, if not the ultimate necessity, of market integration.
*   **Culture:** The commercialization process encouraged the spread of a "commercial ethos"—a shift toward rationalized accounting, time-discipline, and a valuation of innovation. This cultural transformation provided the behavioral foundation required for the sustained shift into the modern growth regime.
