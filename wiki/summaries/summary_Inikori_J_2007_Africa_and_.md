# Africa and the globalization process: western Africa, 1450–1850

> **Source:** Inikori, J (2007) “Africa and the globalization process: Western Africa, 1450–1850” _Journal of Global History_ , 2, pp. 63-86. (read pages 79-84, skim the rest) [PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1FVPGnZxyrXcSz_TzStInGiW81YSRbD6l/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
In this article, Joseph E. Inikori argues that the modern global economy is the result of a specific historical process originating in the Atlantic basin during the 16th century. He defines globalization not merely as the existence of trade linkages, but as the creation of an integrated economic system characterized by a global division of labor and the transition of populations from subsistence to market-based production. Inikori situates Africa at the center of this process, positing that coerced African labor was a primary driver of the Atlantic economy, while simultaneously arguing that the nature of the slave trade fundamentally retarded economic development and market integration within western Africa.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Atlantic Economy as the Nucleus:** The modern global economy originated from the integration of the Atlantic basin (Europe, Africa, and the Americas) between 1450 and 1850. This integration was driven by the expansion of plantation agriculture and mining in the Americas.
*   **The Role of Coerced Labor:** Inikori utilizes the "Wakefield thesis" to argue that because land was abundant in the Americas, a free labor market could not emerge naturally; therefore, coerced (enslaved) African labor was essential to facilitate the "gang organization" of large-scale staple production (sugar, cotton, etc.).
*   **The "Industrious" vs. Industrial Revolution:** The massive increase in plantation commodities lowered prices in Europe, which incentivized European populations to shift from subsistence to market-based consumption (the "industrious revolution"). This rise in demand for manufactured goods ultimately fueled England’s Industrial Revolution.
*   **Economic Retardation in West Africa:** While the Atlantic economy flourished, West Africa was forced into an extractive relationship. The slave trade necessitated a move away from internal regional specialization and inter-African commodity trade. The rise of enclave economies centered on the procurement of captives (to be exchanged for firearms and consumer goods) hindered the growth of domestic markets and trapped the region in subsistence production.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & Globalization:** Inikori contests contemporary definitions of globalization that equate it with late 20th-century interdependence. He argues for a historical, process-oriented view that emphasizes the development of integrated production sites rather than just long-distance trade.
*   **Institutions & Coercion:** The paper challenges the narrative that capitalist development in Europe was purely internal. It argues that European capitalism was facilitated by the external, forced-labor-based economies of the Atlantic.
*   **Geography & Factor Endowments:** The argument draws heavily on the relationship between land abundance and labor institutions. Where land was abundant and easily accessible, slavery was institutionalized to bypass the lack of a wage-labor pool.
*   **The Malthusian Epoch/Subsistence:** A central theme is the "commercializing process." Inikori identifies the shift from subsistence production (pre-1500) to market production as the prerequisite for modern economic growth, highlighting how certain regions (the Americas and Europe) underwent this transition through Atlantic commerce, while West Africa was forcibly prevented from doing so by the structural impact of the slave trade.
