# The Consequences of Radical Reform: The French Revolution

> **Source:** Acemoglu, Daron, Davide Cantoni, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson. 2011. "The Consequences of Radical Reform: The French Revolution." _American Economic Review_  101(7): 3286–3307. [Paper](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.7.3286)


## Overview
This paper, authored by Daron Acemoglu, Davide Cantoni, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson, investigates the long-run economic impact of the radical institutional reforms imposed by the French Revolution and Napoleon on various German polities. By exploiting the variation in "French treatment"—the differing lengths of French occupation and administration—the authors examine whether externally imposed, radical institutional changes (such as the Civil Code, the abolition of guilds, and the removal of feudalism) hinder or foster subsequent economic growth. Contrary to theories suggesting that "designed" institutions are ineffective, the authors find that areas subjected to these reforms experienced more rapid urbanization and industrialization in the late 19th century.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The "Exogenous" Treatment:** The authors argue that French occupation was driven primarily by defensive military motives and a desire to create buffer states, rather than a selection of regions based on inherent growth potential. This allows the occupation to be treated as an exogenous shock.
*   **Institutional Barriers of the *Ancien Régime*:** The paper identifies the primary impediments to growth in 18th-century Europe as the landed nobility, restrictive urban guilds, and unfree labor relations (serfdom/feudalism). These institutions blocked entry, innovation, and trade.
*   **The Reform Package:** French rule introduced the Civil Code (equality before the law), abolished feudal land relations, and dismantled guilds. This leveled the playing field and facilitated a more efficient, market-oriented economy.
*   **Mechanism of Growth:** The institutional reforms did not necessarily trigger an immediate economic explosion. Instead, by removing the institutional bottlenecks of the *ancien régime*, the reforms created an environment conducive to the adoption of new industrial technologies during the 19th-century Industrial Revolution.
*   **Instrumental-Variables Strategy:** Using French occupation as an instrument for institutional reform, the authors demonstrate a robust, positive relationship between the reforms and subsequent urbanization rates, controlling for geography, initial development, and cultural factors.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Institutions:** This paper provides a seminal case study for the "Institutional Hypothesis," arguing that the quality of economic institutions—defined here by the protection of property rights, the rule of law, and the absence of restrictive monopolies—is a primary driver of long-run prosperity.
*   **The Industrial Revolution:** The research highlights that the Industrial Revolution was not merely a matter of technological innovation; it required an institutional framework that allowed new methods (like mechanized textile production) to replace obsolete practices held by guilds and oligarchies.
*   **Demographic Transition & Urbanization:** The authors use urbanization (specifically the share of the population in cities > 5,000 inhabitants) as a reliable proxy for income per capita and industrial productivity in the pre-statistical era. This connects directly to the course study of how shifting labor from agriculture to industry leads to urbanization.
*   **Path Dependence vs. Reform:** While the paper challenges the "evolutionary" view of institutions (e.g., Hayek), it also acknowledges that reforms can be reversed (e.g., in areas where old elites returned). It suggests that the *radicalism* of the French reforms—making a return to the status quo ante significantly more difficult—was a key factor in their durability and ultimate success.
