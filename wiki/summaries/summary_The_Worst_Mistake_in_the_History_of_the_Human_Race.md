# The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race

> **Source:** Diamond, D.W., 1999. "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race". Discover Magazine. ([PDF](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~rogaway/classes/188/materials/Diamond-TheWorstMistakeInTheHistoryOfTheHumanRace.pdf))


## Overview
In this provocative 1987 essay, Jared Diamond challenges the "progressivist" view of human history—the widely held belief that the transition from foraging (hunting and gathering) to agriculture represents an unmitigated advance in human welfare. By synthesizing evidence from paleopathology and anthropology, Diamond argues that the Neolithic Revolution was not a conscious choice for a "better life," but rather a transition that led to declining health, shorter life spans, social stratification, and the emergence of institutionalized inequality. He posits that humanity was "trapped" by the population growth that agriculture enabled, forcing a reliance on a system that sacrificed quality of life for sheer quantity of people.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Health Trade-off:** Contrary to the belief that farming provided a more stable food supply, archaeological evidence (e.g., Dickson Mounds) shows that early farmers suffered from increased malnutrition (enamel defects), anemia, and infectious diseases. This was caused by a narrowed, starchy diet and the proximity to sedentary populations and domesticated animals.
*   **The "Trap" of Population Density:** Agriculture supported higher population densities than hunting/gathering. Once populations increased, they could not easily return to a foraging lifestyle. Groups that adopted agriculture were able to "outbreed" and subsequently displace or kill off hunter-gatherer bands, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of expansion.
*   **The Origins of Inequality:** The shift from nomadic foraging to sedentary farming created the conditions for social hierarchy. Because farmers could store food surpluses, it became possible for an elite class to extract resources from a productive peasantry. This established the foundations for "despotism" and systematic class and gender disparities.
*   **Quality vs. Quantity:** Diamond argues that the Neolithic Revolution represented a shift where humanity chose (or was forced into) a path of maximizing the number of humans supported by the land, regardless of the individual cost in health, leisure, and freedom.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** This text serves as a foundation for understanding the Malthusian trap. Agriculture allowed for population growth, but this growth was often checked by the "Malthusian positive checks" of disease and famine—the very results Diamond observes in early agricultural sites.
*   **Economic History:** Diamond challenges the economic assumption that "progress" is synonymous with technological adoption. He highlights that the "efficiency" of agriculture is only measured by caloric yield per acre, not by labor input or nutritional health.
*   **Institutions & Social Structure:** The text illustrates the birth of institutionalized inequality. In foraging societies, the lack of storable surplus made social stratification nearly impossible; in agricultural societies, the control of food surplus became the primary mechanism for establishing political authority and class division.
*   **The Demographic Transition:** The author touches upon the shift in fertility patterns. Hunter-gatherers managed population growth through nomadic constraints and reproductive practices (infanticide/birth spacing), whereas agricultural societies incentivized higher birth rates, fundamentally altering human demographic trajectories.
