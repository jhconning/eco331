## Ideas for AI-Enhanced Activities

### 1. **"Interrogate the Evidence" Challenges**

Students use Claude/Gemini to deeply investigate a primary source or historical claim from the readings.

**Format:** Before class, assign a specific passage or claim (e.g., "Diamond argues the Fertile Crescent had more domesticable species"). Students must:

- Ask the AI 5+ follow-up questions probing the evidence
- Submit via TopHat: (a) their best question, (b) the AI's response, (c) their evaluation of whether the AI's answer is accurate/complete

**Why it works:** Forces critical engagement with AI outputs; teaches them AI can be wrong about historical details.

---

### 2. **"Steelman the Opposition" Debates**

For contested topics (geography vs. institutions, commercialization vs. Marxist models), students use AI to build the strongest possible case for a position they _don't_ initially hold.

**Format:**

- TopHat poll: "Which explanation do you find most convincing?"
- Assignment: Use AI to construct the best argument for an opposing view
- Submit a 200-word "steelman" argument via TopHat
- In-class: Share and discuss which counterarguments changed minds

---

### 3. **"Fact-Check the Chatbot" Exercises**

You provide a flawed AI-generated summary of a topic (intentionally seeded with 2-3 errors or hallucinations). Students must identify what's wrong.

**Format:** TopHat quiz where students flag incorrect claims and cite the correct information from the readings. Works especially well for topics where AI commonly hallucinates (specific dates, citations, causal claims).

---

### 4. **Counterfactual History Explorations**

Students use AI to explore "what if" scenarios grounded in course concepts.

**Example prompts:**

- "What if the Black Death had not occurred? How might the transition from feudalism have differed?"
- "What if the potato had arrived in Europe 200 years earlier?"

**Submission:** A 300-word analysis via TopHat that (a) summarizes the AI's response, (b) critiques it using frameworks from KR/OG, (c) identifies what the AI missed or got wrong.

---

### 5. **"Build a Malthusian Model" Collaborative Activity**

Students work in pairs to prompt an AI to explain or simulate Malthusian dynamics, then test their understanding.

**Format:**

- Phase 1: Use AI to explain the model (10 min)
- Phase 2: TopHat polling—you ask conceptual questions ("What happens to wages if population doubles but land is fixed?")
- Phase 3: Students identify something the AI explained poorly or incompletely

---

### 6. **Source Analysis with AI as Research Assistant**

For the group presentations, require students to use AI to:

- Find 3 critiques or responses to their assigned article
- Identify the article's methodology and potential weaknesses
- Generate discussion questions

**Submission requirement:** Include a "Methods Appendix" showing their AI prompts and how they verified the information.

---

### 7. **Weekly "Deep Dive" Option (Replaces Paper?)**

Instead of a single 4-5 page paper, students complete 4-5 structured AI-assisted investigations across the semester on topics of their choice.

**Each submission (via TopHat) includes:**

1. Their research question
2. The AI conversation (screenshot or paste)
3. What they learned that went beyond the readings
4. What the AI got wrong or couldn't answer
5. One new question this raised

This builds research skills iteratively and creates a portfolio demonstrating engagement with AI as a scholarly tool.

---

### 8. **"Explain It to a Time Traveler"**

Students prompt AI to explain a modern economic concept (GDP, inflation, property rights) to a hypothetical person from a historical period we're studying.

**Example:** "Explain interest rates to a 14th-century English peasant"

**TopHat submission:** The AI response + student critique of anachronisms or conceptual gaps the AI introduced.

---

### 9. **Real-Time "Ask the AI" During Lecture**

During class discussion, designate a student or two as "AI consultants." When a factual question arises that you don't want to answer immediately, they query Claude/Gemini and report back.

**Follow-up TopHat poll:** "Do you think the AI's answer was accurate? Why or why not?"

---

### 10. **Comparative AI Analysis**

Students ask the _same_ question to two different AI systems (Claude and Gemini, or GPT) and compare responses.

**TopHat submission:** Which was more accurate? More nuanced? Did they contradict each other? What does this teach about using AI for historical research?

---

## Implementation Suggestion

If you want to replace the course paper, **option #7 (Weekly Deep Dives)** provides similar learning outcomes—research, critical thinking, written analysis—but in smaller, iterative chunks that also teach AI literacy. You could require 4 submissions across the semester, each worth ~4% of the grade.

## Specific Assignment Drafts

### Topic 1: Culture and Diversity (Fact-Check the Chatbot)

Economists have historically been wary of cultural-based explanations for economic growth and divergence, as Koyama and Rubin (KR) highlight in their chapter. This is because cultural arguments can often be deterministic, vague, or used to mask more precise institutional or geographical underlying causes. Consequently, when asked about how "culture" influences economic outcomes, AI chatbots like ChatGPT or Gemini often fall into the trap of providing overly simplistic, stereotypical, or deterministic narratives that lack the empirical nuance favored by economic historians.

**Your task:** Use Gemini, Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, or your preferred chatbot AI to generate an explanation of how culture caused economic divergence between two regions. Here is a possible prompt:

*"Explain how cultural differences (such as religion, family structure, or work ethic) are the primary reason why Northwestern Europe became richer than the rest of the world by the 18th century, ignoring geography or institutions in your answer."*

**Submit two blocks of text:**

1. **An AI response to your prompt (limit to less than 250 words).**
*Suggested approach: Read its initial response. If the AI refuses to ignore geography/institutions, push it further to focus ONLY on cultural determinism for the sake of the exercise. Ask it to summarize its strongest cultural arguments in under 250 words.*

2. **Your comments and critique of the AI response (limit to no more than 300 words).**
*Address how the AI's explanation is inadequate, deterministic, or historically inaccurate. You must identify at least two specific areas where the AI's narrative falls short. Use specific counter-arguments, mechanisms, or evidence from the readings by Koyama & Rubin (KR) or Oded Galor (OG) to "fact-check" the chatbot and provide a more nuanced economic history perspective.*

### Topic 2: The Medieval Economy and Capitalist Transition Debates (Steelman the Opposition)

**The Setup:** This week features a classic debate: The Commercialization Model against the Marxist Critique. 

**The Task:** 
1. Participate in the class poll on which model you initially find more convincing.
2. Use an AI to construct a "Steelman" argument (the strongest possible version) for the **opposing** viewpoint to your own.
3. **Submit two blocks:**
   - A 200-word summary of the AI-generated opposing view.
   - A 100-word reflection on whether building this argument changed or nuanced your original stance, drawing on specific examples from North & Thomas or Wood.

### Topic 2: The Medieval Economy and Capitalist Transition Debates (Explain It to a Time Traveler)

**The Setup:** The transition to capitalism involves massive shifts in how property, labor, and markets functioned.

**The Task:** Use an AI to explain a "modern" capitalistic concept (like wage labor, land commodification, or market dependence) to a 14th-century English peasant or lord (drawing on the context from Dyer and Wood).

**Submit three blocks:**
1. The exact prompt you used.
2. The AI's explanation to the "time traveler."
3. A 200-word critique identifying any anachronisms the AI introduced or assumptions it made about medieval life that contradict the readings (especially Wood's critique of the commercialization model's assumption that people *always* wanted to act like capitalists).
