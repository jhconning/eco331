# An Age of Transition? Economy and Society in England in the Later Middle Ages

> **Source:** Manual PDF

### Overview
Christopher Dyer’s work examines the "long fifteenth century" (1350–1520) as a pivotal transitional phase between the medieval and modern eras in England. Moving beyond the traditional binary of "feudalism vs. capitalism," Dyer argues that the socio-economic transformations of this period were rooted in earlier developments (dating back to the 13th century) and were driven as much by the agency of the peasantry as by the landed aristocracy. The text challenges the notion of a sharp break around 1500, instead proposing a gradual process of commercialization, the decline of serfdom, and the emergence of new social classes like yeomen and farmers.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Social Basis of Change:** Dyer argues that historical change is driven by the internal dynamics of social structures. While aristocrats (nobles, gentry, and the church) held initial power, their retreat from direct land management created a vacuum filled by upwardly mobile peasants. 
*   **Demographic Agency:** The mid-14th-century population collapse (post-Black Death) fundamentally altered the economic landscape. The resulting scarcity of labor and surplus of land empowered the working classes, leading to higher wages, increased individual incomes, and the erosion of serfdom.
*   **The "Middle" Sort:** The author identifies the emergence of a class of producers—farmers, clothiers, and middlemen—who operated outside the rigid traditional hierarchies of "lords and peasants." These actors were central to the development of commercial agriculture and proto-industrialization.
*   **Informal Administration:** A key methodological point is that as the traditional manorial system declined, record-keeping became more decentralized and informal. The rise of a market-oriented society paradoxically left fewer administrative footprints for historians to track compared to the highly documented feudal era.
*   **Continuity over Rupture:** Dyer posits that "modern" features, such as market involvement, private property concerns, and wage labor, were steadily building throughout the 14th and 15th centuries rather than appearing suddenly in the 16th century.

### Connections to Course Themes
*   **Malthusian Epoch & Demographic Transition:** The text highlights the relationship between population fluctuations and economic outcomes. The period 1350–1520 serves as a case study where population decline led to higher individual living standards, illustrating how demographic pressures can force structural institutional change.
*   **Institutions & Property Rights:** Dyer notes that while England lacked the centralized state controls seen on the Continent, its political regime provided a relatively secure environment for property rights and trade. This institutional stability was a precursor to England's later economic expansion.
*   **Economic History (Feudalism to Capitalism):** The text serves as a critique of simplistic transition theories. It engages directly with the "Brenner Debate," emphasizing that the transition was not merely a byproduct of market forces, but the result of power struggles between social classes regarding land tenure and labor obligations.
*   **Culture:** The author notes the rise of a "consumer society" and a culture of "improvement," where individuals began to prioritize profit-seeking and defined property boundaries, suggesting that cultural shifts toward individualism were intertwined with the economic transition.
