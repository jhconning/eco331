# Population Control: Bondage and War

> **Source:** Scott, J.C., 2017. "Population Control: Bondage and War," Chapter 5 in _Against The Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States_. Yale University Press. (p 150-157, 171-179) ([PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Dkhj_i8nTCgnpL29LKuuQJzlf-RtHXOB/view?usp=share_link))


## Overview
This text examines the structural relationship between early state formation and various forms of human bondage. By analyzing historical examples ranging from the city-states of Mesopotamia to the empires of Athens, Sparta, and Rome, the author argues that slavery was not merely a peripheral economic activity but a fundamental pillar of state building. The text challenges modern conceptions of "freedom" by contextualizing slavery as a ubiquitous feature of antiquity, often linked directly to military conquest and the extraction of labor from captive or indigenous populations.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The "No Slavery, No State" Hypothesis:** The author posits a strong link between the emergence of organized states and the institutionalization of forced labor. The state requires a mechanism to extract surplus, and slavery serves as the primary engine for this production.
*   **War as a Labor Procurement System:** Military campaigns were historically accompanied by slave merchants. The author highlights that war was a primary driver for the "marketization" of humans; for instance, the Gallic Wars significantly inflated the slave population in Rome.
*   **Taxonomy of Bondage:**
    *   **Chattel Slavery (Athens):** Characterized by war captives who were commodified (even valued against draft animals like mules).
    *   **Indigenous Enslavement (Sparta):** The "Helot" model, where a militarized elite conquered a sedentary population in situ to extract agricultural surplus.
*   **The "Utopian" Exception:** Through an analysis of Sumerian hymns, the text argues that the necessity of rituals to "suspend" bondage (e.g., equality between slave and master) confirms that the default state of these societies was one of deep structural inequality and permanent bondage.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & Institutions:** The text illustrates how institutions of bondage were essential for the development of "grain complexes" and early economic systems. It suggests that labor scarcity in early states was solved through coercion rather than market-based wage systems.
*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** While the text focuses on political structure, it intersects with Malthusian constraints by highlighting how states managed population growth and labor distribution. The appropriation of sedentary grain-producers mirrors the struggle for resources typical of the Malthusian epoch.
*   **Culture and Geography:** The text touches on the geography of state formation, noting how states formed in favorable agricultural areas (like the Malay Peninsula or the Fertile Crescent) were often surrounded by "hill peoples" or non-state actors who were then targeted for slave raids.
*   **State Formation:** It challenges the student to view the "state" not as a guarantor of liberty, but as a predator that relies on the "tongue of the whip" to maintain its internal organization and external military power.
