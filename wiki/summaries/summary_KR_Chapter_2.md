# Koyama & Rubin Chapter 2: Did Some Societies Win the Geographic Lottery?

> **Source:** KR 2 "Did Some Societies Win the Geography Lottery" (19-36)


## Overview
Chapter 2 explores the role of geography in determining global economic development. While geography provides an exogenous baseline—influencing trade costs, disease environments, and resource availability—it is insufficient as a standalone explanation for the onset of modern economic growth. The chapter balances the "geography hypothesis" against historical evidence, noting that while geographic factors influence population density and city location, they often fail to explain the timing of the Industrial Revolution or significant "reversals of fortune" in wealth across history.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms

*   **Trade and Geography:** Access to water-based transportation significantly reduces trade costs. Landlocked nations often suffer from geographic disadvantages that limit trade and economic integration.
*   **The Disease Environment:** Geography dictates disease ecology, which impacts economic development. Malaria (limiting productivity and state formation) and the tsetse fly (preventing large-scale livestock rearing and, consequently, state centralization) serve as major hurdles in specific geographic zones.
*   **Diamond’s Continental Axis Theory:** Jared Diamond argues that the horizontal orientation of Eurasia allowed for faster diffusion of agricultural technology and crops compared to the Americas, where climatic variation across a vertical axis hindered trade and technical spread.
*   **Smithian Growth:** This refers to growth driven by trade and the division of labor. Transportation infrastructure (roads, canals, railroads) lowers trade costs, allowing for greater market access and specialization. However, this growth faces diminishing returns unless accompanied by technological innovation.
*   **The Role of Natural Resources (Coal):** The proximity of coal to industrial centers in Britain is often cited as a catalyst for the Industrial Revolution. While some argue coal was essential for moving from an organic to a mineral-based economy, others (like Mokyr) suggest that coal’s importance was contingent on the invention of technologies like the steam engine.
*   **Limits of the Geographic Explanation:** Geographic features are largely fixed, making them poor predictors of the *timing* of economic growth. Historical events like the "urban reset" in Britain demonstrate that human agency and changing trade needs can override the initial geographic conditions established by previous powers (like the Romans).

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **Malthusian Epoch:** Before 1800, better-endowed land did not necessarily lead to higher per capita wealth; instead, it supported higher population densities. This is a classic hallmark of the Malthusian trap, where increased productivity is absorbed by population growth rather than rising standards of living.
*   **Geography vs. Institutions:** The text highlights that while geography is exogenous and easy to measure, it cannot account for "reversals of fortune" (e.g., why some colonized areas became rich while others remained poor). This suggests that institutions and culture are necessary complements to geography in explaining long-run growth.
*   **Economic History:** The chapter employs historical case studies—such as the Roman road network, the collapse of British towns in the 5th century, and the US expansion via railroads—to illustrate how infrastructure acts as a tool to mitigate geographical constraints.
*   **Demographic Transition & Industrialization:** The transition to a mineral-based economy (coal) marks a break from the organic limits of the Malthusian era, paving the way for the sustained modern economic growth observed during the 19th-century Industrial Revolution.
