## Neolithic Revolution: Summary 


**Diamond (1999) - "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race":**
Diamond argues that the agricultural revolution, rather than being humanity's great leap forward, was a catastrophe from which we've never recovered. Archaeological evidence from skeletal remains shows that early farmers experienced:
- Dramatic height decreases (5'9" to 5'3" for men in Greece/Turkey)
- Increased malnutrition, anemia, and infectious disease
- Shorter life expectancy (26 years for hunter-gatherers vs 19 for early farmers)
- Greater inequality (both class and gender divisions)

Hunter-gatherers had more varied diets, better health, and more leisure time. Agriculture spread not because it improved lives, but because it supported higher population densities. Farming societies could field larger armies and displace hunter-gatherers through sheer numbers—"a hundred malnourished farmers can still outfight one healthy hunter."

**Galor & Scott readings** (referenced by students):
- Early states needed to control and concentrate populations through grain agriculture
- Warfare and slavery were essential tools for population control and state-building
- Geographic factors and timing of agricultural adoption had long-term consequences for development

## Most Common Student Questions and Comments

**The Persistence Paradox** was the dominant theme: If agriculture made people less healthy and more unequal, why did it spread universally? Why didn't societies return to hunting and gathering? Students grappled with the tension between Diamond's evidence of agricultural harm and agriculture's apparent success as a historical development.

**Cost-Benefit Analysis** was the second major theme: Many students questioned whether the benefits (population growth, technological advancement, eventual quality of life improvements) outweighed the costs (disease, inequality, shorter lifespans, harder work). Several explicitly asked about the trade-offs and whether we should view this as "progress."
