Why, When, and Where Did Industrialization Begin
Between 1700 and 1870, the British economy expanded expanded by a factor of 10
Population was 4x greater and per capita GDP roughly doubled
A transformation of this scale had never occurred in history before. It defied the logic of the Malthusian economy
Most importantly, this growth was sustained. Unlike previous growth episodes, it did not end in stagnation or reversal

--- Slide 3 ---
Preconditions for Economic Growth I
Commercialization
Absence of warfare on British territory since Civil War (with some exceptions)
Commercialization, market integration, monetarization. Historians have called this the “Commercial Revolution”
New goods became available: tea, coffee, tobacco, ready-to-wear clothing, etc.
The Commercial Revolution was linked to an industrious revolution (De Vries, 2008) as households supplied more labor for the market

--- Slide 4 ---
Preconditions for Economic Growth II
Capitalist Agriculture
Labor productivity began to increase in the late Middle Ages
Enclosures led to a reallocation of productive land (Heldring, Robinson, and Vollmer, 2021)
Innovations improved agricultural productivity (e.g., introduction of new crops)



--- Slide 5 ---
Preconditions for Economic Growth III
Political Institutions
Parliament governed on behalf of economic elites
Property rights were protected
Draconian laws against the poor
Mercantilist policies that favored the rise of an Atlantic-based trading system



--- Slide 6 ---
The Rise of London
London had only around 50,000-70,000 people in 1550 (about 2% of the country’s population)
London was only about twice as big as Norwich, the second largest city
But by 1700 London had an estimated population of 575,000 (over 10% of the population)
London was now 19x bigger than Norwich

--- Slide 7 ---
Improvements in Transportation Infrastructure
Transport revolution in 18th-century England
Stagecoach speeds increased from 1.96 journey MPH in 1700 to 7.96 journey MPH in 1820
Improvements in the road network, stagecoach design, number of way-stations
By 1840, Britain had twice the road density of France or Spain
Canals also played a key role in linking Britain’s industrial heartland in the northwest with coal
Greatest improvement in land transport: railways
Rail travel in 1870 was 10x faster than coach travel had been in 1700

--- Slide 8 ---
The Economic Geography of Industrialization
The rise of London was part of a broader shift in the economic geography of England towards ports, especially those involved in Atlantic trade
Bristol and Liverpool grew on the basis of trade with the Americas, especially the slave trade
Industrialization shifted economic activity to the northwest
New economic centers arose in Manchester, Leeds, Birmingham and Sheffield based on manufacturing, textiles, and steel

The Slave and Sugar Nexus
Eric Williams (1944) drew attention to the importance of the Caribbean slave economies for commercial growth and the rise of the Atlantic economy
Sugar was an important import and slavery was central to the economies of the Caribbean in the 18th century
The question is whether this was directly related and/or critical to subsequent industrialization? 

--- Slide 11 ---
King Cotton? 
Textiles were among the leading sectors of the Industrial Revolution
The price of cotton fell by 85% between 1780 and 1850
Beckert (2014) argues that slave-produced cotton textiles were critical to early industrialization
In contrast, Mokyr (2009) argues “If there had been no cotton, there still would have been an Industrial Revolution, even if its exact shape would have differed.”

--- Slide 13 ---
The High Wage Economy
Allen (2009) constructed estimates of real wages for workers across different European and Asian cities
By the 18th century, workers in London were earning higher wages than almost anywhere else
The high cost of labor, he argues, provided a crucial incentive for innovators to develop labor-saving technologies

--- Slide 14 ---
The High Wage Economy
Wages in England were higher relative to the cost of capital (left)
Wages in English cities were high relative to the cost of energy (right)
This configuration of factor prices was the proximate determinant of the pattern of innovation in 18th-century England
Key innovations were labor-saving but expensive in terms of capital and energy

--- Slide 15 ---
The Spinning Jenny
For Allen (2009), the invention of the spinning jenny epitomizes the importance of factor prices and incentives
Hargreaves’s spinning jenny was a multi-spindle spinning wheel
Thread wound and twisted by a flyer
Trains of rollers were used to stretch out the roving
Rollers were the general purpose technology of the period



--- Slide 16 ---
The Spinning Jenny
Allen (2009) argues that technological innovation choice is determined by factor prices
Cost lines depict the ratio of the cost of labour (wages) to the cost of capital (interest rates)
Isoquants depict the cost minimizing combination of inputs for each technology
The new technique is cost minimizing in the high wage economy but not in the low wage economy
If the cost of R&D is too high then the new technique may not be worthwhile if it pushes the high wage isoquant upwards

--- Slide 17 ---
The Spinning Jenny
Allen (2009) argues that regardless of how intensive the machine was being used, it was always profitable for industrialists in England to purchase a spinning jenny
But it was often not profitable for a French industrialist to do so (French wages were lower and capital more expensive)
And it never paid to employ a spinning jenny in India 

--- Slide 18 ---
The Water Frame
A similar analysis can be applied to other innovations
Arkwright's water frame was designed for mechanical power
Relies on two rollers rather than one
First used in 1765, patented in 1769
Downside: could spin only one thread at a time
In 1771, Arkwright built a factory centered around water frames
Arkwright's machines was smashed by angry spinners


--- Slide 19 ---
The Steam Engine I
The spinning jenny did not require prior scientific innovation. But the steam engine did
Steam engine required an understanding of atmospheric pressure (elements of which had been known by the Greeks)
Otto von Guericke demonstrate an air pump in 1657. But it was difficult to develop a machine for commercial use
Breakthrough was due to Thomas Newcomen (used early steam engines to pump water out of mines)
Allen (2009) argues it was this commercial incentive that explains why the steam engine was invented in Britain c. 1700 and not elsewhere

Schematic of the Newcomen steam engine

--- Slide 20 ---
The Steam Engine II
Early steam engines were inefficient, heavy, and cumbersome
But a series of micro-innovations greatly improved it, so that it could be employed outside coal mining
The coal consumption needed to run a steam engine fell from around 45 to 17 pounds per horsepower hour between 1712 and the 1770s (Allen, 2009)
James Watt introduced a separate condenser which greatly improved energy retention
Together with Matthew Boulton he was able to bring to market a commercially viable steam engine

--- Slide 22 ---
The Enlightened Economy

The Scientific Revolution (Chapter 7) was a background condition for the Industrial Revolution
But what linked the Scientific Revolution to industrialization and why was the Industrial Revolution British whereas the Scientific Revolution was pan-European? 
Whereas Allen focuses on the proximate incentives facing innovators, Mokyr analyzes the cultural and institutional factors that made Britain c. 1800 so innovative
Despite their disagreements, Allen and Mokyr’s accounts are complementary (Crafts, 2011)

--- Slide 23 ---
The “Culture of Growth”
Individual scientific genius was not enough. What was required was a “culture of growth”
Mokyr outlines institutions that made this possible:
Scientific societies like the Royal Society (founded in 1660)
Academic journals
A reliable Europe-wide postal system
Comparative intellectual freedom
A market for ideas
This culture wasn’t just academic; it influenced businessmen, mechanics and engineers 
Joseph Wright of Derby
“A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery”

--- Slide 24 ---
The Enlightenment: the link from the Scientific Revolution to the Industrial Revolution
The Scientific Revolution provided a framework for scientists and engineers to think about problems
Knowledge was widely disseminated; importance of testable experiments established
As engineers and inventors improved their machinery, they had a body of knowledge to draw upon
Hence in the long-run the Scientific Revolution prevented the wave of inventions associated with the Industrial Revolution from petering out
The Enlightenment changed how people thought about knowledge and the possibilities of progress
It lowed access costs to knowledge and made best-practice knowledge widely available




--- Slide 25 ---
Human Capital, Skills, and Industrialization
Knowledge interacted with skills
High human capital was key to the production of skilled goods in 18th-century England like watches (Kelly and O'Grada, 2016)
Kelly, Mokyr, and O'Grada (2020): prior to the Industrial Revolution, market integration in England led to specialization in manufacturing in some areas with lower productivity land
Areas which specialized in manufacturing in the 18th century also specialized in (textile) industrialization in the early 19th century
This accounts for the shift in England's economic geography

--- Slide 26 ---
Chapter Summary 
This chapter identified many preconditions that Britain had on the eve of industrialization
Limited government, a large domestic economy, access to the Atlantic economies, plentiful skilled mechanical workers
Many interacted with others so that one mattered only in the presence of another
We examined two recent theories of why Britain industrialized first
Allen emphasizes Britain’s high wages and cheap energy
Incentivized producers to innovate the types of labor-saving technologies that were the hallmark of the Industrial Revolution
Allen’s argument emphasizes economic incentives over science
Mokyr argues that an “Industrial Enlightenment” pervaded late 18 c. Britain, drawing on scientific principles from across Europe
Unlike other parts of Europe, Britain was able to complement scientific production with mechanical skill. This combination enabled the many innovations of the Industrial Revolution and ensured that Britain’s industrialization was not just a blip
