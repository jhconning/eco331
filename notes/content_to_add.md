## State building

The evolution of army recruitment and finance was closely tied to state formation:

1. Initially, kings relied primarily on feudal levies where nobles were obligated to provide knights and armed retainers for limited periods. This system gave nobility significant leverage and limited royal power.
2. By the 14th-15th centuries, monarchs increasingly turned to paid professional soldiers and mercenary companies. This shift required more reliable revenue sources and administrative capacity to manage recruitment and payment.
3. Military pressure from neighboring states created what historians call a "competitive state-building spiral" - innovations in one state forced adaptations in others. The military revolution of the 16th-17th centuries (gunpowder weapons, larger armies, trace italienne fortifications) dramatically increased the costs of warfare.

The relationship with villages and peasantry evolved accordingly:

1. Direct taxation became increasingly important. The French taille evolved from an occasional levy to a permanent tax, while England developed parliamentary taxation that became more systematized over time.
2. When granting concessions to villages, monarchs often demanded direct military service or tax payments in return. Some regions developed militias drawn from the peasantry.
3. Conscription systems emerged gradually. The Swedish system under Gustav Vasa distributed military obligations across villages, each providing recruits and support. France later developed similar systems.
As military needs grew, the relationship between state and peasantry became more direct and bureaucratized. Villages negotiated collective obligations rather than individual feudal services. This created pressure for standardization of peasant rights and obligations, as variable local customs complicated administration.

the general pattern was that military competition drove states to develop more direct relationships with rural populations, gradually undermining seigneurial intermediation and creating new types of state-subject relationships.


## East vs West

- The Elbe River does mark a significant institutional divergence in European economic and social development, and while population density was a factor, there were several other crucial differences:

1. The timing and nature of colonization differed significantly. East of the Elbe was settled later through a medieval process called the "Ostsiedlung" (eastern settlement), where German nobles, merchants, and peasants moved eastward into Slavic territories. This created a distinctive institutional template, with lords granted extensive rights over newly established settlements.
    
2. Urban development followed different patterns. Western European towns often evolved organically and gained significant autonomy, creating exit options for peasants. Eastern cities were fewer, more controlled by territorial rulers, and less integrated with their rural hinterlands. The Hanseatic League towns along the Baltic were exceptions but remained somewhat isolated from their agricultural surroundings.
    
3. Political representation evolved differently. West of the Elbe, representative institutions like the English Parliament or French Estates General included broader social groups beyond the nobility. East of the Elbe, institutions like the Polish Sejm or Hungarian Diet became dominated almost exclusively by noble interests.
    
4. The grain trade to Western Europe became a defining economic feature. Eastern landowners responded to Western European demand by creating large export-oriented estates (Gutsherrschaft system), which required securing a dependent labor force through legal restrictions on peasant mobility.
    
5. The military organization diverged significantly. Western states gradually shifted toward professional armies and direct taxation. Eastern states (particularly Poland-Lithuania) maintained systems where nobles provided military service in exchange for control over land and peasants, reinforcing seigneurial power.
    
6. Legal systems evolved differently, with Western Europe gradually developing more uniform state-administered law, while Eastern Europe maintained stronger legal distinctions between nobles and commoners, with nobles retaining significant judicial authority over their peasants.
    

The "second serfdom" that emerged east of the Elbe wasn't simply economic backwardness, but a rational response to specific economic opportunities and constraints within a different institutional framework. This divergence had lasting consequences, creating what some economic historians call the "Little Divergence" within Europe, predating the larger divergence between Europe and the rest of the world.
