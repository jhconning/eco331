# Slavery and the British Industrial Revolution

> **Source:** VoxTalks Economics podcast, "Slavery and the Industrial Revolution" (Tim Phillips interviews S Heblich and J Voth on recent research) ([link](https://cepr.org/multimedia/slavery-and-industrial-revolution), ~27 min)


## Overview
This paper, by Heblich, Redding, and Voth (2022), investigates the causal link between the wealth derived from colonial slaveholding and Britain’s economic development during the Industrial Revolution. While the "Williams Thesis" long posited that profits from the slave trade fueled British industrialization, quantitative economic historians have historically remained skeptical, often citing the relatively small profit margins of the trade itself. 

The authors provide a new perspective by shifting the focus from the trade of enslaved people to the massive wealth generated by colonial slaveholding (plantations producing sugar, cotton, coffee, and tobacco). Using granular data from the 1833 Abolition of Slavery Act compensation records, they map this wealth spatially across Britain and utilize a dynamic spatial model and a novel instrumental variables (IV) strategy to demonstrate that slaveholding wealth was a significant catalyst for industrial growth.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The authors argue that slavery wealth accelerated the Industrial Revolution through three primary mechanisms:

1.  **Increased Investment Capacity:** Access to slave-derived wealth raised the returns to capital accumulation, stimulating the domestic capital stock.
2.  **Collateralization:** Enslaved persons were used as collateral for loans, which alleviated financial frictions and enabled greater investment in domestic manufacturing.
3.  **Structural Transformation:** Capitalists with access to slavery investments shifted their capital toward capital-intensive manufacturing sectors (e.g., cotton mills, steam engines) rather than agriculture.

To establish causality, the authors instrument 1833 slaveholding wealth using "voyage outcome" data:
*   **The Instrument:** They use weather shocks (wind conditions) that occurred during the transatlantic "Middle Passage." Unfavorable weather increased voyage duration, which in turn increased mortality rates among the enslaved and reduced profitability.
*   **The Logic:** Reduced profitability caused slave-trading ancestors in specific British locations to exit the trade. This exogenous variation in ancestral participation allows the researchers to isolate the causal effect of slavery wealth on subsequent regional industrialization.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & Industrialization:** The paper provides empirical support for the idea that the Industrial Revolution was not merely an endogenous technological "miracle," but was significantly boosted by capital accumulation linked to the Atlantic colonial system.
*   **Institutions:** The 1833 Abolition of Slavery Act serves as a "natural experiment" that reveals the distribution of wealth, illustrating how historical colonial exploitation left a lasting imprint on the domestic economic geography of the metropole.
*   **Geography and Spatial Economics:** The study uses a hexagonal grid across England and Wales to account for commuting distances and regional specialization. It identifies that the economic impact of slavery was highly uneven, concentrating in the North and West, and effectively creating a "path dependency" for industrial centers like Liverpool and Bristol.
*   **Structural Transformation:** The findings illustrate a classic transition from an agricultural base to a capital-intensive manufacturing economy, with slavery wealth providing the necessary "push" to accelerate this transition in specific localities.
*   **Financial Frictions:** The model highlights how financial constraints in the 18th and 19th centuries were loosened by the ability to monetize human property (slavery), thereby fueling the rise of industrial capitalists.
