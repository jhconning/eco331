# Slavery and the British Industrial Revolution

> **Source:** Heblich, Stephan, Stephen J. Redding, and Hans-Joachim Voth. 2022. "Slavery and the British Industrial Revolution." Working Paper. National Bureau of Economic Research. [PaperLinks to an external site.](https://www.princeton.edu/~reddings/papers/SBIR_Paper.pdf)


### Overview
This paper, authored by Heblich, Redding, and Voth (2022), investigates the extent to which wealth generated from the British colonial plantation economy—specifically slave-holding—contributed to the acceleration of the Industrial Revolution. While the "Williams Hypothesis" historically suggested a link between the triangular trade and British industrialization, quantitative economic historians have long been skeptical, often arguing that slave-trading profits were too small relative to total GDP. The authors shift the focus from the slave trade to **slave-holding** (ownership of plantations and enslaved people), using granular compensation data from the 1833 Abolition of Slavery Act to identify the geographic distribution of slavery-derived wealth within Britain.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The authors argue that slavery wealth acted as a catalyst for industrialization through three primary mechanisms:
1. **Investment Productivity:** Access to highly profitable colonial investments raised the overall rate of return on capital, stimulating higher levels of total capital accumulation.
2. **Collateralization:** Enslaved persons were frequently used as collateral to secure loans. This ability to collateralize slavery wealth helped alleviate financial constraints, facilitating investment in domestic manufacturing.
3. **Structural Transformation:** Using a dynamic spatial model, the authors show that in regions with high slavery investment, capital was disproportionately reinvested locally. This promoted a shift away from agricultural production toward capital-intensive manufacturing (e.g., cotton mills and steam-powered factories).

To establish causality, the authors introduce a novel instrumental variable based on **middle-passage mortality**. They demonstrate that adverse weather conditions (wind/shocks) during transatlantic voyages caused higher mortality, which lowered trade profits and caused slave-traders to exit the industry. By linking the geographic locations of slave-traders' ancestors to the location of slave-holders in 1833, they show that areas with exogenously higher exposure to the slave economy experienced faster industrial growth.

### Connections to Course Themes
* **Economic History & Structural Transformation:** The paper provides empirical evidence for the transition from an agricultural economy to an industrial one, mapping the "geography" of the Industrial Revolution. It demonstrates how capital flows from the periphery (colonies) were re-invested in the core (Britain).
* **Institutions & Financial Development:** The authors emphasize the role of financial frictions and the importance of collateral availability. The ability to use human beings as collateral within a specific legal framework allowed for the mobilization of capital that otherwise might have remained stagnant.
* **The Malthusian Epoch and the Demographic Transition:** The paper connects slavery wealth to the escape from Malthusian constraints. By boosting income and industrial capacity, slavery wealth provided the capital necessary for Britain to sustain growth that outpaced population increases, contributing to the broader demographic and economic transition observed in the 19th century.
* **Geography:** The research uses a hexagonal spatial grid to analyze how wealth effects were distributed locally, showing that the benefits of the colonial economy were not spread evenly but were concentrated in areas linked to the slave trade and plantation ownership.
