Industrial Revolution
did not rely directly on

• After 1850, however,

<a name="br6"></a>The Demographic Transition

• A key reason why per capita
 incomes increased during the 2nd
 Industrial Revolution was that
 demographic behavior changed

• Beginning with France (and then
 later in England), people started
 to control fertility within marriage

• This demographic transition
 meant that economies stopped
 behaving in a Malthusian fashion

• Population growth slowed even

as incomes improved

<a name="br9"></a>Late Industrializers

• The key insight of Alexander
 Gershenrkon was that later
 industrializers did not have to go
 through the same steps as
 Britain

• Early stages of development

could be leap-frogged

• Late industrializers had a

backlog of advanced

technologies they could adopt

• Germany harnessed capital on a
 larger scale (via investment
 banks) than Britain had ever
 done, becoming a leader in new
 sectors such as chemical
 fertilizers (Bayer, IG Farben) and
 arms (Krupp)




<a name="br10"></a>Human Capital and the
Diffusion of Industrialization

• Human capital played a critical role
 in the diffusion of industrialization
 and in the Second Industrial
 Revolution

• Becker et al. (2011) document this
 by studying regional variation in
 industrialization within Germany in
 the late 19th century

• They main finding is that pre-
 industrial levels of education
 (measured in 1816) predict which
 locations industrialized in the
 second part of the 19th century

• They show that education was
 associated with non-textile-based
 industrialization




<a name="br11"></a>Human Capital and the Diffusion of
Industrialization

• The effects of
 education were
 strongest on non-
 textiles and non-
 metal based

industrialization

• There was no effect
 on textile industries
 (which required less
 human capital)

<a name="br18"></a>The Rise of the United States

• America’s growth in the 19th century

was also on the extensive margin

• Vast swathes of land were added
 and the population increased from
 around 2.5 million in 1776 to 76
 million in 1900.

• Staples Theory

• Immigration (especially from Ireland,
 Germany, Italy, and Eastern Europe)
 fueled this expansion

• This expansion in the size of the
 market complemented a distinctively
 “American” system of manufacturing

US population and immigrant share, 1850-2010




<a name="br19"></a>The Second Industrial Revolution and American
Economic Growth

• The American system of manufacturing
 centered on machine tools and the
 development of interchangeable parts

• A large market and homogeneous tastes

encouraged product standardization

• Standardized products could be easily

tweaked or improved upon

• Standardized products encouraged the
 standardization of other parts, and this
 made further innovation easier

• America became the leader in global
 manufacturing and in technology (though
 not necessarily in science until WWII)




<a name="br20"></a>The Soviet Detour I

• In the 20th century the Soviet Union was
 seen as pioneering an alternative growth
 model to that of the United States or
 Western Europe

• The Soviet Union achieved rapid
 industrialization in the 1930s (at vast human
 cost including millions of deaths in Ukraine
 due to famine) and fast growth for several
 decades

• This was interpreted as due to the success

of economic planning



<a name="br21"></a>The Soviet Detour II

• Russia under the Tsars was a

rapidly growing and industrializing economy, on
course to achieve rapid catch-up
growth and industrialization prior
to WWI

• Much of the early Soviet growth

was recovery from the

devastation the Russian economy experienced in WWI
and during the Russian civil war

• This growth was largely achieved

by mobilizing tremendous resources (labor, land, natural
resources, and physical capital)



<a name="br22"></a>The Soviet Detour III

• Planning mobilized resources on a large scale but

at tremendous costs

• Even during the early phase of rapid growth,
 inefficiencies and wastage were legendary
 (Gregory and Harrison, 2005). The price system
 existed to allocate scarce resources. Soviet
 planners struggled to “price” intermediate goods

• Centralization of all economic decisions in the
 hands of a dictator produced chaos. Stalin spent
 his time making seemingly trivial decisions,
 including setting the price of metro tickets and
 making detailed managerial appointments
 (Gregory, 2004a, pp. 112–14)

• Corruption and rent-seeking were endemic

• There were reforms after the death of Stalin. But
 after the 1950s, the USSR struggled to provide
 basic consumer goods that people wished to buy
