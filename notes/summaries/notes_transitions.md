# The Feudal Origins of Thornfield Manor (c. 1200-1300)

## Manor Formation and Early Structure

In the centuries before our Tudor example, Thornfield would have originated as a Saxon settlement that was reorganized under Norman feudal principles after 1066. By around 1200, it would have been structured as a classic feudal manor:

### Land Organization

- **Demesne lands**: Approximately one-third to one-half of the arable land was the lord's demesne, worked directly for his benefit
- **Tenant lands**: The remainder was held by various tenants in the open fields
- **Common lands**: Meadows, pasture, woodlands, and waste were used collectively under regulated access

## Labor Relations and Tenant Classes

### Unfree Tenants (the majority in this period)

1. **Villeins** (also called serfs): The primary unfree class who:
    
    - Held standard landholdings called "virgates" (typically 20-30 acres) or "half-virgates"
    - Owed significant labor services on the lord's demesne (often 2-3 days weekly)
    - Paid additional dues in kind (grain, chickens, eggs)
    - Were bound to the manor and needed permission to leave, marry, or sell animals
    - Were subject to special payments like "merchet" (fine paid when a daughter married)
    - Had to use the lord's mill, oven, and other monopolies ("banalities")
    - Were subject to the manor court rather than royal courts
2. **Cottars/Bordars**: Smaller-scale unfree tenants who:
    
    - Held just a cottage and perhaps 5 acres or less
    - Provided fewer day-works but often specialized labor
    - Might work for larger tenants to supplement their income

### Free Tenants (a minority in most manors)

1. **Free Sokemen**: Independent farmers who:
    
    - Paid primarily money rent rather than labor
    - Had limited or no labor obligations
    - Could leave the manor and sell their land
    - Had access to royal courts
2. **Free Sergeants**: Specialized servants who:
    
    - Held land in return for specific services (smithing, carpentry, administrative duties)
    - Often enjoyed privileged status

## Origins of the Open Field System

The open field system predated the Norman Conquest and likely emerged gradually between 800-1100 CE:

1. **Agricultural factors**:
    
    - Heavy plow technology required cooperative ox teams
    - Risk management through scattered strips with different soil types
    - Shared resources (animals, equipment) necessitated coordination
2. **Social factors**:
    
    - Community regulation of cropping patterns
    - Balancing equity with hierarchy
    - Family division of holdings over generations
3. **Implementation**:
    
    - Fields divided into furlongs (groups of strips running the same direction)
    - Each tenant assigned strips in different areas
    - Rotation between crops and fallow managed communally

## Daily Life on the Manor

The agricultural year was structured by:

1. **Seasonal labor**: Plowing, sowing, harvesting, with specific obligations for each
2. **Boon works**: Extra labor during critical periods (harvest) often followed by feasts
3. **Week works**: Regular labor on the demesne by unfree tenants
4. **Court obligations**: Attendance at the manor court where disputes were settled and rules enforced

## Gradual Changes Leading to Tudor Period

By the 14th-15th centuries, Thornfield would have undergone significant changes:

1. **Effects of the Black Death (1348-1350)**:
    
    - Severe population decline (30-50%)
    - Labor shortages forcing lords to commute labor services to money rents
    - Weakening of traditional bondage as peasants gained bargaining power
2. **Evolution of tenures**:
    
    - Many villain tenures gradually evolving into copyhold
    - Increased social mobility
    - Decline in personal serfdom but continuation of the open field system
3. **Rise of yeoman farmers**:
    
    - Former villeins or their descendants accumulating land
    - Some achieving effective freedom through prosperity
    - Creating the social foundation for the more stratified Tudor village we saw earlier

This manorial system, with its mix of unfree and free tenures, provided the foundation upon which later developments like Tudor enclosures would act, fundamentally transforming English rural society and agriculture.

# The Tudor Enclosures: A Local Case Study

To understand the Tudor enclosures (roughly 1485-1603), let me illustrate how this process might have unfolded in a hypothetical English village called Thornfield.

## Thornfield Before Enclosure (c. 1500)

Thornfield was organized around three large open fields divided into strips, with a common pasture, woodland, and waste areas. The community included:

- The lord of the manor (Sir William Harrington), who held the demesne lands
- 12 yeoman farmers with substantial holdings (a mix of freeholders and copyholders)
- 30 smaller tenant farmers (mostly copyholders)
- 25 cottagers with small garden plots and common rights
- Various landless laborers

Most villagers participated in the open-field system, with:

- Scattered strips in each field
- Rights to graze animals on the stubble after harvest
- Access to common pasture, woodland for pig foraging, and waste for gathering fuel

## The Process of Enclosure in Thornfield (1520-1540)

### Initial Motivation

Sir William noticed wool prices rising dramatically while grain prices stagnated. Converting arable land to sheep pasture could be far more profitable than traditional farming.

### Step 1: Legal Groundwork (1520-1525)

Sir William began by:

- Reviewing manor court rolls to identify copyholders with weak claims
- Not renewing tenancies when they expired
- Challenging customary rights in court
- Offering to buy out some freeholders

### Step 2: Initial Enclosures (1525-1530)

- The first enclosures focused on the manor's home farm
- Hedges and ditches were constructed around former demesne strips
- Some tenants were evicted when their customary tenure could be legally challenged
- Several small copyholders were compensated (minimally) to surrender their holdings

### Step 3: Resistance and Negotiation (1530-1535)

- Villagers initially resisted by tearing down hedges and continuing to graze animals
- Some filed legal complaints with the Court of Requests
- The local priest spoke against the enclosures
- Eventually, Sir William negotiated with the more powerful yeomen:
    - Offered them enclosed consolidated holdings
    - Provided leases on favorable terms
    - Created divisions within the community

### Step 4: Completion (1535-1540)

- A formal enclosure agreement was drawn up
- Remaining open fields were divided and allotted
- Common rights were extinguished
- Hedges, ditches, and fences marked new boundaries
- Many smaller tenants received tiny plots or nothing at all

## Thornfield After Enclosure (c. 1545)

The village landscape and society transformed dramatically:

- Large enclosed sheep pastures replaced open fields
- About 15 households had been displaced entirely
- The remaining cottagers struggled without common rights
- Sir William's profits increased substantially
- Several yeomen prospered with their new consolidated holdings
- The village population declined by nearly a third
- New wage relationships replaced customary obligations
- Several cottages were abandoned and fell into ruin

## Broader Context

This pattern, while hypothetical for Thornfield, represents what happened across many parts of England during the Tudor period. The government responded with various anti-enclosure statutes and commissions, but these were irregularly enforced and often ineffective against determined landowners.

Unlike the later Parliamentary enclosures of the 18th-19th centuries, Tudor enclosures typically occurred through these piecemeal, often semi-legal or extralegal methods that gradually transformed both the physical landscape and social structure of rural England.
