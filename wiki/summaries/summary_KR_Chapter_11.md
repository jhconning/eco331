# Chapter 11: The World is Rich

> **Source:** KR 11 "The World is Rich"


## Overview
Chapter 11 of Koyama and Rubin’s *How the World Became Rich* serves as the synthesis of the authors' previous analysis. Having examined the historical divergence between the "Great Divergence" (the rise of the West) and the subsequent "Great Convergence" (the rise of the Rest), this chapter addresses the current state of global wealth. The authors argue that the modern world is fundamentally defined by the escape from Malthusian constraints, a process driven by institutional foundations, state capacity, and the spread of technology. The chapter emphasizes that while extreme poverty has declined significantly, the "Great Convergence" is not a uniform process, and institutional variation continues to dictate why some nations flourish while others remain trapped in middle-income stagnation.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The chapter posits that the global shift toward prosperity is not inevitable but contingent upon specific historical and political choices. Key mechanisms include:

*   **The End of the Malthusian Trap:** The authors reiterate that modern economic growth is characterized by sustained increases in per capita income, which decouples growth from population pressure—a feat impossible in the pre-1800 world.
*   **The Importance of State Capacity:** Wealth creation in the modern era requires a state capable of providing public goods, enforcing property rights, and maintaining order. The authors argue that countries that failed to develop bureaucratic and fiscal capacity during the early stages of the Great Convergence struggle to foster the private-sector investment necessary for modern industry.
*   **The Role of Ideas and Culture:** Beyond mere physical capital, the "World is Rich" because of the global adoption of specific social and intellectual norms, including the scientific method, the value of education, and institutional tolerance for creative destruction.
*   **The Contingency of Success:** Koyama and Rubin argue that the "East Asian Miracle" (e.g., South Korea, Taiwan, China) demonstrates that growth is possible for non-Western nations, provided they adopt specific institutional frameworks that incentivize export-oriented growth and human capital investment.

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **Economic History and the Great Divergence:** This chapter acts as the bookend to the course's study of the Great Divergence. By tracing how the mechanisms that once led to European supremacy (state-building, inclusive institutions) have been adapted and adopted globally, the authors explain the current transition toward a more multipolar economic distribution.
*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** The authors contextualize the "rich world" by contrasting it with the millenia of stagnation preceding the Industrial Revolution. They highlight that the transition to the modern world is defined by the permanent cessation of the Malthusian cycle, where fertility decline accompanies rising income.
*   **Institutions vs. Geography:** Consistent with the course's focus on the "Acemoglu-Robinson" institutional tradition, this chapter reinforces that geography (e.g., being landlocked, tropical disease burdens) acts as a headwind, but it is not destiny. The authors illustrate that institutional reforms can overcome even the most daunting geographical disadvantages.
*   **Demographic Transition:** The chapter underscores that the transition to a rich world is inextricably linked to the demographic transition. As nations become wealthier, they transition from high-fertility/high-mortality societies to low-fertility/low-mortality societies, a process that enables the "demographic dividend" necessary for sustained modern growth.
