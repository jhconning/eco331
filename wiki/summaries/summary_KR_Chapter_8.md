# Koyama & Rubin Chapter 8: Britain’s Industrial Revolution

> **Source:** KR 8 "Britain's Industrial Revolution"


## Overview
Between 1700 and 1870, the British economy underwent a transformation unprecedented in human history. Unlike previous growth episodes that succumbed to the Malthusian trap, Britain experienced sustained expansion—with population quadrupling and per capita GDP doubling. This chapter examines the preconditions for this "Great Divergence," including advancements in infrastructure, institutional stability, and the shifting geography of trade, before evaluating the primary competing theories for why this transition originated specifically in Britain.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms

### Preconditions for Growth
*   **Commercial and Agricultural Revolution:** A transition toward market integration, increased labor supply for the market (the "industrious revolution"), and advancements in agricultural productivity through enclosure and new crops provided the necessary surplus to support an industrializing population.
*   **Institutional Framework:** Britain benefited from a political environment that favored economic elites, protected property rights, and utilized mercantilist policies to dominate Atlantic trade.
*   **Geographic and Infrastructural Shifts:** The rise of London and the shift of economic activity to the northwest (Manchester, Leeds, etc.) were facilitated by major investments in transportation (turnpikes, canals, and eventually railways).

### Competing Theories of Industrialization
*   **The High-Wage Economy (Robert Allen):** Allen argues that Britain’s unique factor prices—specifically, high wages relative to the cost of capital and energy—provided a massive incentive to innovate. Producers adopted labor-saving technologies (like the spinning jenny and steam engine) because they were cost-minimizing in Britain, whereas they remained unprofitable in low-wage economies like France or India.
*   **The Enlightened Economy (Joel Mokyr):** Mokyr emphasizes the "Industrial Enlightenment" and a "culture of growth." He argues that the Scientific Revolution created a framework of knowledge that was widely disseminated through scientific societies, journals, and a marketplace for ideas. This culture lowered the cost of accessing knowledge, allowing engineers and businessmen to apply scientific principles to practical mechanical problems.
*   **Human Capital and Skills:** Recent research (Kelly, Mokyr, & O'Grada) suggests that Britain’s prior specialization in high-skilled manufacturing created a foundation of human capital that allowed the country to successfully adopt and scale new industrial technologies.

## Connections to Course Themes

*   **The Malthusian Epoch:** The Industrial Revolution represents the definitive break from the Malthusian trap. While previous eras saw growth limited by resource constraints and population booms, the innovations of the 18th century enabled sustained, long-run growth in living standards.
*   **Institutions:** The chapter highlights how political institutions (the protection of property rights and pro-market government) served as critical "background conditions." This aligns with broader course discussions on how secure property rights encourage long-term investment.
*   **Geography and Atlantic Trade:** The "Slave and Sugar Nexus" demonstrates how institutionalized trade routes and the Atlantic economy provided the commercial surplus necessary for early industrialization, though scholars like Mokyr argue that the underlying culture of innovation was equally, if not more, critical than any single trade commodity like cotton.
*   **Culture:** The "Enlightened Economy" thesis underscores the importance of cultural norms—specifically the valuing of intellectual freedom, empirical experimentation, and the dissemination of best-practice knowledge—in fostering an environment where technological progress becomes cumulative rather than stagnant.
