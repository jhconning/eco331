# Q_Geography


Here are five of the best or most representative questions or comments from the student discussion on geography and economic development:

1. **Causation vs. Correlation:** Brittany questions whether geography directly causes development or is merely correlated with it, citing the Fertile Crescent and New Guinea as examples.
    
2. **Impact of the Columbian Exchange:** Amy points out that while the readings discuss the impact of new crops on the Old World, they neglect to mention the effects on indigenous populations in the Americas.
    
3. **Persistence of Poverty:** Joseph asks why some countries remain poor despite having good land and resources, suggesting that factors beyond geography are at play.
    
4. **Role of Insects:** Elma questions the extent to which insects like the tsetse fly are solely responsible for hindering economic growth in Africa, prompting further discussion on other environmental factors.
    
5. **Geography and Human Agency:** Jubayer and Fatjeta raise questions about the interplay between geography and human agency, asking how much of a society's success is determined by geography versus human adaptation and whether societies can overcome historical geographic advantages or disadvantages.
    
 **Summary of best or most representative comments and questions from 0204_neolithic.pdf:**

6. **Was agriculture a mistake?** Many students engage with Diamond's provocative claim that agriculture was the worst mistake in human history. They consider the trade-offs between the benefits of settled agriculture (e.g., increased food production, technological advancements) and its costs (e.g., malnutrition, disease, inequality, coerced labor). Some are unconvinced by Diamond's argument, while others express curiosity about alternatives or how negative consequences could have been mitigated.
    
7. **Impact on health and lifestyle:** Students note the evidence presented on the negative health effects of agriculture, such as reduced height and life expectancy. They also reflect on the broader lifestyle changes associated with agriculture, including increased sedentism, changes in diet, and the emergence of social hierarchies. Some question whether hunter-gatherers might have been happier or healthier.
    
8. **Social inequality and coerced labor:** Students pick up on the themes of social inequality and coerced labor that arose with agriculture and the formation of early states. They ask questions about the dynamics of slavery, the role of women, and the emergence of social hierarchies.
    
9. **Long-term implications:** Students consider the long-term implications of the Neolithic Revolution, asking how it shaped modern societies and economies. They ponder whether agriculture was inevitable, whether its negative consequences can be mitigated, and whether people were ultimately better off with or without it.
    
10. **Alternative perspectives:** Some students offer alternative perspectives or challenge the readings. For example, Elma suggests that Diamond romanticizes hunter-gatherer societies, while Catherine questions the validity of Diamond's arguments against agriculture.
    

**Suggested questions for the class:**

- Diamond argues that agriculture led to a decline in human health and well-being. How does this perspective challenge traditional narratives of progress?
- Scott highlights the role of coercion and control in early states. How did the transition to agriculture contribute to the rise of these coercive institutions?
- Many students raise questions about the trade-offs between the benefits and costs of agriculture. How can we weigh these trade-offs in evaluating the long-term impact of the Neolithic Revolution?
- Some students express curiosity about alternatives to agriculture or ways to mitigate its negative consequences. What might these alternatives or mitigation strategies look like?
- The readings offer contrasting perspectives on the Neolithic Revolution. How can we reconcile these different viewpoints in understanding this pivotal period in human history?
