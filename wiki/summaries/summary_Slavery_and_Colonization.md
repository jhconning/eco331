# The Economic Geography of American Slavery

> **Source:** Solow, Barbara, 1993. "Slavery and Colonization," Chapter 1 in _Slavery and the Rise of the Atlantic System_. Cambridge University Press. [PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1eq6wZ9QlW77dBg46yDFD0swRFFWcZ1TX/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
"The Economic Geography of American Slavery" (Allen, Chen, and Naidu, 2025) provides a comprehensive general equilibrium analysis of the U.S. antebellum economy. By constructing a massive micro-dataset combining the 1860 free and slave censuses with occupational and geographic data, the authors examine how slavery distorted the spatial and sectoral allocation of labor. The paper develops a quantitative spatial model to demonstrate that slavery acted as a profound drag on economic efficiency, welfare, and structural transformation. Their findings quantify the massive welfare loss experienced by the enslaved and reveal how the institution inhibited the growth of manufacturing and services by tethering labor to unproductive, disease-ridden agricultural regions.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The paper conceptualizes slavery as an institution that allowed slaveholders to impose three specific, quantifiable distortions:
1. **Coercion:** Slaveholders forced enslaved workers to supply higher labor effort than they would have chosen under a free market, accounting for a significant portion of the welfare loss.
2. **Markdowns (Extraction):** Slaveholders captured the proceeds of enslaved labor; the authors estimate a markdown of approximately 82% of the marginal product, leaving the enslaved with only a fraction of their economic contribution.
3. **Misallocation:** Enslaved workers were sorted into specific occupations and geographies—typically high-coercion and high-malaria environments—to maximize owner profits rather than worker utility or marginal productivity.

**Quantitative Findings:**
* **Welfare:** Complete emancipation would have provided a welfare gain equivalent to a ~1,200% increase in wages for the enslaved.
* **Structural Transformation:** Slavery significantly slowed the transition from an agrarian economy to one balanced with manufacturing and services. Emancipation results in a predicted 9.1% increase in aggregate GDP, driven by the flight of formerly enslaved workers from agriculture into more productive sectors and regions (including the North).
* **General Equilibrium Effects:** While the enslaved gain significantly, the model predicts a slight decrease in welfare for already-free workers (0.7%) due to increased competition and lower equilibrium wages in previously "exclusive" high-amenity locations.

## Connections to Course Themes
* **Economic History & Growth:** The paper directly challenges the Fogel and Engerman thesis by using modern spatial general equilibrium modeling to show that slavery was a substantial barrier to aggregate growth and industrialization.
* **Institutions:** The authors treat slavery as a set of property rights and coercion mechanisms. By comparing the counterfactual "emancipated" economy to the 1860 baseline, they quantify the economic cost of these extractive institutions.
* **Geography:** The research emphasizes how innate geographic features (cotton suitability, malaria prevalence, and distance to the Northern border) interacted with coercion. It highlights that the "Cotton Belt" was not just a natural comparative advantage but a product of forced labor allocation designed to minimize escape and maximize coercion.
* **Demographic Transition:** The paper touches on labor supply elasticities and demographic sorting, illustrating how the end of slavery functioned as a massive, abrupt reallocation of labor that mirrors the structural changes seen in the transition from agrarian to industrial societies.
