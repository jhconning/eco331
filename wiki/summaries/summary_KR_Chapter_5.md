# Koyama & Rubin Chapter 5: Fewer Babies?

> **Source:** KR 5 Fewer Babies?


## Overview
Chapter 5 explores the role of demography in preindustrial society, focusing on how population dynamics shaped living standards and influenced the transition toward modern economic growth. It examines the constraints of the "Malthusian Trap"—where gains in income are typically consumed by population growth—and investigates whether specific demographic shocks (such as the Black Death) or social structures (such as the European Marriage Pattern) provided an escape route toward higher per capita incomes.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
* **The Malthusian Model:** Proposes that in the preindustrial era, any rise in income led to lower mortality and higher fertility. Due to the diminishing marginal product of labor, population growth eventually outstripped resource gains, keeping per capita income stagnant. Technology, in this model, only leads to higher population density, not higher long-run living standards.
* **The Demographic Shock of the Black Death:** The massive mortality event (40–60% of the population) forced an increase in real wages by drastically reducing the labor supply. While this supports Malthusian predictions, it also triggered structural shifts, such as the decline of serfdom in Western Europe and increased urbanization.
* **The European Marriage Pattern (EMP):** Characterized by a later age of first marriage (often ~25) and higher rates of permanent celibacy. This served as a "low pressure" demographic regime that limited fertility. Scholars debate whether this practice encouraged economic development by fostering female labor markets, human capital investment, or shifting toward pastoral agriculture.
* **Unified Growth Theory:** Suggests that the transition to modern growth occurred through an interaction between demography and the incentive to invest in human capital. As societies shifted from quantity to quality in child-rearing, they broke the Malthusian cycle, leading to the demographic transition and sustained growth.

## Connections to Course Themes

* **The Malthusian Epoch:** The chapter defines the core constraints of the pre-1800 world. It utilizes the Malthusian framework to explain why technological progress before the Industrial Revolution resulted in larger, rather than wealthier, populations.
* **Institutions:** The text notes that demographic shocks interacted with political institutions (e.g., the Statute of Laborers, 1349). While elites attempted to use legislative power to suppress wage growth after the Black Death, these institutions were ultimately unable to override the fundamental market forces of labor scarcity.
* **Geography:** The concept of the "Hajnal Line" is introduced to distinguish the demographic behavior of Northwestern Europe from the rest of Eurasia, highlighting how geographic and social norms can influence regional economic divergence.
* **Demographic Transition:** The chapter links preindustrial demographic behavior to the later, modern transition, arguing that changes in fertility behavior were essential to escaping stagnation and enabling the higher human capital investment required for sustained economic growth.
