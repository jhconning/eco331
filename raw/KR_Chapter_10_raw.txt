--- Slide 2 ---
Is The World Rich?
There is still much abject poverty in the world
But billions have been lifted out of poverty in the last century alone
As recently as 1960, Asia was extremely poor
Far from all poverty has been eradicated, but East Asia is well on the path to becoming rich
Plenty of reason to believe this will spread to sub-Saharan Africa, poorer parts of Latin America, Central and South Asia

--- Slide 3 ---
The Shadow of Colonization
Labor-scarce, land-abundant colonies in North America, Australia, New Zealand, and Argentina flourished in the late 19th century
Institutional development followed that of parent countries, attracted migrants and capital
Elsewhere, extractive institutions were put in place
Many of these former colonies still languish behind the world’s leading economies
Conditions prior to colonization also mattered, especially ethnic fragmentation
Policy choices mattered too, especially those favoring certain groups over others


--- Slide 4 ---
India’s Decline
Around 1600, per capita income in India was around 60% of British
By 1870, it was 8.7% of British p.c. GDP. What happened?
Worsening climate, multiple invasions, and endemic warfare after 1700
Conquest by the British East India company involved protracted warfare and pillaging
Indian textile industry hit hard by cheap British exports
Economy remained Malthusian: productivity gains led to more population, not more per capita income
Colonial India

--- Slide 5 ---
India after Independence
After independence, colonial legacy left growth opportunities circumscribed
Central economic planning, state led industrialization led to disappointment
“License Raj” created incentives for rent-seeking, corruption
Liberalizing reforms in 1990s have brought economic growth
Growth has delivered on poverty reduction: dramatic drop in those living in abject poverty, though still a long way to go
Serious environmental degradation

--- Slide 6 ---
How Japan Became Rich
Japan: the first non-Western country to achieve sustained economic growth
After Meiji Restoration (1868): implemented a rapid program of state-building and industrialization
Prior to industrialization, relatively high urbanization (13%); expansion of markets and internal trade, de facto property rights for peasants, but low real wages
Japan did not need to reinvent the wheel: adopted technologies from technological leaders
But not all countries have successfully caught up with economic leaders. Why was Japan different?


Emperor Meiji

--- Slide 7 ---
Why Was Japan Able to Catch Up?
Japan had preconditions noted in previous chapters!
Human capital: prior to Meiji Restoration, high human capital for a pre-industrial society (literacy and numeracy comparable to Western Europe)
High per capita tax revenue at local level funded public goods (schools, road infrastructure, drainage) (Sng and Moriguchi 2015)
Meiji reforms: compulsory education, higher taxes, abolished guild and local monopolies
Favored business, but not protectionist; became highly integrated in the world economy (Vries, 2019)
A Meiji School

--- Slide 8 ---
Export-led, Manufacturing Growth
Japan: export-led growth based on manufacturing
By the beginning of the 20th century, the structure of the Japanese economy already resembled that of the industrial leaders
The share of manufacturing in total output matched the US, even though Japan was far poorer
Required mobilization of industrial workforce from the countryside; large number of women subject to harsh conditions
Growth of Western-style scientific and technical education
Redesigned Western technology to make it cost-effective in low-wage economy
Kojo
(female factory workers)

--- Slide 10 ---
How the East Asian Tigers Became Rich
The “East Asian Tigers” (Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan) became rich in the 2nd half of the 20th century
Despite having very low GDP per capita as recently as the 1960s
By 2019, per capita incomes in Latin America and the Caribbean were only 28.6% of East Asian Tigers
East Asian Tigers: catch-up growth by importing new ideas and technologies
But why were these countries able to catch up while many other parts of the world remained poor?
Per capita GDP in various parts of the world, 1960–2019

--- Slide 11 ---
Catch-up Growth
Because of technological advances in developed countries, catch-up potential is greater than ever before
This is illustrated by Parente and Prescott (2000): countries that reached 2000 (1990 $) earlier took longer for their incomes to subsequently double
East Asian countries after WW2 were far from the economic frontier and hence had tremendous catch-up “potential”

--- Slide 12 ---
Catch-up Growth
Catch-up growth is not simply a matter of importing technology
Other factors highlighted in this book matter (institutions, culture, geography, demography, colonial past)
Economic geography: emergence of a cluster of densely populated manufacturing and consumption hubs in East Asia was an important precondition
Well placed to follow the path pioneered by Japan
Late growth meant they were far from the economic frontier and had a larger “backlog” of modern technologies to import

--- Slide 13 ---
Trade, Institutions and Catch-up Growth
The East Asian economies were all relatively small: were forced to rely on international markets
Did not fall into the trap that many larger developing countries did of relying on protective tariffs and subsidies to support domestic manufacturing firms (e.g., Brazil, India)
Institutions that constrain executive power are important … but the East Asian Tigers were not initially democratic
Each solved in their own way
Hong Kong (a British colony at the time): most reliant on markets, open to trade
Singapore (ex-British colony): pragmatic combination of market & government
South Korea: weak formal constraints; large industrial conglomerates (chaebols) relied on export markets, providing much-needed market discipline

--- Slide 14 ---
How China is Becoming Rich
After a long delay, the Chinese economy has grown rapidly since the late 1970s
As recently as 1990, 66.2% of Chinese were earning less than $1.90 per day, and 98.3% of the population made less than $5.50 per day
As of 2016, only 0.5% of the population made less than $1.90 per day and 23.9% made less than $5.50 per day
Per capita GDP has risen from $71 in 1962 (around $0.20 per day) to $10,262 in 2019
What happened?

--- Slide 15 ---
China under the Qing
Pomeranz (2000): around 1750 there was little to distinguish the economy of Europe from that of China
Subsequent scholarship does not support this claim
China remained Malthusian: technological developments led to greater population, not more income per capita
Many advantages under the Qing (1644-1911)
Large, unified state, long period of internal peace
High levels of market integration
Low taxes
Central state did not regulate the economy
Governed by a large, meritocratic bureaucracy
Qing Empire in 1820

--- Slide 16 ---
Why did the Chinese economy decline after 1750?
Political institutions: emperor was relative unconstrained (bureaucracy subject to imperial discretion), allowed Qing to govern autocratically
Centralization limited competition; weak state capacity and endemic local corruption
Violent rebellions (White Lotus Rebellion 1796-1804; Taiping Rebellion 1850-1864)
Imperial exam encouraged investment in human capital, but knowledge of Confucian classics, with little practical application to science or technology
This limited the capacity of the Qing to copy from the West; the first attempts were carried out in a Confucian context
Clan-based nature of Chinese families: increased safety net, reduced incentive to form corporations that provide mutual aid or financial institutions for interpersonal risk sharing

--- Slide 17 ---
China’s Difficult Path to Catching Up
The Chinese miracle is remarkable: nearly one billion people taken out of extreme poverty within the span of our lifetime
China has remained an autocracy: very few constraints on executive power
The ”Century of Humiliation” between 1840-1949 saw attempts at modernization stall
Communist period (1949-present): modern centralized state at tremendous human cost
Forced collectivization of agriculture and a series of Five-Year Plans culminating in the disastrous Great Leap Forward (1958–62)
Misguided attempt to jump-start industrialization in the absence of a market mechanism

Mao Zedong

--- Slide 18 ---
Unlimited Governance and the Great Famine
China’s Communist government suffered from severe lack of checks and balances
Local bureaucrats thus had incentive to inflate estimates of grain production, leading to massive famine
Local cadres used extreme violence to enforce quotas
Peasants accused of hiding food or of shirking were beaten and sometimes killed
Collectivization saw productivity plummet as the tacit knowledge peasant farmers had of their local soils was lost
Plausible estimates range from around 15 to 45 million dead from mass famine

Great Leap Forward Propaganda Poster

--- Slide 19 ---
China’s Reversal
China began to successfully reverse course under Deng Xiaoping, the Chinese leader who initiated market-oriented reforms in 1979
But it remained autocratic. How did China reverse course?
China did not need to reinvent the wheel of industrialization or the modern economy. It could borrow industrial inputs and managerial know-how from abroad via opening up to foreign direct investment
Tremendous under-utilized economic potential had clearly been there for decades prior to the reforms of the late 1970s
Deng Xiaoping

--- Slide 20 ---
Market-Based Reforms and the Rule of Law
China’s first reforms (1978-95) dismantled command economy and collectivization, freeing up hundreds of millions of workers to move into industrial labor
Post-1995 reforms involved widespread privatization
State’s share of industrial output declined from ~50% in 1995 to 24% in 2008
But there was no rule of law in pre-reform China
As a market economy was introduced, a corresponding legal system had to be developed. One important development was the adoption of elements of German civil law such as the right for private citizens to sue the government
Relatively minor moves towards greater rule of law were sufficient to induce a radical transformation of Chinese economy and society
Foreign investment poured in, trade ratio rose from 9.7% to 31.9% between 1978 and 1993

--- Slide 21 ---
Importance of Political Institutions
One difference between the way that the Chinese and Europeans legitimated political rule: for the Chinese, good rule meant legitimate rule
A good Chinese emperor was viewed as having the “Mandate of Heaven”
This principle continued to hold influence in Communist China
Even though the state could have (and has) infringed on the rights of its citizens at any time, doing so in a way that would have undermined economic growth would have also undermined the legitimacy of the ruling Communist Party
‘Portrait’ of Confucius

--- Slide 22 ---
Importance of Culture: Value Placed on Education
Culture: like Japan and other East Asian countries, literacy was relatively high in pre-modern China
This was a product of a culture that viewed education as a means of rising economically and socially (via bureaucracy)
This legacy of high educational attainment is likely one reason that Chinese economic success has not petered out
Ming ‘scholar-official’ (bureaucrat)

--- Slide 23 ---
Chapter Summary
Some societies have been able to catch up with the economic frontier; others have lagged behind
Colonial past, factor endowments, and political institutions have all played a major role in determining capacity for catch-up growth
Since 1800 the pace of growth around the world has been very uneven
Japan was the first non-Western country to grow; predicated on major changes in political institutions
Followed by East Asian Tigers; eventually by China
Still a long way to go, but there are plenty of reasons to believe the poorest parts of the world will become rich in the next half-century
