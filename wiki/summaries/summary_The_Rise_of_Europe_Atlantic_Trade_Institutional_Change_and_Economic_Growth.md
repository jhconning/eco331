# The Rise of Europe: Atlantic Trade, Institutional Change, and Economic Growth

> **Source:** Acemoglu, Daron, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson. 2005. "The Rise of Europe: Atlantic Trade, Institutional Change, and Economic Growth." _American Economic Review_  95(3): 546–79. [Paper](https://drive.google.com/file/d/14sA0peLFc1O-EY16t6Mv75NZR5DFGjdU/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
This paper, authored by Nauro F. Campos, Fabrizio Coricelli, and Luigi Moretti (2019), addresses the long-standing debate regarding the economic growth effects of European Union (EU) membership. Previous literature has struggled with the methodological challenge of country heterogeneity, often failing to provide robust causal estimates. Utilizing the **Synthetic Control Method (SCM)**, the authors construct custom counterfactuals for 17 countries that joined the EU between 1973 and 2004. Their findings indicate that, with the exception of Greece, EU membership has generated significant and positive long-term growth and productivity dividends, with per capita incomes estimated to be, on average, 10% higher in the first ten years of membership compared to their counterfactuals.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **Methodological Innovation:** The authors argue that standard panel regressions mask individual country experiences. By using SCM, they create an "artificial country" (a weighted combination of non-member countries) to serve as a baseline, allowing for a more nuanced analysis of how institutional integration impacts specific economies.
*   **The "Deep" Integration Dividend:** The paper distinguishes between shallow trade agreements (tariffs) and deep institutional integration (regulatory policies, competition frameworks). The authors posit that EU membership provides a boost to growth by forcing institutional alignment, which accelerates investment and structural efficiency.
*   **Drivers of Growth:** The authors identify three primary channels through which EU membership drives economic success:
    *   **Trade Openness:** Traditional gains from trade liberalization.
    *   **Financial Integration:** The ability to leverage cross-border capital flows.
    *   **Euro Adoption:** The common currency is associated with an approximate 2 percentage point increase in growth payoffs compared to non-Euro members.
*   **Anticipation Effects:** For the 2004 eastern enlargement, the paper notes that the long pre-accession preparation phase meant growth dividends were often realized *before* official membership, necessitating the use of 1998 as the de facto treatment date for analysis.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Institutions:** This paper provides a modern empirical test of the institutional hypothesis in economic history. It illustrates how "institutional integration"—the forced adoption of common regulatory, legal, and economic standards—acts as a catalyst for economic convergence, supporting the theory that institutional quality is a primary driver of long-run development.
*   **Economic History & Modernization:** The paper contributes to the study of European exceptionalism. By comparing the "Golden Age" of post-WWII growth to the subsequent eras of integration, it highlights the transition from simple trade-based growth to growth driven by complex, integrated institutional frameworks.
*   **Geography and Heterogeneity:** The authors acknowledge that geography and initial conditions matter, but they argue that membership in a high-quality institutional bloc can help overcome domestic structural disadvantages. The unique case of Greece—which experienced lower GDP growth relative to its synthetic counterfactual—serves as a cautionary example of how institutional integration can potentially expose non-competitive domestic industries to sudden competitive pressures if the domestic reform process is incomplete.
