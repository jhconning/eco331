## Course Description

The world today is rich. Billions of people today enjoy living standards multiple times higher than their ancestors.  Yet most of the technological and economic transformations that made this possible occurred only in the last two centuries—and for many, even more recently. While this era of sustained economic growth has brought serious challenges— wealth has become very unevenly distributed, too many people still live lives of misery and want, and the planet is warming —most people today live longer, healthier lives and enjoy services and technologies their ancestors could not have imagined. But why did it take human civilizations, with their five millennia of history, so long to unlock the key to sustained economic growth? Where and how did this transformation first occur? Why has it spread so unevenly across the globe? Can this growth be sustained?

![](growth_chart1.png){width=350px}

This course explores major debates in economic history surrounding the timing, causes and consequences of important transitions: the shift to agriculture, the origins and purposes of early states, the evolution of property rights and labor systems (including coerced labor and feudalism), the emergence of modern nation states and taxation, the development of capitalism, and the Industrial Revolution—both the first and subsequent waves. We will examine competing explanations that emphasize different factors including: geography, politics and institutions, culture, demography, colonialism and exploitation, and other factors. While these are 'big questions' that invite theorizing, we will ground our analysis in detailed case studies and empirical historical research. The course demonstrates how economic theory and quantitative methods can complement historical investigation to evaluate competing explanations and provide frameworks for understanding both the modern world and its past.

### Participation and Discussion Challenge exercises

Grades for this activity will be based on your contributions thoughout the semester to the following sub-activities:

1. **Weekly Discussion Challenges:** Usually due the night before each class, you will be asked to contribute questions comments based on the readings for the upcoming class. These challenges will change through the semester.  Sometimes you'll just be asked to post comments and questions, at other times you will be asked to do prior research on debating points, or group activities.  You will be graded based on submission and preparation in class.
2. **Group presentations:**  Groups of 4 students will be responsible for reading and providing a short slide presentation and discussion of a reading.  Each student will partipate in one of these. A schedule of papers will be posted (approx 11).
3. **Class Participation:** Contributions to class discussion, office hour visits, and other measures of constructive engagement.

I may post a few simple graded online problem sets to replace weekly discussion challenges, primarily as an aid to help you study for the midterm and final.

Regular attendance and class participation is important. If you should miss a lecture, you are responsible to keep fully informed about class material discussed and all course changes, should they occur. I will ask that all phones and electronic devices be put away during class (unless they are being used exclusively for note-taking) in order to avoid distractions and disturbing others.

Submitted written work must be original, with clear references indicating reference works consulted and any quoted text in quotation marks with proper attribution.  Large language models such as Gemini, Claude, ChatGPT, Meta AI, and other AI assistants can be useful _complements_ to learning about historical topics. Their use will be *required* in a few assignments.  I _encourage_ you to explore these tools, they are a fantastic aid to a history course (read up on historical events and interpretations!). But think of them as a tool that enhances your efforts, not one that replaces them.
