# The Economic Impact of the Black Death

> **Source:** * Jedwab, Remi, Noel D. Johnson, and Mark Koyama. 2022. "The Economic Impact of the Black Death." _Journal of Economic Literature_  60(1): 132–78. [PaperLinks to an external site.](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1jPaAvfrkHJaxkGBV-qMqZLI6pDOnS1JU/view?usp=sharing)


## Overview
The Black Death (1347–1352) represents the most significant demographic shock in European history, killing between 30% and 50% of the population. As a "pure" demographic shock that targeted human life while leaving physical and natural capital largely intact, it serves as a crucial natural experiment for economic historians. Authors Remi Jedwab, Noel D. Johnson, and Mark Koyama evaluate the plague's immediate disruptions and its complex, heterogeneous long-run impacts on European economic development, institutional change, and the divergence between Europe and the rest of the world.

## Core Arguments & Mechanisms
*   **The Exogeneity of the Shock:** The authors argue that mortality rates were largely idiosyncratic and independent of city size, trade potential, or institutional quality, allowing for a causal analysis of the plague's effects.
*   **Competing Frameworks:**
    *   **Malthusian:** A collapse in population increased land-to-labor and capital-to-labor ratios, theoretically driving up per capita income and real wages.
    *   **Smithian:** The plague disrupted trade networks and increased transaction costs, creating negative feedback loops that hindered specialization and market integration.
    *   **Institutional:** The shock shifted de facto economic power, disrupting political equilibria and facilitating the decline of serfdom in Western Europe.
*   **Short-Run Disruption vs. Long-Run Heterogeneity:** While the initial impact was universally disruptive (market breakdown, inflation, and temporary wage decreases), the long-run outcomes varied. Northwestern Europe maintained higher per capita incomes and wages, contributing to the "Little Divergence," while Southern Europe and parts of the Middle East experienced long-term decline or stagnation.
*   **Urban Recovery:** City recovery was primarily driven by migration rather than natural increase. Cities with better "locational fundamentals" (e.g., coastal/riverine access) recovered faster, demonstrating a growth-enhancing reallocation of the population.

## Connections to Course Themes
*   **Economic History & the Malthusian Epoch:** The Black Death is the definitive case study for testing Malthusian trap predictions. The authors show that while the plague provided a temporary relief from population pressure, the "escape" from the Malthusian trap required institutional and structural changes (such as the European Marriage Pattern) rather than just a population decline.
*   **Institutions:** The plague acted as a "critical juncture." In Western Europe, the labor shortage triggered the decline of serfdom, as the leverage of the peasantry increased and the "cartel" of landlords collapsed. Conversely, in Eastern Europe, the same labor scarcity contributed to the "Second Serfdom," highlighting the importance of initial institutional conditions (like the strength of urban centers and state capacity) in mediating the shock.
*   **Culture & Diversity:** The authors examine the tragic "scapegoating" of Jewish communities. Pogroms were more frequent in areas with pre-existing antisemitic sentiment, but economic "complementarities"—such as the presence of Jewish moneylenders—provided a protective effect that partially mitigated persecution.
*   **Geography:** The "Little Divergence" (the shift in economic power from the Mediterranean/South to the Northwest) is partly attributed to the differential response to the plague. Geographic suitability for pastoral versus arable farming influenced labor markets, the demand for female labor, and subsequent fertility regimes, which in turn influenced long-run growth paths.
