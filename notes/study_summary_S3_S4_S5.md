# DECK 3: Geography

**Big question:** Does physical geography explain why some regions became rich and others didn't?

### Fundamental vs. Proximate Causes
- Proximate causes of growth: capital, trade, technology — but what causes *those*?
- Deeper candidates: **Geography, Institutions, Culture, Demography**
- Geography is a candidate for the deepest ("fundamental") cause

### Econometrics Primer
- Linear regression: fitting $y = a + b_1 x + e$ to data
- Omitted variable bias: leaving out a relevant variable biases estimates (e.g., attendance affects exam scores)
- Spurious correlation vs. causation: ice cream doesn't cause drownings — hot weather causes both
- ML vs. econometrics: ML maximizes prediction; econometrics seeks unbiased *causal* estimates for policy

### Geography and Development — Jeff Sachs
- Many of the world's poorest countries are landlocked; water transport is cheapest
- U.S. has exceptional internal waterways; most of Africa and interior Asia are far from navigable water

### Geography and Disease
- **Malaria belt**: regions in malaria zones grow ~1.3% slower per year (Gallup & Sachs)
- **Tsetse fly**: kills livestock, prevents animal traction, linked to less state formation (Alsan 2014)
- Disease environment shaped where dense settlement and early states could form

### Jared Diamond — Guns, Germs, and Steel
- Continental orientation matters: **Eurasia is horizontal** (East-West) → similar climate along latitude → crops, animals, ideas spread easily
- **Americas and Africa are vertical** (North-South) → different climate zones block diffusion
- Earlier agriculture → more people → better technology → military advantage → conquest

### Geography and Climate
- Roman Empire rose during warm period; declined during cold
- Post-1000 CE warming → higher yields and growth in Western Europe
- Cold spells → famines, conflict, pogroms

### Transport Infrastructure
- Geography is not fate: Roman roads reshaped Europe's economic geography for 1,500+ years
- Investment in ports, roads, canals can offset natural disadvantages
- Effects persist: location of European cities still traces Roman infrastructure

### The Potato (Columbian Exchange)
- Nunn & Qian (2011): potato introduction to Old World → large increases in population and urbanization
- A natural experiment: suitability of land for potatoes is essentially exogenous
- Shows geography (crop suitability) can have direct, measurable causal effects on development

### Centralization vs. Competition
- **Witfogel hydraulic hypothesis**: large-scale irrigation requires centralized coordination → despotism
- **Tilly / Hume**: Europe's fragmented, competitive geography → rival states competing for talent → innovation
- Fractal coastline, mountain barriers → many small polities rather than one empire

### Origins of Extractive vs. Inclusive Institutions
- Geography shaped colonial institutions: disease environment determined where Europeans settled vs. extracted
- Tropical plantation crops (sugar, cotton) → slavery, concentrated power
- Engerman & Sokoloff: agroclimate → factor endowments → institutional path

### Geography, Crops, and Culture
- Regions with higher potential caloric yield → more future-oriented populations (survey evidence)
- Plough agriculture (vs. hoe/rake) → different gender roles; persists in second-generation immigrants
- Geography shapes cultural traits, which then shape institutions and growth

# DECK 4: Malthus, Population, and Human Capital

**Big question:** Why did living standards stagnate for 300,000 years despite technological progress, and what forces were building beneath the surface?

### Pre-Industrial Demography
- Life expectancy in Roman Egypt: ~27–28 years
- 1700 England: 38 years at birth (best in the world)
- Infant mortality ~30% before age 1 in Roman world
- Queen Anne: 17 pregnancies, 4 live births, no child survived past 10

### The Malthusian Model
- Birth rate rises with income; death rate falls with income
- When $b(y) > d(y)$: population grows → diminishing returns → income falls back to $y^*$
- **Steady state**: population $N^*$ and subsistence income $y^*$ — always return here
- Technology improves → income rises temporarily → population rises → income returns to $y^*$
- **Key insight**: in a Malthusian world, technology raises *population*, not *living standards*

### Malthusian Comparative Statics
- **Lower birth rate** (preventative check: delayed marriage, contraception) → smaller population, *higher* income at new steady state
- **Higher death rate** (epidemic) → smaller population, *higher* income at new steady state
- **Technology advance** → larger population, same income per capita

### Earlier Onset of Agriculture
- Earlier agriculture → larger, more technologically advanced population → able to conquer others
- But NOT higher per-capita incomes — explains Diamond's puzzle of why some civilizations conquered others

### Why Malawi Today is Poorer than England 1800
- Modern medicine lowered death rates, but birth rates remained high → population surge → lower per-capita income
- Purely Malthusian prediction: foreign aid that lowers death rates without changing birth rates makes things *worse* per capita

### The Irish Famine (1845–1850)
- Potato introduction → rapid population growth; British colonial tenancy system
- Potato blight 1845 → 1 million deaths (1/8 of population), millions emigrate
- Malthusian "positive check" in action; fueled Irish nationalism

### The Black Death (1347–1353)
- Killed ~30–50% of Europe's population
- Malthusian result: massive positive shock to per-capita income; wages rose sharply
- Elites tried "Seigneurial Reaction" to freeze wages (Statute of Laborers 1349) — largely failed
- Serfdom disappeared in Western Europe; hardened in Eastern Europe (landlords were stronger there)

### Three Horsemen: Did Plague Lead to Europe's Escape?
- Voigtlander & Voth: 1350–1700 death rate curve was *upward-sloping* in income (unusual)
  - Plague → income up → urbanization up → city mortality up → death rate up
  - Plague → income up → war up → death rates up
- **Multiple equilibria**: a large enough shock (Black Death) could move Europe from low to high steady state
- Wars and state formation: constant warfare (9/10 years 1500–1800) forced state-building and centralization

### Galor's "Storm Beneath the Surface"
- During the Malthusian epoch, forces were building that would eventually break the trap
- **Quantys vs. Qualys**:
  - Quantys: many children, little investment per child
  - Qualys: few children, heavy investment in skills
- Technological change raised demand for skilled workers → Qualys gained evolutionary/economic advantage
- Positive feedback: tech → Qualy incomes and population → more tech → more Qualys → fertility falls → escape

### Elements of Galor's Unified Theory
Population size and composition drive progress via:
- Supply of innovations, demand for innovations
- Diffusion of knowledge, division of labor, extent of trade
- Cultural traits complementary to growth (future-orientation, adaptability) transmitted as norms

### From Malthus to Solow
- **Malthusian model**: land is fixed → technology raises population, not per-capita income (long-run growth ≈ 0)
- **Solow model**: capital is *accumulated* → technology raises per-capita income to new steady state
  - Growing population can be equipped with new capital
  - Long-run growth in per-capita income requires *sustained* technological improvement (TFP growth)

### Neoclassical Growth and Convergence
- Two countries with same technology and savings rate: poorer country (lower K/L) grows faster → **convergence**
- Mechanisms: capital flows, labor migration, trade
- Policy implication: markets + openness → poor countries catch up
- Critique: this didn't happen — divergence persists → institutions must matter

# DECK 5: Institutions

**Big question:** What are institutions, how do they shape economic development, and why do some countries get stuck with bad ones?

### Proximate vs. Fundamental Causes
- Capital, trade, technology are *proximate* — what's the deeper cause?
- Institutions: the rules that shape incentives to invest, trade, and innovate

### A Brief History of Political Economy
- **Mercantilists**: zero-sum nationalism; monopolies, colonies, rent extraction
- **Classical Liberals** (Locke, Smith, Hume): natural rights, limited government, free markets, rule of law
- **Marx**: institutions are social relations of production shaped by class conflict; capitalism dispossesses peasantry
- **Neoclassical**: institutions assumed away; markets + prices → efficiency (Lerner: "a solved political problem")
- **Keynesians / Hamiltonians**: market failures are real; government creates and repairs markets
- **Neo-liberals** (Hayek, Friedman / Thatcher, Reagan): back to classical liberalism; Washington Consensus
- Today: resurgent mercantilism and economic nationalism

### Neoclassical Growth Accounting
- $Y = A \cdot K^\alpha \cdot L^{1-\alpha}$: output explained by capital, labor, and TFP ($A$)
- Solow residual: only 13% of variation explained by factor growth; 87% in TFP — unexplained
- Cross-country regressions tried adding geography, culture, institutions to explain the residual
- Problem: endogeneity — institutions may be caused by the same things that cause income

### Neo-Institutional Economics
- **Ronald Coase**: firms exist to minimize transaction costs; markets vs. hierarchy
- **Douglas North**: institutions are the *rules of the game* — humanly devised constraints (formal and informal)
  - Reduce uncertainty, enable commitment and trust, shape incentives
  - Formal: laws, constitutions, contracts
  - Informal: norms, customs, codes of conduct (far more resistant to change)
- Early North: institutions evolve to minimize transaction costs (efficiency)
- Later North: institutions shaped by elites → inefficient institutions can *persist for a long time*

### Property Rights
- Define who can use, exclude others from, and transfer assets
- Secure PRs → incentives to invest and improve → growth
- France/Spain: monarchies relied on monopoly privileges → resisted efficient property rights
- England/Netherlands: commercial interests stronger (especially after 1688) → more fluid property rights

### The Glorious Revolution (1688)
- William of Orange overthrows James II; English Bill of Rights constrains the monarch
- Parliamentary control over taxes and spending → **credible commitment** to repay debts
- Falling interest rates on government debt → more investment, lower cost of capital
- Critics: also strengthened centralized state to advance commercial class interests; enclosures hurt the poor

### Sokoloff & Engerman: Factor Endowments and New World Divergence
- **Puzzle**: Caribbean colonies were *richer* than future U.S. in 1700; Haiti possibly world's richest in 1790
- Three paths:
  1. **Sugar/slave economies** (Caribbean, Brazil): extreme inequality, elite capture of all institutions
  2. **Spanish America** (Mexico, Peru): mineral resources + native labor → extractive grants, concentrated wealth
  3. **North American mainland**: no plantation crops → small farms → broad-based land/wealth distribution
- Initial factor endowments → type of institutions → persistence of inequality through voting rights, education, credit

### AJR (Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson 2001)
- **Settler mortality** as instrument for institutions: where Europeans died of disease, they set up extractive colonies; where they settled, they built inclusive institutions
- Expropriation risk (institutional quality) → per-capita income: strong causal effect
- Causal chain: settler mortality → institutions → GDP per capita

### Historical Case Studies in Institutional Change
- **Korea natural experiment**: same geography, culture, history up to 1950 → South 20x richer today; only institutions differ
- **Dissolution of Monasteries (1535–1540)**: Henry VIII's land redistribution → "rise of the gentry" → higher agricultural yields, more industrialization centuries later
- **Napoleonic invasions**: French-occupied German regions got new civil law codes → long-run positive effects on urbanization and per-capita income

### Inclusive vs. Extractive Institutions (Acemoglu & Robinson)
- **Inclusive**: broad property rights, competitive markets, political constraints on elites → innovation, investment
- **Extractive**: concentrated power, rents extracted by narrow elite → stagnation
- Transition from extractive to inclusive is the key to development — but elites resist it

### Medieval Political Institutions
- **City-states** (Italy, Low Countries, Germany): self-governing, oligarchic, favorable to trade
- **Parliaments**: emerged ~1240s; gave political nation (towns, nobles) leverage over monarchs
- Representative institutions → lower borrowing costs for states (city-states and parliamentary regimes paid half the interest of absolute monarchies)

### The Peruvian Mita
- Spanish forced-labor draft on conquered Inca empire to mine silver and mercury
- Natural experiment: boundary of mita district still predicts lower incomes and public goods today — 500 years of persistence

| Theme | Geography | Malthus | Institutions |
|-------|-----------|---------|--------------|
| Why some countries are rich | Geographic luck, disease, access to trade | Earlier ag onset → more tech, not higher income | Inclusive vs. extractive rules of the game |
| Why traps persist | Fixed geography, disease | Diminishing returns on land | Elite capture; informal norms resist change |

| Role of colonialism | Disease environment determined settler vs. extraction | Earlier tech → conquest | Extractive institutions persist centuries after independence |
| Econometric challenge | Geography may be correlated with institutions | Malthusian steady state — hard to test | Institutions are endogenous; natural experiments needed |

### The Core Debate in Three Sentences
1. **Geography** says initial physical conditions set the development path — disease, crops, coastlines, continental orientation.
2. **Institutions** says geography only matters because it shaped the rules of the game — property rights, inclusive politics, credible commitments.
3. **Malthus** says neither geography nor institutions changed living standards for 300,000 years because population always expanded to absorb any surplus — the real question is what finally broke that trap.
