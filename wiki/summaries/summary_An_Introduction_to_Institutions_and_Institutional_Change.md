# A Study in Resistance to Institutional Change: The Lost Game of Latin American Land Reform

> **Source:** North, Douglas, 1990. "An Introduction to Institutions and Institutional Change", Chapter 1 in _Institutions, Institutional Change, and Economic Performance_. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge ; New York. ([PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nMW7CIDLL1vvffRtlqeHOqQlqpW5_piy/view?usp=sharing))


### Overview
This paper by Alain de Janvry and Elisabeth Sadoulet examines why redistributive land reforms in Latin America, despite being high on political agendas following the 1961 Punta del Este conference, largely failed to alter the region's polarized land tenure structure. The authors argue that the failure was not accidental but a result of a path-dependent "game" between the state and landowners. By choosing to prioritize agricultural modernization—using the threat of expropriation to induce productivity increases on large farms—states inadvertently empowered landlords, who subsequently utilized their newfound economic and political influence to block future redistribution.

### Core Arguments & Mechanisms
The authors integrate two primary theoretical frameworks for institutional change: **transactions costs** and **collective action**.

*   **The Initial Market Failure:** The Latin American agrarian sector suffered from an inverse relationship between Total Factor Productivity (TFP*) and farm size due to the lack of a functioning land/credit market and high supervision costs for hired labor. Theoretically, redistributing large estates into family farms would have improved social efficiency (TFP*).
*   **The Strategic Error:** The state attempted to force modernization on large farms by using the threat of expropriation. While this successfully raised productivity (modernization), it failed to redistribute land.
*   **The Political Feedback Loop:** Modernization granted large farmers increased economic power. This wealth allowed them to engage in successful collective action (rent-seeking), which manifested in two ways:
    1.  **Credible Promises:** Lobbying the state to shift budgets away from land reform toward general agricultural development, making the state’s threat of expropriation no longer credible.
    2.  **Institutional Rents:** Securing subsidized credit, tax advantages, and infrastructure that artificially inflated the profitability of large farms, rendering redistribution economically "infeasible" under existing compensation frameworks.
*   **Political Myopia:** The state failed to foresee that its attempt to achieve short-term gains (modernization) would trigger a shift in the political landscape that prevented long-term institutional improvement (redistribution).

### Connections to Course Themes
*   **Institutions:** The paper serves as a classic application of Institutional Economics, demonstrating that institutions are not just efficient outcomes of transactions cost minimization (North) but are deeply embedded in power dynamics and political markets (Olson). It highlights how "second-best" institutional settings can create path-dependent traps.
*   **Economic History:** The study provides a mid-20th-century case study of how structural reforms are thwarted. It mirrors broader themes in development economics where the state is treated not as an autonomous actor, but as an arena of competition between interest groups.
*   **Geography & Agriculture:** By focusing on the agrarian structure, the text links to the broader Malthusian/post-Malthusian context of the "Agrarian Question." It shows how land tenure—a foundational institution—can either facilitate or severely restrict the transition to a modern, productive economy.
*   **Culture/Power:** The text illustrates how historical power structures (the legacy of the latifundio) are resilient; even when challenged by reformist state initiatives, the elites effectively used modern economic tools (technology/modernization) to reinforce existing cultural and political hierarchies.
