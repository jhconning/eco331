## Joseph Henrich

- There were other _Homo_ species relatively recently—Neanderthals, Denisovans, _H. floresiensis_—but we either interbred with them or they went extinct. The fact that we could interbreed with Neanderthals suggests we were barely separated enough to even count as distinct species.
- Ants have been evolving for roughly 100-140 million years, while anatomically modern humans emerged only about 300,000 years ago. That's a 300-fold difference in evolutionary time. More importantly, even as humans dispersed across the globe, we maintained enough gene flow between populations to prevent reproductive isolation.  Our adaptability is primarily **behavioral and cultural** rather than biological. When humans encountered new environments—Arctic tundra, tropical rainforests, high-altitude plateaus—we adapted through technology, clothing, shelter, and social organization rather than through genetic divergence. An ant lineage facing a new ecological niche must adapt biologically or die; humans just invent new tools.
- Contrast to ants: millions of years of geographic isolation on different continents and islands, coupled with very short generation times. They've radiated into wildly different ecological niches (think leaf-cutters, army ants, honeypot ants) that required actual biological specialization. Each isolated population evolved reproductive barriers over time.
